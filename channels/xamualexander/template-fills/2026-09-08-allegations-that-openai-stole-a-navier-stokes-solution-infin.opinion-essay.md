## Thesis

OpenAI's alleged use of user prompts to train models that produced a Navier-Stokes proof is a major scandal that could undermine trust in AI, even as it marks a historic breakthrough for mathematics.

## Argument

The mechanism is the training data. OpenAI's own policies explicitly reserve the right to train models on user content, and Tristan Buckmaster's account shows that when he directly asked whether his and Levent Alpoge's Codex sessions were used, OpenAI dodged the question—first answering about direct lookup, then staying silent on training data. That's the equivalent of pleading the fifth. The timing is damning: OpenAI only started working on Navier-Stokes after a rumor reached them, and they chose the exact same non-standard approach that Buckmaster and Alpoge had been pursuing for months. The pressure campaign—urgent meetings, proposals to remove Alpoge from authorship, and the threat 'If you don't want me to be nice, then I don't have to be nice'—reads like a mafia script. Even if the proof is correct, the theft of credit would be a betrayal of the mathematical community. Yet the host insists that the mathematical result stands on its own: truth doesn't depend on who stole it. This is a Newton-Leibniz moment for AI, but with the added twist that one of the combatants is a black box that may have been trained on the other's private work.

## Counterpoints

- OpenAI's model did not directly look up user data, but that dodges the real question about training data.
- Some argue it's unlikely OpenAI reads individual prompts; a leak elsewhere or espionage between Anthropic and OpenAI is more plausible.
- Even if the proof was stolen, the mathematical result is still true and valid.
- OpenAI's policies explicitly allow training on user content, so it may be technically legal.
