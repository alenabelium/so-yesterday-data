## Thesis

OpenAI's release of nearly 400 AI-generated mathematical proofs, including the Unique Games Conjecture, marks a 'mathocalypse' that shatters the notion that AI cannot be creative and forces mathematicians to become tour guides to the.

## Argument

The mechanism is the sheer scale and nature of the results. OpenAI dumped 372 huge results, including a proof of the Unique Games Conjecture, a problem Scott Aaronson's wife worked on her entire career, solved in about three hours. The proofs are so alien and obfuscated that no human has understood them yet, and Dana Moshkovitz's text describes it as 'something written by someone who's on psychedelics.' This directly refutes the claim that AI can't invent new things — what's more creative than something that strikes an expert as 'alien craziness'?

Beyond the headline, the list includes L=BPL, a new Fourier transform for integer multiplication, a positive solution to the unitary synthesis problem, and the uncomputability of solving polynomial equations over the rationals — Hilbert's 10th problem over the rationals, arguably the biggest open problem in computability theory. Each alone could be result of the year; together they collapse whole sub-communities.

OpenAI's strategy of dumping undigested proofs forces a race to understand them, and the host argues this is superior to Anthropic's approach of paying experts to explain results, which vanished in obscurity. The host frames this as a victory over 'elitist gatekeepers' who have held mathematics back.

The implications are profound: mathematicians become tour guides to the Platonic realm, and the host predicts more interest in math than ever. The host also notes the conspicuous absence of cryptography results, suggesting OpenAI may be keeping practical breakthroughs for itself. The host's tone is celebratory, mocking

## Counterpoints

- The alleged solutions might be AI slop, not real proofs — but the host notes the Lean certificates and the sheer number of solved open problems.
- Maybe none of the 372 problems were real math, just glorified contest puzzles — the host retorts that Hilbert's 10th problem over the rationals is hardly a contest puzzle.
- There's still no Riemann hypothesis, so AI is overhyped — the host asks what they'll say when AI does prove it.
- The future of math can't consist only of understanding AI results; mathematicians must formulate their own questions — but the host sarcastically questions whether the advisory group will open their libraries to the masses.
- Cryptography's absence suggests OpenAI is hiding practical breakthroughs for its own monopoly — a hot take the host shares.
