## Thesis

OpenAI's independent math advisory group is a diplomatic olive branch that mathematicians should accept, as refusing it would accelerate their irrelevance.

## Argument

The mechanism is reputational and strategic. OpenAI could simply ignore the 25 Fields Medalists' open letter and face no consequences, but instead it created an advisory group hosted at the Institute for Advanced Study, with members like Timothy Gowers and Edward Witten, to advise on review and communication of results. This is a calculated PR move, but it's also a genuine offer of cooperation. The group operates independently, members aren't paid, and it won't advise on pacing internal progress. The alternative is worse: if mathematicians refuse to engage, OpenAI will publish results anyway, and the community will lose all influence. As one commenter notes, "If you're not at the table, you're on the menu." The field is already being transformed—AI has solved over 100 open problems, including Navier-Stokes—and the pace will only accelerate. Mathematicians who cling to the old ways, like the Luddites fighting benchmarking, will be left behind. The smart move is to accept the olive branch, help shape how results are communicated, and reform academia to value human contributions like exposition. The castle is already shattering; cooperation is the only way to salvage something.

## Counterpoints

- OpenAI is cynically using mathematicians for legitimacy and crisis management, as Burt Totaro suggests.
- Refusing to cooperate could delegitimize OpenAI's math-solving enterprise, as one anonymous commenter argues.
- The advisory group's independence is compromised by perks like free access to pro models, as with Timothy Gowers.
- Mathematicians are being replaced, and helping OpenAI only accelerates that process.
