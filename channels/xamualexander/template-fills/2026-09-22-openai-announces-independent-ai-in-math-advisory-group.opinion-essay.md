## Thesis

OpenAI's independent math advisory group is a calculated olive branch that mathematicians should accept, because refusing it would accelerate their irrelevance.

## Argument

The mechanism is reputational leverage. OpenAI has already solved over 100 open problems, including Navier-Stokes, and could simply publish them without any community input. By creating an advisory group hosted at the IAS, with members like Gowers and Witten, they are offering mathematicians a seat at the table—a chance to shape how results are communicated and absorbed. The alternative is inaction, which the host argues is far worse. As one commenter puts it, "If you're not at the table, you're on the menu." Mathematicians who refuse to cooperate only hasten their own marginalization, as the public narrative already paints them as Luddites protecting job security. The host acknowledges the conflict-of-interest concerns (e.g., Gowers' free access to pro models) but dismisses the idea that OpenAI aims to replace mathematicians—that's human hallucination. The real risk is not cooperation but the price of inaction: a shattered glass castle with no one left to guide the transition. The host concludes that even if OpenAI is the enemy, mathematicians have already lost the war; the best they can do is negotiate favorable surrender terms.

## Counterpoints

- Burt Totaro's concern that OpenAI is exploiting mathematicians' trust for crisis management, and that the group can't change OpenAI's behavior.
- The anonymous reply arguing that refusing to cooperate can delegitimize OpenAI's math-solving enterprise.
- The PhD student's fear that their years of training will be wasted, as AI could prove results in days.
- The satirical 'foxes and chickens' comment, suggesting the advisory group is just helping OpenAI eat mathematicians more smoothly.
