## Thesis

OpenAI's independent AI-in-Math Advisory Group is a calculated olive branch that mathematicians must accept, because refusing it would accelerate their irrelevance and force unconditional surrender.

## Argument

The mechanism is strategic legitimacy. OpenAI, having announced over 100 solved open problems including Navier-Stokes, faces a credibility gap and a hostile math community. By creating an advisory group hosted at the IAS with Fields Medalists like Gowers and Witten, OpenAI buys legitimacy and frames itself as responsible, while the group's independence is carefully bounded—it won't advise on pacing internal progress. Mathematicians are in a bind: refusing to serve deprives OpenAI of legitimacy but requires collective action that won't hold, and inaction has its own price. As one commenter notes, 'If you're not at the table, you're on the menu.' The field is already losing the PR war, and cooperation offers a chance to shape how results are communicated and to reform academia, which has been 'broken for a while.' The alternative—sticking heads in the sand—leads to faster irrelevance and gutted funding within 25 years. Even the PhD student's despair is understandable, but pure math was already a terrible career decision; the love of the game is the only reason to stay, and this is the most exciting time to be in it.

## Counterpoints

- OpenAI is cynically exploiting mathematicians' trust for PR, and the group cannot change how OpenAI does business.
- Refusing to cooperate can delegitimize OpenAI's math-solving enterprise.
- OpenAI explicitly aims to replace mathematicians, so helping them is self-defeating.
- The advisory group's independence is compromised by perks like free access to OpenAI's pro models.
- Using math as a benchmark is harmful and should be stopped.
