## Thesis

OpenAI's independent math advisory group is a PR olive branch that mathematicians should accept, because refusing it would accelerate their own irrelevance.

## Argument

The mechanism here is reputational and strategic. OpenAI has already solved over 100 open problems, including Navier-Stokes, and could simply publish them without any mathematician input. Instead, they've created an advisory group hosted at the Institute for Advanced Study, with heavyweights like Timothy Gowers and Edward Witten, explicitly to give mathematicians a voice. This is a calculated move, but it's also a genuine opportunity. The alternative—refusing to cooperate—doesn't stop AI progress; it just makes mathematicians look like Luddites clinging to a glass castle that's already shattering. As one commenter put it, "If you're not at the table, you're on the menu." The advisory group won't control OpenAI's internal pace, but it can shape how results are communicated and how the field adapts. Mathematicians who refuse to engage are choosing to be left behind, while those who participate can at least influence the transition. The field of mathematics itself isn't dying—it's being reborn, and the work mathematicians do will change, but the discipline will survive. The real risk isn't AI; it's inaction.

## Counterpoints

- Some argue the advisory group is just a PR stunt to legitimize OpenAI, and mathematicians should refuse to serve.
- Others claim mathematicians can delegitimize OpenAI's math-solving enterprise by withholding cooperation.
- There's concern that the group's independence is compromised by perks like free access to OpenAI's pro models.
- A PhD student worries their years of training will be wasted if AI can solve problems in a day.
- Some see the advisory group as foxes inviting chickens to help plan their own consumption.
