## Thesis

The AI-in-Math Advisory Group's guidelines are a mix of sensible, self-serving, and hypocritical recommendations that fail to address the real challenges of AI-generated mathematics.

## Argument

The guidelines start with a noble but impractical demand: that AI labs stop using proprietary models. This is as realistic as asking for a billion dollars. The group's appeal to traditional norms like peer review and human understanding ignores that peer review is already collapsing under a mountain of papers, and the four-color theorem was accepted without human comprehension. The recommendation that AI labs fund human understanding is a blatant conflict of interest, serving the paychecks of the mathematicians who wrote it. The call for open access is hypocritical when journals charge thousands for open access, and the group fails to address the arXiv's own exclusionary submission policies. The insistence on traditional 'papers' enshrines a medium that is already obsolete. While some suggestions, like citing precise versions and publishing prompts, are good, the group misses obvious things like random seeds for reproducibility. The group's own survey is opaque, and they fail to include the community's call for immediate release of results. The mathematical community is in chaos, and these guidelines are a product of that confusion.

## Counterpoints

- The group's request for AI labs to stop using proprietary models is impractical and unrealistic.
- Traditional norms like peer review are already failing, and the four-color theorem shows human understanding isn't always necessary.
- The recommendation for AI labs to fund human understanding is self-serving and creates a conflict of interest.
- The call for open access is hypocritical given the high costs of open access publishing in mathematics.
- The insistence on traditional 'papers' is an anachronism that enshrines an outdated medium.
