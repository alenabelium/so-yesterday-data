## Thesis

The AI-in-Math Advisory Group's guidelines are a mix of sensible proposals and self-serving, hypocritical demands that fail to address the real challenges AI poses to mathematical practice.

## Argument

The guidelines open with a noble but toothless plea for AI labs to stop using proprietary models, which Alexander dismisses as performative—comparing it to asking for a billion dollars. He then attacks the core norm of human understanding, pointing out that the four color theorem was accepted without human comprehension, proving the norm is already broken. The document's call for community feedback rings hollow, as Alexander notes he was never consulted despite being part of the mathematical community. He finds some genuine value in the recommendations, particularly the push for AI labs to release their results promptly and the insistence on persistent, citable repositories—a point he praises as 'entirely correct.' However, he savages the funding demands, calling them self-serving and hypocritical, especially the stipulation that human understanding must be 'community-led' and not directed by labs, which conveniently funnels money to the committee's peers. He also criticizes the absence of a call for deterministic runs and random seed publication, a glaring omission for a group of mathematicians. The guidelines' insistence on traditional norms like seminars and papers is lampooned as enshrining outdated media, akin to mandating papyrus scrolls. Ultimately, Alexander sees the document as a reflection of a community in chaos, torn between resisting AI and profiting from it, and he urges mathematicians to adapt or be left behind.

## Counterpoints

- The norm that authors must understand and verify proofs is already violated by the four color theorem, which was accepted without human comprehension.
- The demand for community feedback is undermined by the fact that Alexander, a mathematician, was never consulted.
- The recommendation that AI labs fund human understanding is self-serving, as it funnels money to the committee's peers and conflicts with their stated principles.
- The call for traditional publication norms ignores that the medium has already shifted to electronic PDFs, making 'papers' an anachronism.
- The guidelines fail to address the need for deterministic runs and random seed publication, which Alexander considers a higher priority than chain-of-thought summaries.
