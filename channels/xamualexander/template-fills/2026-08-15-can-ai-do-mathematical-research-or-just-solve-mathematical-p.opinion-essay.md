## Thesis

AI can reproduce completed mathematical research but cannot yet conduct the exploratory, iterative process of discovering false assumptions and building theories, which remains central to human mathematical work.

## Argument

The distinction between reproducing and conducting research is the core issue. As the article notes, a cutting-edge model could likely reproduce aspects of a completed paper given a well-formulated query, but that is a far cry from the years-long process of starting with broad directions, encountering failures, and letting those failures reshape understanding. This is not a matter of raw problem-solving skill; it's about learning the rules of an unfamiliar problem environment while working, maintaining a consistent, evolving goal over months of iterative failure. The author's own feedback linearization project illustrates this: initial assumptions failed, numerical errors revealed hidden conditions, and each failure forced a theoretical revision. None of the individual mathematical elements were advanced, but the challenge was in the adaptive, exploratory process itself. Current LLMs, with their finite context windows, are subject to cumulative logical errors and context degradation over long horizons, making them poor at this kind of sustained investigation. Tests like ARC AGI-3, where leading models perform poorly, specifically probe this ability to form and correct hypotheses in a new environment. While there's no a priori reason AI couldn't eventually do this, the current limitations in permanent memory and continuous adaptation leave a clear role for human theorists.

## Counterpoints

- A priori, there is no reason AI can't automate the discovery process; it seems closer to how theoretical research actually happens.
- The distinction between reproducing and conducting research may disappear as AI systems improve at permanent memory and continuous adaptation.
- The author questions whether the end reader can even tell the difference, since the point of publishing is to expand human knowledge, not to benefit the researcher.
