## Thesis

AI can reproduce completed mathematical research but cannot yet conduct the exploratory, iterative discovery process central to original work, so human theorists remain essential.

## Argument

The distinction is not about solving well-defined problems but about the messy process of discovery. Morgan Jones's graduate students spent years on projects that began as broad directions, not clear problems, and their failures reshaped their understanding. This is like ARC AGI-3, where AI agents must investigate an unknown environment, form and test hypotheses, and correct false assumptions—a task where current models perform poorly. The author's feedback linearization example illustrates this: each simulation failure revealed a false assumption, leading to new theoretical insights, not just a tweak of the controller. The challenge was maintaining a consistent, evolving goal over months of iterative failure, which current LLMs struggle with due to context window limits and cumulative errors. While the author sees no a priori reason AI can't eventually do this, the current lack of permanent memory and adaptation means human theorists still have a role. The host adds a personal angle: flashes of insight, which often initiate research, cannot be controlled or directly automated, and it's unclear if AI can replicate that. So, while the boundary may blur, the core of mathematical research—discovering what's wrong and building theory around it—remains a human strength for now.
