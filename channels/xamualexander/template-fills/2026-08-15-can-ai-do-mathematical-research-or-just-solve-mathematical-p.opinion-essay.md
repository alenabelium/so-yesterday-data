## Thesis

AI can reproduce completed mathematical research, but it cannot yet conduct the exploratory, iterative process of discovering false assumptions and building theories, which remains the core of human mathematical work.

## Argument

The distinction the author draws is not about solving well-defined problems but about the research process itself. OpenAI's Astra model solved 10 big problems, yet the author's graduate students' projects lasted years, starting from broad directions and repeatedly hitting failures that reshaped their understanding. This is the key gap: reproducing a completed research program is not the same as conducting the research that led to it. The author's feedback linearization example illustrates this—each simulation failure revealed a false assumption, forcing a return to theory and the discovery of a missing condition in the literature. None of the individual mathematical elements were advanced; the challenge was learning the rules of the problem environment while working, maintaining a consistent, evolving goal over months of iterative failure. This is precisely what current LLMs struggle with, as they suffer from context degradation and cumulative logical errors over long horizons. The author points to ARC AGI-3 as a test that captures this exploratory process, and notes that leading models perform poorly on it. The author admits a priori there is no reason AI can't eventually do all this, especially as systems improve at permanent memory and continuous adaptation. But for now, the hope is that human theorists still have an active role.
