## TL;DR

Aristotle's paradox of the wheel asks how a point on a larger circle can travel farther than a point on a smaller circle when both are part of the same rolling wheel. Though mathematically explainable, the paradox is fundamentally psychological: our intuition expects both points to move the same distance, but they trace different cycloid paths. This mismatch between intuitive straight-line perception and actual curved motion is why the paradox persists.

## The argument

### The Setup: Two Points, One Wheel
- **Anchor Timestamps**: ['00:00:00']
- **Claim**: A wheel has an outer circle and an inner circle. Mark a point on each. When the wheel rolls one revolution, each point traces a circle; stretched out, the outer circle's line is longer because its circumference is larger.
- **Role**: definition

### The Paradox: Thousands of Miles Apart
- **Anchor Timestamps**: ['00:01:05']
- **Claim**: Over thousands of miles, the wheel makes millions of revolutions. If the outer radius is twice the inner, the blue point's path is twice the yellow's—a difference of thousands of miles. Yet both points are inside a tiny wheel, so they should move roughly the same amount.
- **Role**: evidence

### It's Psychological, Not Mathematical
- **Anchor Timestamps**: ['00:02:10']
- **Claim**: Mathematical analysis explains the paradox, but the explanation is unsatisfying. The real issue is psychological: our intuition expects both points to move the same distance, but they don't. This pattern—physics or math turning out to be early psychology—is common in Aristotle.
- **Role**: synthesis

### Historical Impact: Galileo to Cantor
- **Anchor Timestamps**: ['00:03:25']
- **Claim**: The paradox influenced Galileo (who used a hexagon version to argue for atomism) and Bolzano, whose book 'Paradoxes of the Infinite' influenced Cantor, the father of modern infinity studies. This shows the paradox's deep mathematical significance.
- **Role**: evidence

### The Cycloid Solution and Its Limits
- **Anchor Timestamps**: ['00:05:10']
- **Claim**: The standard solution shows that points trace cycloid paths of different lengths—the inner point's path is shorter and more direct. But this doesn't address the psychological paradox: our intuition sees straight lines, not cycloids, so the mind hits a 'does not compute' state.
- **Role**: counter

## Evidence and caveats
