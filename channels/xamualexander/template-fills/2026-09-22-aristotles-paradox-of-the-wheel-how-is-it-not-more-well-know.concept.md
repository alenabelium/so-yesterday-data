## TL;DR

Aristotle's paradox of the wheel asks how a point on a larger circle can travel farther than a point on a smaller circle when both are fixed to the same rolling wheel. The mathematical answer—that the points trace different cycloid paths—is unsatisfying because it misses the real puzzle: our intuition sees straight lines, not curves. This reveals the paradox as a psychological one, not a mathematical one.

## The argument

### The Setup: Two Points, One Wheel
- **Anchor Timestamps**: ['00:00:00']
- **Claim**: A wheel with an outer and inner circle has a blue point on the outer rim and a yellow point on the inner rim. When the wheel rolls one revolution, each point traces a circle, and when straightened, the outer circle's line is longer because its circumference is larger.
- **Role**: definition

### The Paradox: Scale Makes It Absurd
- **Anchor Timestamps**: ['00:01:05']
- **Claim**: Over thousands of miles, the wheel makes millions of revolutions, and the blue point's path is twice as long as the yellow's (if radii are 2:1). Yet both points are inside the same small wheel, so the difference should be negligible—how can one point move twice as far?
- **Role**: definition

### The Mathematical Solution: Cycloid Paths
- **Anchor Timestamps**: ['00:05:10']
- **Claim**: The standard solution shows that each point traces a cycloid path. The outer point's cycloid is longer and less efficient because it travels farther above and below the center line, while the inner point's path is shorter and more direct. This explains the distance difference mathematically.
- **Role**: evidence

### Why the Math Fails: It's Psychological
- **Anchor Timestamps**: ['00:02:10']
- **Claim**: The mathematical solution is unsatisfying because it doesn't address the real paradox: our intuition sees straight lines, not cycloids. The paradox arises from a clash between physical intuition and mathematical reality, making it a psychological paradox, not a mathematical one.
- **Role**: counter

### The Legacy: Influencing Infinity
- **Anchor Timestamps**: ['00:03:25']
- **Claim**: The paradox has a rich history: Galileo used a hexagon version to argue for atomism, and Bolzano discussed it in 'Paradoxes of the Infinite,' which influenced Cantor's work on infinity. This shows the paradox's deep impact on mathematics and philosophy.
- **Role**: synthesis
