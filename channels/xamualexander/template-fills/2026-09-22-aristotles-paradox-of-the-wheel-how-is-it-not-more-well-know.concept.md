## TL;DR

Aristotle's paradox of the wheel asks how a point on a larger circle can travel farther than a point on a smaller circle when both are part of the same rolling wheel. The paradox has mathematical explanations, but it is fundamentally a psychological paradox because our intuition expects both points to move the same distance. The true source lies in the mismatch between our intuitive straight-line perception and the actual cycloid paths traced by the points.

## The argument

### Define the Paradox
- **Anchor Timestamps**: ['00:00:00']
- **Claim**: A wheel with an outer and inner circle rolls along. Mark a point on each circle. Over one revolution, each point traces a circle, and when straightened, the outer point's line is longer. Over thousands of miles, the difference becomes enormous, yet both points are on the same wheel, so intuitively they should move roughly the same distance.
- **Role**: definition

### It's a Psychological Paradox
- **Anchor Timestamps**: ['00:02:10']
- **Claim**: The paradox is more psychological than mathematical because mathematical analysis explains the mechanics, but the real puzzle is why our intuition rebels. This pattern is common in Aristotle: what seems like physics or mathematics often turns out to be early psychology.
- **Role**: definition

### Historical Influence
- **Anchor Timestamps**: ['00:03:25']
- **Claim**: The paradox influenced Galileo, who used a hexagon version to argue for atomism, and Bernard Bolzano, whose work on the paradoxes of the infinite influenced Georg Cantor, the father of modern infinity studies. This shows the paradox's deep impact on mathematics and philosophy.
- **Role**: evidence

### Mathematical Solution Falls Short
- **Anchor Timestamps**: ['00:05:10']
- **Claim**: A standard solution shows that points on the outer circle trace longer cycloid paths than points on the inner circle, explaining the distance difference. However, this solution is unsatisfying because it doesn't address the psychological paradox: our intuition sees straight lines, not cycloids, leading to a 'does not compute' state.
- **Role**: counter
