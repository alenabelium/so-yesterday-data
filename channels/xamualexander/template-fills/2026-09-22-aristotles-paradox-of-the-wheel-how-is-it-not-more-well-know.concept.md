## TL;DR

Aristotle's paradox of the wheel asks how a point on a larger circle can travel farther than a point on a smaller circle when both are part of the same rolling wheel. The mathematical answer involves cycloid paths, but the paradox persists because our intuition sees straight lines, not curves. It's a psychological paradox about the limits of physical intuition.

## The argument

### The Paradox Setup
- **Anchor Timestamps**: ['00:00:00']
- **Claim**: A wheel with an outer and inner circle rolls; each point traces a circle. When straightened, the outer circle's line is longer than the inner's. Over thousands of miles, the difference seems enormous, yet both points are on the same small wheel.
- **Role**: definition

### The Psychological Core
- **Anchor Timestamps**: ['00:02:10']
- **Claim**: The paradox is psychological, not mathematical. Mathematical analysis explains the motion, but our intuition expects both points to move the same distance. This mismatch between intuition and analysis is the true source of the paradox.
- **Role**: definition

### Historical Influence
- **Anchor Timestamps**: ['00:03:25']
- **Claim**: The paradox influenced Galileo's atomism and Bolzano's work on infinity, which in turn influenced Cantor. This shows the paradox's deep impact on mathematics and philosophy.
- **Role**: evidence

### The Unsatisfying Mathematical Solution
- **Anchor Timestamps**: ['00:05:10']
- **Claim**: The standard solution explains that points trace cycloid paths of different lengths, but this doesn't resolve the psychological paradox. Our intuition sees straight lines, not cycloids, so the paradox persists.
- **Role**: counter
