## Thesis

Amrit Sahai's vision of needing more mathematicians to understand AI-generated breakthroughs is appealing but unrealistic, as it ignores the unsustainable exponential demand for mathematicians and the changing incentives in the field.

## Argument

The core problem with Sahai's proposal is that it assumes a fixed rate of AI-generated breakthroughs, but the rate is actually dependent on how fast we prompt the AIs. If we increase the number of mathematicians to understand every breakthrough, those mathematicians will use AI to generate even more breakthroughs, leading to an exponential need for mathematicians that is unsustainable. This would eventually require every human to be a mathematician, which is not realistic. Furthermore, the idea of a 'deployable intellectual reserve' of pure mathematicians is flawed because pure mathematicians are, by nature, concerned with things that do not have applications, whereas Sahai's examples, like the fusion power plant, are more in the realm of physics. The government would likely prefer physicists and chemists in such a reserve. Additionally, the prestige and career incentives in pure mathematics are already eroding due to AI, as the excitement of proving new theorems is diminished when AI can do it faster, and the job market remains highly competitive with few tenure-track positions. The article itself, drafted with the help of GPT-6, ironically undermines the argument by showing that even the author relies on AI, and the comments highlight that the field's gatekeeping and lack of material incentives make it hard to attract more mathematicians.

## Counterpoints

- Sahai argues that struggle is essential to understanding, but the host challenges this, suggesting that with a perfect tutor, struggle might not be necessary.
- Sahai's thought experiment about a fusion power plant is extreme; the host suggests starting with a smaller version, questioning the need to restructure the economy for such a speculative scenario.
- The host notes that pure mathematicians are like artists, and it's unrealistic to expect government funding for a reserve of them when physicists and chemists are more directly relevant.
- The host points out that the article itself was drafted with AI assistance, undermining the argument that humans must understand everything.
- The host highlights that the prestige of being a pure mathematician is evaporating, reducing the incentive for young mathematicians to enter the field.
