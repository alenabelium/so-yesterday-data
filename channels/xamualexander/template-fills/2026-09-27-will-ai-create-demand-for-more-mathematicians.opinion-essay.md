## Thesis

Amrit Sahai's vision that AI will create demand for more mathematicians is appealing but unrealistic, as it ignores the unsustainable exponential growth of AI-generated breakthroughs and the changing incentives in pure mathematics.

## Argument

The core problem with Sahai's proposal is its failure to account for the rate at which AI can generate new mathematical ideas. If we aim to have every AI breakthrough thoroughly understood by a human, the number of mathematicians needed would grow exponentially, since those mathematicians would use AI to produce even more breakthroughs. This would lead to an absurd scenario where every human must be a mathematician, which is not a realistic goal. The thought experiment of a terawatt fusion plant based on new physics is extreme; we could instead test a smaller version. It's not worth restructuring the economy on the off chance such a scenario occurs. Moreover, the idea of a 'deployable intellectual reserve' of pure mathematicians is flawed. Pure mathematicians are, by nature, concerned with things that do not have applications, yet Sahai's argument is based on applied problems. The government would more likely want physicists, chemists, or biologists in such a reserve, not pure mathematicians. The prestige of pure mathematics is also evaporating as AI can now produce novel results, making it a less attractive career. The incentive to prove new theorems for recognition is gone, and the field's 'dinner party' appeal is diminished. The commenter's point about reading papers is key: if an AI can generate a paper in less than an hour, why spend an hour reading it? This undermines the value of incremental research that sustains many careers. ## Counterpoints

- Sahai argues that struggle is essential to understanding, but this is not an axiom; with a perfect tutor, struggle might not be necessary.
- Sahai's fusion plant thought experiment is extreme; we could test a smaller version first.
- A counterargument is that AI will make each human more effective, so fewer people could do the work, but Sahai says humans have biological limits.
- Some commenters argue that the essay provides leadership, but others say it offers no concrete policies or ideas.
