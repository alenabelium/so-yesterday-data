## Thesis

Amrit Sahai's vision of needing more mathematicians to understand AI's breakthroughs is appealing but unrealistic, as it ignores the exponential growth of AI-generated ideas and the changing incentives in pure mathematics.

## Argument

The core problem is the rate of AI-generated breakthroughs. If we scale up mathematicians to understand every AI idea, those mathematicians will use AI to generate even more breakthroughs, leading to an exponential need for mathematicians until everyone must be one. This is not a sustainable or realistic goal. Moreover, the argument for a 'deployable intellectual reserve' of pure mathematicians is flawed. Pure mathematicians are, by nature, concerned with things that do not have applications, yet Sahai's examples, like the fusion power plant, are applied problems better suited for physicists and engineers. The government would not fund a reserve of pure mathematicians for such tasks; it would fund physicists and chemists. The prestige and career incentives in pure mathematics are also evaporating. The social recognition of proving theorems is gone when AI can do it faster, and the job market is already brutal. Young mathematicians face a future with no excitement or credit for new theorems, only the unglamorous role of preventing existential risk. This is not a compelling reason to enter the field, especially when the very nature of mathematical work is changing so rapidly that we don't know what a mathematician will do in a year.

## Counterpoints

- Sahai argues that struggle is essential to understanding, but with a perfect AI tutor, struggle may not be necessary.
- Sahai's thought experiment of a terawatt fusion plant designed by AI is extreme; we could start with a smaller, safer version.
- A counterargument is that AI will make each human more effective, so fewer people are needed, but Sahai says humans have biological limits.
- Some commenters argue that the essay provides leadership and that the old way of doing math is untenable, which is a matter of fact, not belief.
- The incentive problem: young mathematicians lose the excitement of proving new theorems, but they gain the essential role of preventing human disempowerment.
