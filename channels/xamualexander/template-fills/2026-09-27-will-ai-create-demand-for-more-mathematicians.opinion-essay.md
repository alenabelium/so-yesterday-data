## Thesis

Amrit Sahai's vision of a 'deployable intellectual reserve' of mathematicians to understand AI breakthroughs is appealing but unrealistic, and AI will not create demand for more mathematicians.

## Argument

The core problem is that the rate of AI-generated breakthroughs is not fixed; it scales with the number of mathematicians using AI to produce more. If we try to ensure every breakthrough is understood, we'd need exponentially more mathematicians, eventually requiring every human to be one—an absurd outcome. This is a classic [[runaway-feedback-loop]] scenario. Moreover, the premise that struggle is essential to understanding is questionable; with a perfect AI tutor, struggle might be unnecessary, undermining the need for a large human workforce. The thought experiment of a fusion plant based on new physics is extreme; we could test a smaller version first, and it's not worth restructuring the economy for such a speculative case. Also, the argument is self-centered on mathematicians, ignoring physicists, chemists, and biologists who would be more relevant for such applications. Pure mathematicians are like artists, and a government reserve of artists is not a sensible use of tax money. Finally, the prestige of pure mathematics is evaporating as AI makes it seem replaceable, and the incentive structure for young mathematicians is already broken, with few jobs and a publish-or-perish culture that discourages deep thinking.

## Counterpoints

- Struggle is essential to understanding difficult concepts.
- We need a deployable intellectual reserve to understand AI breakthroughs for safety.
- Human agency is a fundamental value that requires understanding AI decisions.
- AI will make each of us more effective, so fewer people could do the work.
- The prestige and excitement of proving new theorems will attract mathematicians.
