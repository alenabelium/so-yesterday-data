## Thesis

The Fields Medalists' letter on AI in mathematics misdiagnoses the crisis: the real threat is not the volume of AI-generated results but the collapse of social structures that support mathematical digestion and training, and adaptation.

## Argument

The letter's core claim—that problem-solving is merely a tool for conceptual understanding—is a convenient reframing that emerged only after AI trivialized the proving of theorems. For decades, the mathematical community's actual priorities, as evidenced by journal acceptance criteria, have been answers to problems, not mere conceptual essays. This hypocrisy undermines the letter's authority.

Gowers rightly rejects the letter's oversimplified dichotomy between problem-solving and understanding, pointing to a spectrum of mathematical temperaments. The real crisis, he argues, is not that AI will flood us with results we can't digest—pre-AI mathematics already outpaced our reading capacity—but that the social structures supporting digestion and training will be destroyed. The letter's proposed solution, an international council to control the release of results, is a bureaucratic fantasy that would merely entrench the establishment's gatekeeping role.

Adaptation is the only viable path. AI can be used creatively to enhance mathematical study, such as providing tailored hints for active reading. The fear of losing deep understanding from struggling with problems is real, but it's unclear that progress would suffer. The credit system will collapse, but that's a temporary problem. The primary risk is that future mathematicians will lose motivation, and we must find imaginative ways to keep them engaged. Resistance is futile; the best we can do is recognize the changes and work out the least unsatisfactory way of dealing with them.
