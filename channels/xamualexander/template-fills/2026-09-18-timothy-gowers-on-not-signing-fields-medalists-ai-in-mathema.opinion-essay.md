## Argument

Gowers' refusal to sign the letter stems from a deeper disagreement about the nature of mathematical practice. He argues that the letter's emphasis on conceptual understanding over problem-solving is a false dichotomy, pointing to a spectrum of mathematical temperaments where both are valued as means to each other's ends. The real crisis, he contends, is not the flood of AI-generated proofs but the potential destruction of the social structures that enable mathematical digestion and the training of new mathematicians. He advocates for adaptation, suggesting that AI can be used to enhance active reading and problem-solving, and that the community should focus on creative ways to integrate AI rather than resisting its inevitable impact. The letter's lack of a clear call to action and its implicit gatekeeping are also criticized, as the establishment's concern about attribution and the 'human transmission chain' masks a fear of losing control over the mathematical canon.

## Counterpoints

- The letter's concern that AI-generated results will destroy fertile ground for new mathematics, which Gowers addresses by arguing that the volume of results is not inherently problematic and that mathematicians can choose what to digest.
- The fear that AI will lead to a loss of deep understanding and ownership of proofs, which Gowers counters by suggesting that AI can provide tailored hints to support active learning and that the value of such deep involvement may diminish when AI is superior at finding proofs.
- The worry that AI will overwhelm the mathematical community with too many results to digest, which Gowers addresses by noting that mathematics is specialized and can be processed in parallel, and that the current system already produces more papers than can be read.
- The potential loss of motivation for future mathematicians if solving famous problems becomes trivial, which Gowers acknowledges as a primary risk but suggests that the community must find new ways to motivate and train the next generation.
- The letter's implicit demand for controlled release of AI results, which Gowers criticizes as unrealistic and potentially counterproductive, as it would only delay the inevitable and might not address the underlying concerns.
