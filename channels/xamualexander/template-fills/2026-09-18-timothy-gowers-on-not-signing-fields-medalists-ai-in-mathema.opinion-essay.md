## Thesis

Timothy Gowers declined to sign the Fields Medalists' letter because it misidentifies the real crisis—not the volume of AI-generated results but the collapse of the social structures that support mathematical digestion and training.

## Argument

Gowers's refusal is not a dismissal of the letter's concerns but a rejection of its oversimplified framing. He argues that the letter's emphasis on conceptual understanding over problem-solving misrepresents the spectrum of mathematical temperaments, a point he made in his essay "The Two Cultures of Mathematics" a quarter-century ago. The real crisis, as he sees it, is the potential destruction of the social structures that enable the mathematical community to digest new results and train the next generation of mathematicians. He warns against tribalism, urging adaptation and creative use of AI rather than resistance. Gowers's own experience—discovering difference sequences as a child and learning more from the attempt than the solution—illustrates the value of struggle, but he insists this doesn't justify opposing AI. He proposes that AI could actually enhance individual understanding by providing tailored hints, and that the flood of results, while overwhelming, could be managed through selective digestion, much like choosing what to watch on streaming services. The letter's lack of a clear call to action further undermines its effectiveness, as AI's release is inevitable and resistance only delays the inevitable. Gowers's position is pragmatic: recognize the changes, adapt, and find the least unsatisfactory way forward.

## Counterpoints

- The letter's core worry that AI-generated results will be too numerous to digest properly, but Gowers argues this is manageable through selective digestion and parallel work.
- The concern that AI will destroy the motivation for young mathematicians, but Gowers suggests this is a social structure issue that can be addressed with imaginative mentoring.
- The fear that AI will produce opaque proofs, but Gowers notes that current AI proofs often build on human work and are not entirely opaque.
- The letter's emphasis on conceptual understanding over problem-solving, which Gowers argues misrepresents the spectrum of mathematical temperaments.
- The idea that AI will make mathematicians obsolete, but Gowers counters that the value of human mathematical experts extends beyond finding proofs.
