## Thesis

AI's disruption of mathematics is an opportunity for the field to evolve and thrive, not a death knell, and mathematicians should embrace it.

## Argument

The narrow view of mathematics as solving problems is being disrupted, but the broader culture of rigorous reasoning and communication remains stable and essential. The challenge is to pursue mathematical understanding in the age of AI, which Avigad addresses with three directives: solve harder problems, think bigger thoughts, and try new things. AI can handle routine problems, but it may miss creativity and higher-level strategizing. Mathematicians should focus on problems that require insight and on expanding the field's conceptual horizons, as seen in the historical examples of Riemann and others. Engaging with AI and proof assistants is a legitimate form of mathematical practice, not mere technical work. This engagement will keep the discipline strong and relevant, and the message to the next generation is one of support and optimism. The host echoes this, urging a glass-half-full perspective and highlighting the unprecedented opportunities for discovery.

## Counterpoints

- The job market for mathematicians will contract, and some argue we should turn inward to preserve traditional skills.
- AI-generated solutions to open problems are often cobbled together from existing techniques, lacking deeper insight.
- There is a risk that AI will surpass humans in the utilitarian value of mathematics, reducing the need for human mathematicians.
- The value of mathematics is tied to producing new interesting proofs, a view that may be skewed within academia.
