## Thesis

AI's disruption of mathematics is a blessing, not a curse, and mathematicians should embrace it by solving harder problems, thinking bigger thoughts, and trying new things.

## Argument

The disruption is real: routine results that once made respectable publications can now be generated by AI, leaving mathematicians worried about their future. But this narrow view misses the stable core of mathematics as a culture of rigorous reasoning and communication. The challenge is to pursue mathematical understanding, not just solve problems. Avigad offers three answers. First, solve harder problems: AI can cobble together existing techniques, but it may miss creativity and higher-level strategizing. There are still hard questions and ambitious research programs. Second, think bigger thoughts: we choose what's interesting, and the most admired mathematics often comes from nowhere, like Riemann's lecture that revolutionized geometry and later enabled general relativity. No reinforcement learning setup could have evaluated Riemann's decisions; the benefits are diffuse and the time horizon too long. We should care about AI only insofar as results are interesting to us. Third, try new things: technologies themselves raise new questions, and mathematicians should be actively involved in understanding and using them. This is not without precedent—algebraic methods, infinitesimals, set-theoretic abstraction, and computation were all alien when new. Developing symbolic automation or training a neural network can be as much a contribution as proving a theorem. Engaging with new technologies will keep the discipline strong. The host adds that the math job market was already brutal, and AI will make it harder, but for those who love math, there's never been a better time.

## Counterpoints

- Some argue that as the job market contracts, we should turn inward to preserve traditional mathematical skills; Avigad disagrees, saying engaging with new technologies will keep the discipline strong.
- A Hacker News commenter credits Lean and mathlib rather than AI for progress, noting that automatic theorem provers haven't seen breakthroughs in other stacks.
- Another commenter worries that society values mathematicians for purely utilitarian reasons, and AI may surpass humans in those pursuits.
- The host notes that some established mathematicians demanded Caltech undergrads cancel their AI mathathon, showing resistance within the community.
