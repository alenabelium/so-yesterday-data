## TL;DR

A standardized format for mathematical proofs in the post-AI era, where the main theorem is easy to follow given lemmas that are either intuitively obvious or supported by a compelling narrative. This prioritizes human understanding over exhaustive formal detail, addressing the overwhelming volume of AI-generated papers.

## The argument

### Define the Core Format
- **Anchor Timestamps**: ['00:02:34']
- **Claim**: A proof is in expository normal form if: (1) the theorem is preceded by lemmas, (2) the theorem's proof is easy assuming the lemmas, and (3) the lemmas are either intuitively obvious or explained in a compelling narrative that makes the reader wish they were obvious.
- **Role**: definition

### Address the AI Paper Avalanche
- **Anchor Timestamps**: ['00:03:57']
- **Claim**: With AI churning out mountains of papers, readers rely on AI summaries. Expository normal form lets the author provide the summary directly, bypassing the middleman, and allows readers to understand why a theorem is correct without wading through full proofs or trusting Lean blindly.
- **Role**: evidence

### Illustrate with Intuitive Lemma
- **Anchor Timestamps**: ['00:05:00']
- **Claim**: The fundamental theorem of calculus is proved using the mean value theorem as an intuitively obvious lemma. The proof becomes a simple telescoping sum, showing how a non-obvious theorem can be made clear through an obvious lemma.
- **Role**: evidence

### Illustrate with Narrative Lemma
- **Anchor Timestamps**: ['00:09:23']
- **Claim**: The fundamental theorem of algebra is proved using two lemmas: the Cauchy integral formula (non-obvious but explained by the 'holographic' story of complex analysis) and Liouville's theorem (derived from the first). This shows how a compelling narrative can support a non-obvious lemma.
- **Role**: evidence

### Address the 'Perception Engine' Objection
- **Anchor Timestamps**: ['00:16:20']
- **Claim**: Critics argue that the format optimizes for a 'sense of understanding' and could produce plausible but false statements. The speaker acknowledges this risk but counters that in practice, readers don't check details anyway; the format at least provides a high-level understanding, and formal verification can be layered on.
- **Role**: counter

## Evidence and caveats

The speaker provides two worked examples: the fundamental theorem of calculus (using the mean value theorem as an intuitive lemma) and the fundamental theorem of algebra (using Cauchy's integral formula and Liouville's theorem, explained via the 'holographic' narrative of complex analysis). He notes these are not formal proofs; details are withheld, and Lean wouldn't accept them. He acknowledges criticisms: the format could be a 'perception engine' for false statements, and the 'obvious' lemma may only be obvious to those who already understand it. He also concedes that the definition is unfalsifiable, which is a feature for job security but a weakness for rigor. The speaker suggests that formal verification (e.g., Lean) can be layered on to address concerns, but the core value is human understanding.
