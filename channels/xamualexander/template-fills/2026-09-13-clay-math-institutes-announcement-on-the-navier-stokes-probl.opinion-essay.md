## Thesis

OpenAI's AI has solved the Navier-Stokes Millennium Problem, but the Clay Mathematics Institute's deliberately vague announcement and outdated verification rules signal a struggle to adapt to AI-driven mathematics.

## Argument

The announcement's vagueness is telling. It never names OpenAI, instead saying the problem has 'apparently been settled.' This is a deliberate move to buy time while they figure out how to handle credit and verification. The rules, which require publication in a peer-reviewed journal and two years of scrutiny, are an anachronism. AI has trivialized formal verification with tools like Lean, making the old process obsolete. The real question is credit: OpenAI won't claim the prize, so who gets it? The rules allow Clay to recognize prior work, and they'll likely give it to a human whose insights were crucial. This is a pattern we'll see again as AI solves more Millennium Problems. The deeper issue is the fear that AI proofs are black boxes that don't advance understanding. But that's overblown—we can analyze these proofs, even with AI's help, and they will open new areas of mathematics. The speed of AI progress makes the old timelines feel like centuries, and we're living in a singularity of our own.

## Counterpoints

- AI proofs are black boxes that don't help us understand the universe.
- The two-year peer-review rule is outdated and should be scrapped.
- OpenAI's solution might depend on prior human insights, complicating credit.
- The announcement's vagueness is a sign of uncertainty, not strategy.
