## Thesis

AI's mathematical strengths lie in broad knowledge and brute-force search, making it good at finding examples and counterexamples, but humans may retain an edge in conceptual, deep-probing proofs.

## Argument

Gowers notes that AI's most celebrated results, like the non-sofic group and the Ramsey number bound, are examples or counterexamples rather than universal theorems. He argues this isn't because AI has a special affinity for existential statements—many theorems can be recast existentially—but because its methods align with certain problem types. AI excels when the path to a solution involves trying many standard ideas until one works, leveraging its vast knowledge and speed. This suits methods like testing ready-made examples, 'just do it' proofs, and probabilistic constructions. However, methods requiring judgment about promising directions—using metavariables, proving the opposite, successive approximation—may still favor humans. Gowers suggests AI's lack of intuition is evident in its tendency to offer superficially promising but ultimately unproductive approaches. He speculates that AI's training data, filled with polished proofs, may not teach it the art of pruning the search tree, and its speed might remove the incentive to do so. The true test of human-level mathematical ability would be a proof that is surprising, beautiful, and natural, like the 2016 capset solution.

## Counterpoints

- AI can also prove universal statements, but its strongest results are examples or counterexamples.
- The distinction between example and counterexample is context-dependent, not purely logical.
- AI might develop human-like intuition as an emergent property of scaling, possibly within a year or two.
- A different reward structure during training could encourage more human-like research behavior.
