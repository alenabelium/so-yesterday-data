## Thesis

AI's mathematical strengths lie in broad knowledge and brute-force search, making it good at finding counterexamples but not yet at producing surprising, beautiful, and natural proofs.

## Argument

The mechanism is the combination of two capabilities: vast mathematical knowledge and the ability to explore many branches of a search tree. These make AI well-suited to tasks with a probabilistic element, where the best method is to try many ideas until one works. This style naturally leads to finding counterexamples or examples, as seen in the non-sofic group and Ramsey number results. However, the distinction between an example and a counterexample is subtle, depending on whether the statement was believed to be true. The key is that AI's success comes from methods that are not necessarily new, but are found by brute force. This is why AI excels at testing ready-made examples, using the 'just do it' method, and applying probabilistic methods. In contrast, methods requiring a sense of which approach is promising—like using metavariables or successive approximation—may still favor humans. The host argues that AI's lack of this intuition is evident in interactions, where it offers approaches that sound promising but fail upon scrutiny. The host concludes that a true sign of human-level mathematical ability would be a proof that is new, surprising, but later seems beautiful and natural, like the capset solution.

## Counterpoints

- AI can also prove universal statements, not just find counterexamples.
- The distinction between an example and a counterexample is not always clear-cut, as seen in Vinogradov's theorem.
- AI might develop human-like intuition as an emergent property of scaling.
- AI's successes might be due to finding arguments in the literature, not original thought.
