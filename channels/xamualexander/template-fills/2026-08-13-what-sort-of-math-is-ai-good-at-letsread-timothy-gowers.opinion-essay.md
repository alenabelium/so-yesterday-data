## Thesis

AI's mathematical strengths lie in broad knowledge and brute-force search, making it adept at finding counterexamples and examples, while humans may retain an edge in conceptual, deep-probing proofs.

## Argument

Gowers observes that AI's most celebrated results, like the non-sofic group and Ramsey number bounds, are examples or counterexamples rather than universal theorems. This isn't because AI prefers existential statements—many theorems are logically existential—but because its methods align with certain problem types. AI excels at tasks with a probabilistic element: it can try many ideas quickly, leveraging vast knowledge and speed. This suits methods like testing ready-made examples, 'just do it' proofs, and probabilistic constructions. However, methods requiring intuition about promising directions—using metavariables, proving the opposite, successive approximation—may favor humans, who can prune the search tree effectively. Gowers suggests AI's style is 'try a lot until you get lucky,' which explains its counterexample successes. Yet, he notes a potential barrier: AI lacks the incentive to prune, and its training data hides the thought processes behind proofs. A true sign of human-level AI would be producing surprising, beautiful, and natural proofs, like the capset solution, which are hard to find by chance.

## Counterpoints

- AI can also prove universal statements, but its strongest results are often counterexamples.
- The distinction between example and counterexample is context-dependent, not purely logical.
- AI might develop human-like intuition as an emergent property, but current models lack it.
- AI's successes might stem from inefficient brute force, not deep understanding.
