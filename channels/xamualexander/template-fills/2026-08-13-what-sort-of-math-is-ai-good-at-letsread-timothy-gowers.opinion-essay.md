## Thesis

AI's mathematical strength lies in finding examples and counterexamples through broad knowledge and brute-force search, not in producing deep, conceptual proofs, which remain a human advantage for now.

## Argument

Gowers observes that AI's most celebrated results, like the non-sofic group and Ramsey number bounds, are examples or counterexamples rather than universal theorems. This isn't because AI prefers existential statements—many theorems are logically existential—but because its methods align with certain proof-finding strategies. AI excels at testing ready-made examples, using standard constructions, and applying probabilistic methods, all of which leverage its vast knowledge and speed. These methods suit problems where trying many ideas until one works is effective. In contrast, humans retain an edge in tasks requiring intuition to prune the search tree, such as using metavariables, attempting to prove the opposite, or successive approximation. Gowers suggests that AI's lack of this intuition is evident in its tendency to offer superficially promising but ultimately shallow approaches. He posits that AI's training data, filled with polished proofs, doesn't teach it to judge promising directions, and its brute-force approach removes the incentive to develop such judgment. The true test of human-level mathematical ability would be a proof that is surprising, beautiful, and natural, like the 2016 capset solution, which would be hard to find by chance.

## Counterpoints

- Some might argue that AI's successes are just examples, not counterexamples, but the distinction is context-dependent and often blurred.
- One might claim that AI's lack of intuition is a temporary scaling issue, but Gowers notes that training data lacks examples of fruitful vs. unfruitful research directions.
- A skeptic might say that AI's brute-force methods will eventually overcome any barrier, but Gowers suggests that certain problems have search trees too large even for computers without pruning.
- Some might believe that human intuition is magical or quantum-based, as Penrose suggests, but Gowers doubts this and sees it as an emergent property that AI might eventually acquire.
