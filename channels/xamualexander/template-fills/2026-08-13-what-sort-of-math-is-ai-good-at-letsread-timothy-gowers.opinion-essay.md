## Thesis

AI's mathematical strengths—broad knowledge and brute-force search—make it good at finding examples and counterexamples, but humans may retain an edge in conceptual, deep-probing proofs.

## Argument

Gowers observes that AI's most celebrated results, like the non-sofic group and the Ramsey number bound, are examples or counterexamples rather than universal theorems. He argues this isn't because AI prefers existential statements—many theorems are naturally existential—but because its methods align with certain problem types. AI excels when a problem has a probabilistic element, allowing it to try many ideas until one works. This suits methods like testing ready-made examples, 'just do it' proofs, and probabilistic constructions. However, methods requiring intuition about promising directions—like using metavariables, proving the opposite, or successive approximation—may favor humans. Gowers suggests AI's lack of incentive to prune search trees, due to its speed, might be a barrier. A true sign of human-level AI would be producing proofs that are new, surprising, yet beautiful and natural, like the capset solution.

## Counterpoints

- AI can also prove universal statements, but its strongest theorems don't reach the level of its counterexamples.
- The distinction between example and counterexample is context-dependent, not purely logical.
- AI might develop human-like intuition as an emergent property, but current models lack it.
- AI's successes might be due to inefficient but fast search, not deep understanding.
