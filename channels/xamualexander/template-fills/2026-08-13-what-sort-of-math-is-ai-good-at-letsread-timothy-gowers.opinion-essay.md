## Thesis

AI's mathematical strength lies in broad knowledge and brute-force search, making it good at finding examples and counterexamples, while humans may retain an edge in conceptual, deep-probing proofs.

## Argument

Gowers starts by noting that AI's most famous results—like the non-sofic group and the Ramsey number bound—were solved via counterexamples or examples, not universal proofs. But he warns against a naive logical distinction: Vinogradov's theorem is a universal statement, yet it's a theorem, not a counterexample. The real difference is contextual: we call something a counterexample when it disproves a claim we had good reason to believe. For instance, the non-sofic group was widely expected, so it's more an example than a counterexample.

What AI is actually good at, Gowers argues, is two things: knowing a lot of math and being able to try many ideas at enormous speed. This suits tasks with a probabilistic element—try lots of things until you get lucky. Humans, at least for now, are better at surprising, conceptual arguments that require digging deeper into the problem.

He then lists eight methods for finding counterexamples, from testing ready-made examples to using metavariables or probabilistic methods. AI is well-suited to the first, third, and seventh—standard methods with lots of training data. But the others—using metavariables, proving the opposite, successive approximation—require a sense of whether an approach is promising. Gowers suspects AI lacks this intuition, noting that ChatGPT often offers approaches that sound promising but fall apart on inspection, and it repeatedly narrows questions without progress.
