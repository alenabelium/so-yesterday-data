## Thesis

AI's mathematical strengths—broad knowledge and brute-force search—make it good at finding counterexamples, but humans may retain an edge in conceptual, deep-probing proofs.

## Argument

Gowers observes that AI's most notable successes, like the non-sofic group and Ramsey number results, are counterexamples or examples rather than universal theorems. He argues that this isn't due to a special affinity for existential statements, but because AI's advantages—broad knowledge and brute-force search—align with methods for finding examples. These methods include testing ready-made examples, using probabilistic methods, and 'just do it' proofs, which are well-suited to AI's style of 'try many things until lucky.' Conversely, methods requiring intuition about promising directions, like using metavariables or successive approximation, may favor humans. Gowers suggests that AI's lack of incentive to prune search trees, due to its speed, might be a barrier to achieving human-like intuition. He proposes that a true sign of human-level AI would be producing proofs that are surprising, beautiful, and natural, like the 2016 capset solution.

## Counterpoints

- AI can also prove universal statements, but its strongest results are counterexamples.
- Some counterexample-finding methods require intuition about promising directions, where humans may have an advantage.
- AI's successes might be due to methods that are inefficient but compensated by speed and knowledge, not deep reflection.
- AI's training data lacks examples of fruitful and less fruitful research directions, which may hinder its ability to develop intuition.
