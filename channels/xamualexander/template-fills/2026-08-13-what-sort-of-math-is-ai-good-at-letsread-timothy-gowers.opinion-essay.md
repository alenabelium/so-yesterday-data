## Thesis

AI's mathematical strength lies in finding examples and counterexamples via broad knowledge and brute-force search, not in producing deep, conceptual proofs where humans may retain an edge.

## Argument

The mechanism is probabilistic. Gowers observes that AI's most celebrated results—the non-sofic group, the Ramsey number bound—are examples or counterexamples, not universal theorems. This isn't because AI prefers existential statements; rather, its twin advantages—encyclopedic knowledge and vast search speed—make it ideal for tasks where the best method is to "try many things until lucky." This style suits certain counterexample-finding strategies: testing ready-made examples, using the "just do it" method, or applying probabilistic constructions. But it struggles with methods requiring judgment about promising directions, like using metavariables, proving the opposite, or successive approximation. These demand a sense of progress that can prune the search tree—a human intuition Gowers suspects AI lacks, as his interactions with ChatGPT show: promising approaches that collapse on inspection, and repeated narrowing without progress. The barrier may persist because training data hides the thought processes behind polished proofs, and AI has no incentive to prune when brute force is cheap. A true sign of human-level math would be a proof that is new, surprising, yet beautiful and natural—like the 2016 capset breakthrough—not just a lucky find.

## Counterpoints

- AI can also prove universal statements, but its strongest theorems don't reach the level of its counterexamples.
- The distinction between example and counterexample is context-dependent, not purely logical.
- AI might develop human-like intuition as an emergent property of scaling, possibly within a year or two.
- A different reward structure—punishing wasted search or cheating—could make AI approach problems more like humans.
