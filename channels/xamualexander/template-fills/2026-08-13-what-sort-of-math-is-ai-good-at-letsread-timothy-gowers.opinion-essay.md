## Thesis

AI's mathematical strengths lie in broad knowledge and brute-force search, making it good at finding examples and counterexamples via probabilistic methods, while humans may retain an edge in conceptual, deep-probing proofs.

## Argument

Gowers observes that AI's most celebrated results, like the non-sofic group and the Ramsey number lower bound, are examples or counterexamples rather than universal theorems. The logical form of these problems often involves existential quantification, but the key is not the quantifier itself—it's the method. AI excels at tasks where the best approach is to try many ideas until one works, leveraging its vast knowledge and speed. This style suits methods like testing ready-made examples, 'just do it' proofs, and probabilistic constructions. However, methods requiring a sense of promising directions—like using metavariables, proving the opposite, or successive approximation—may still favor humans, who can prune the search tree effectively. Gowers suggests that AI's lack of this intuition is evident in interactions, where it offers promising-sounding approaches that fail upon scrutiny. The real test for human-level AI would be producing proofs that are surprising, beautiful, and natural, like the 2016 capset solution, which would indicate genuine conceptual insight rather than brute-force search.

## Counterpoints

- AI can also prove universal statements, but its strongest results are often examples or counterexamples.
- Some counterexample-finding methods, like using metavariables or proving the opposite, require assessing promising directions, where humans may have an advantage.
- AI's apparent lack of intuition might be overcome by scaling or different reward structures, but it's hard to tell if an idea is truly original or just hidden in training data.
