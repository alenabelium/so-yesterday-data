## Thesis

AI's mathematical strengths lie in finding examples and counterexamples through broad knowledge and brute-force search, not in producing surprising, beautiful proofs that require deep human intuition.

## Argument

Gowers observes that AI's most celebrated results, like the non-sofic group and Ramsey number bounds, are examples or counterexamples rather than universal theorems. The distinction isn't logical form—Vinogradov's theorem and Gluskin's counterexample share quantifier structures—but the nature of the task: whether the difficulty lies in constructing an object or in proving a deep property. AI excels when the method is 'try many things until lucky,' leveraging its vast knowledge and speed to explore branches humans would prune. This suits methods like testing ready-made examples, 'just do it' proofs, and probabilistic constructions. However, methods requiring judgment about promising directions—metavariables, proving the opposite, successive approximation—may still favor humans, as AI lacks the intuition to prune search trees efficiently. Gowers suspects this intuition might be an emergent property, but notes AI's training data lacks examples of fruitful versus unfruitful research paths, and its success so far may rely on inefficient but fast search. A true sign of human-level math would be a proof that is surprising yet natural, like the 2016 capset solution, not just a counterexample found by brute force.

## Counterpoints

- AI can also prove universal statements, but its strongest theorems don't match its counterexamples in significance.
- Some counterexample methods, like using metavariables or proving the opposite, require assessing promising directions, where humans may still excel.
- The apparent lack of intuition might be an emergent property that appears with scaling, as AI learns from practice rather than imitation.
- A different reward structure, penalizing excessive search or cheating, could make AI approach problems more like humans.
