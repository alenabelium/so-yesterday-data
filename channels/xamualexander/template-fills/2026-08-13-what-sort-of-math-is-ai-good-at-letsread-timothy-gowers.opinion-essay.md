## Thesis

AI's mathematical strength lies in finding examples and counterexamples through broad knowledge and brute-force search, not in producing deep, conceptual proofs, which remain a human advantage for now.

## Argument

Gowers' analysis starts by noting that AI's most celebrated results, like the non-sofic group and Ramsey number bounds, are examples or counterexamples, not universal theorems. This isn't because AI prefers existential statements—many theorems can be recast existentially. Instead, AI's edge comes from two concrete abilities: vast mathematical knowledge and the speed to try many approaches. This makes it excel at tasks with a probabilistic element, where 'try many things until lucky' works. Methods like testing ready-made examples, 'just do it' proofs, and probabilistic constructions fit this style. But methods requiring judgment—using metavariables, proving the opposite, successive approximation—demand a sense of promising directions, which AI currently lacks. Gowers observes that AI often proposes approaches that sound promising but fail on scrutiny, and it narrows questions without progress. This isn't just a scaling issue; AI's training data lacks examples of fruitful versus fruitless research paths, and its success so far may stem from inefficient but fast search. A true sign of human-level math would be a proof that is surprising, beautiful, and natural, like the 2016 capset solution, not just a clever counterexample.

## Counterpoints

- AI can also prove universal statements, but its strongest theorems don't match the level of its counterexamples.
- Some counterexample methods, like using metavariables or proving the opposite, require human-like intuition about promising directions.
- AI's apparent lack of deep reflection might be hidden by its ability to find arguments in the literature, making it hard to test.
- A different reward structure, punishing inefficient search, might push AI toward more human-like research methods.
