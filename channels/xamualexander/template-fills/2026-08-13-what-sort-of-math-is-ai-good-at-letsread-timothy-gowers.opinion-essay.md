## Thesis

AI's mathematical strength lies in finding examples and counterexamples via broad knowledge and brute-force search, not in producing the surprising, beautiful, and natural proofs that mark human-level mathematical ability.

## Argument

Gowers' analysis begins with the observation that AI's most celebrated results, like the non-sofic group and the Ramsey number bound, are examples or counterexamples rather than universal theorems. The key is not logical form but the nature of the search. AI excels when the method is 'try many things until lucky' — testing ready-made examples, using probabilistic methods, or applying standard 'just do it' constructions. These align with AI's advantages: vast knowledge and the ability to explore huge search trees. In contrast, methods requiring a sense of promising direction — using metavariables, proving the opposite, successive approximation — demand human intuition. Gowers notes that AI's training data lacks examples of fruitful versus less fruitful research paths, and its speed may remove the incentive to prune search branches. He suggests a barrier may exist, and that a true sign of human-level AI would be a proof as surprising and beautiful as the 2016 capset solution. The conclusion: AI will improve rapidly, but if humans retain an edge, it will be in tasks where pruning the search tree is crucial.

## Counterpoints

- AI can also prove universal statements, but its strongest theorems don't yet match its counterexamples.
- The distinction between example and counterexample is context-dependent, not purely logical.
- AI might develop human-like intuition as an emergent property of scaling, within a year or two.
- AI's apparent lack of intuition could be due to training data hiding thought processes, not a fundamental limit.
- A different reward structure during training might encourage more human-like research behavior.
