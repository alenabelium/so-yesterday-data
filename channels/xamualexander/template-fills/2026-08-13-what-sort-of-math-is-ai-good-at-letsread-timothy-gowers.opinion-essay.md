## Thesis

AI's mathematical strength lies in finding examples and counterexamples through broad knowledge and brute-force search, not in producing the surprising, beautiful, and natural proofs that define human-level mathematical ability.

## Argument

Gowers' analysis begins by noting that AI's most celebrated results, like the non-sofic group and the Ramsey number bound, are examples or counterexamples rather than universal theorems. This isn't because AI is inherently good at existential claims—Vinogradov's theorem is also existential but is a theorem, not a counterexample. The real distinction is in the method: AI excels when the path to a solution involves trying many standard ideas until one works, leveraging its vast knowledge and speed. This suits methods like testing ready-made examples, 'just do it' proofs, and probabilistic constructions. However, methods requiring a sense of promising directions—like using metavariables, proving the opposite, or successive approximation—may still favor humans, who can prune the search tree effectively. Gowers suggests that AI's lack of this intuition is evident in interactions, where it often offers superficially promising but ultimately shallow approaches. The barrier may be real, but if AI ever produces a proof as surprising and beautiful as the capset solution, that would signal true human-level mathematical ability.

## Counterpoints

- AI can also prove universal statements, but its strongest results are still examples or counterexamples.
- Some counterexample-finding methods, like using metavariables or successive approximation, require assessing promising directions, where humans may still have an edge.
- AI's apparent lack of intuition might be an emergent property that appears with scaling, as it has for other capabilities.
