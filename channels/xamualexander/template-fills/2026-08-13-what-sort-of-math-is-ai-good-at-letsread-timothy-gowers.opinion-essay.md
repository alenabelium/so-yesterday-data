## Thesis

AI's mathematical strengths lie in finding examples and counterexamples through broad knowledge and brute-force search, while humans retain an edge in conceptual, deep-probing proofs that require pruning the search tree.

## Argument

Gowers observes that AI's most celebrated mathematical results, like the non-sofic group and the Ramsey number bound, are examples or counterexamples rather than universal theorems. The key is not a preference for existential statements, but that these tasks align with AI's advantages: vast knowledge and the ability to try many approaches quickly. This style suits methods like testing ready-made examples or using probabilistic constructions, where brute force can succeed. However, methods requiring judgment about promising directions—like using metavariables or successive approximation—may still favor humans, who can prune the search tree effectively. Gowers suggests that AI's lack of incentive to prune, due to its speed, might be a barrier to deeper conceptual work. A true sign of human-level AI would be producing proofs that are surprising yet natural, like the capset solution, which are hard to find by chance.

## Counterpoints

- AI can also prove universal statements, not just find counterexamples.
- The distinction between examples and counterexamples is context-dependent, not purely logical.
- AI might develop human-like intuition as an emergent property of scaling.
- A different reward structure during training could encourage more human-like research behavior.
