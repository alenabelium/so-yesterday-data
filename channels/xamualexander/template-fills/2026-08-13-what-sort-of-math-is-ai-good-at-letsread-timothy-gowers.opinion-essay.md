## Thesis

AI's mathematical strength lies in finding examples and counterexamples via broad knowledge and brute-force search, not in producing the deep, surprising, and natural proofs that define human-level mathematical insight.

## Argument

Gowers starts by noting that AI's most celebrated results—like the non-sofic group and the Ramsey number bound—were achieved by constructing counterexamples or examples, not by proving universal theorems. He then dissects what counts as a counterexample, showing that logical form alone doesn't decide it. Vinogradov's theorem on three primes is a universal statement, yet it's a theorem, while Gluskin's Banach-Mazur distance result is an existential statement, yet it's a counterexample. The real difference lies in the mathematical context and the role of the quantified variable. This leads to the core insight: AI's advantages are its vast mathematical knowledge and its ability to try many approaches at high speed. This makes it well-suited for tasks with a probabilistic element, where the best method is to try many ideas until one works. In contrast, humans excel at conceptual, deep-probing proofs that require intuition about promising directions. Gowers lists eight common methods for finding examples, from testing ready-made examples to using metavariables and successive approximation. He argues that AI is likely good at the first set—testing, 'just do it' proofs, probabilistic methods—but may struggle with methods requiring a sense of what is promising, like using metavariables or proving the opposite. He notes that AI's training data lacks examples of fruitful versus less fruitful research directions, and its brute-force approach removes the incentive to prune the search tree. ## Counterpoints

- AI can also prove universal statements, but its strongest results are still counterexamples.
- The distinction between example and counterexample is context-dependent, not purely logical.
- AI might develop human-like intuition as an emergent property of scaling.
- A different reward structure during training could encourage more human-like research methods.
- It's hard to tell if an AI's 'original' idea is just hidden in its training data.
