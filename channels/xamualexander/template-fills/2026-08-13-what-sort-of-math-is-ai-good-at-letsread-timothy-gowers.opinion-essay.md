## Thesis

AI's mathematical strengths lie in finding counterexamples and examples through brute-force search and broad knowledge, not in producing deep, conceptual proofs where human intuition still holds an edge.

## Argument

Gowers' analysis starts by noting that AI's most celebrated results, like the non-sofic group and the Ramsey number bound, are primarily counterexamples or examples rather than universal theorems. This isn't because AI is inherently better at existential statements—many theorems can be recast existentially. Instead, the advantage stems from two concrete capabilities: vast mathematical knowledge and the ability to explore many unpromising branches of a search tree. This makes AI well-suited to methods like testing ready-made examples, 'just do it' proofs, and probabilistic constructions. However, methods requiring judgment about promising directions—using metavariables, proving the opposite, or successive approximation—may still favor humans. The host emphasizes that AI's style is 'try many things until lucky,' which works until the combinatorial explosion becomes too large even for brute force. The real test of human-level mathematical ability, Gowers argues, would be a proof that is surprising, beautiful, and natural, like the 2016 capset solution, which is difficult to find by chance.

## Counterpoints

- AI can also prove universal statements, but its strongest results are still counterexamples.
- The distinction between example and counterexample is context-dependent, not purely logical.
- AI might develop human-like intuition as an emergent property of scaling, but current models lack it.
- A different reward structure during training could encourage more human-like research behavior.
- It's hard to tell if an AI's 'original' idea is just retrieved from training data.
