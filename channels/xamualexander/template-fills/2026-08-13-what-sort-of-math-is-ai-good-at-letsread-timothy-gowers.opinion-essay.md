## Thesis

AI's mathematical strengths—broad knowledge and brute-force search—make it good at finding counterexamples and examples, but humans may retain an edge in conceptual, deep-probing proofs.

## Argument

Gowers starts by noting that AI's most famous successes, like the non-sofic group and the Ramsey number bound, were counterexamples or examples rather than universal theorems. He argues that what counts as a counterexample is subtle: it's not just about logical form, but about whether the result disproves a claim we had good reason to believe. For instance, Vinogradov's theorem is a theorem, not a counterexample, even though it's an existence statement, because the interesting part is proving the universal claim. Gluskin's result, on the other hand, is a counterexample because the difficulty lies in constructing the example itself.

Gowers then proposes that AI's advantage comes from two things: its vast knowledge of standard arguments and its ability to try many approaches quickly. This makes AI good at tasks with a probabilistic element—where you can try lots of ideas until you get lucky. Humans, by contrast, are better at finding surprising, conceptual arguments that require digging deeper into the problem. He lists eight methods for finding counterexamples, arguing that AI is well-suited to testing ready-made examples, using the 'just do it' method, and probabilistic methods, but may struggle with methods requiring a sense of promising directions, like using metavariables or successive approximation.
