## Thesis

AI's mathematical strengths lie in broad knowledge and brute-force search, making it good at finding examples and counterexamples, while humans may retain an edge in conceptual, deep-probing proofs.

## Argument

Gowers starts by noting that AI's most celebrated results, like the non-sofic group and the Ramsey number bound, are examples or counterexamples rather than universal theorems. He argues that this isn't because AI has a special affinity for existential statements, but because its two core strengths—vast knowledge and the ability to try many approaches at high speed—align with the probabilistic nature of finding examples. He contrasts this with human mathematicians, who excel at 'digging deeper' into problems to find surprising, conceptual proofs. Gowers then enumerates eight methods for finding counterexamples, suggesting AI is well-suited to testing ready-made examples, 'just do it' proofs, and probabilistic methods, but may lag in methods requiring judgment about promising directions, like using metavariables or successive approximation. He concludes that AI will likely improve rapidly, but a true sign of human-level mathematical ability would be producing proofs that are new, surprising, yet beautiful and natural.

## Counterpoints

- AI can also prove universal statements, but its strongest results are examples or counterexamples.
- The distinction between example and counterexample is context-dependent, not purely logical.
- AI might develop human-like intuition as an emergent property, but current models lack it.
- AI's efficiency might be an artifact of its speed, masking inefficient search strategies.
