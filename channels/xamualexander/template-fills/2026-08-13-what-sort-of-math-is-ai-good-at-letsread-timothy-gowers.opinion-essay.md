## Thesis

AI's mathematical strengths lie in finding examples and counterexamples through broad knowledge and brute-force search, while humans may retain an edge in conceptual, deep-probing proofs.

## Argument

Gowers observes that AI's most celebrated results, like the non-sofic group and the Ramsey number bound, are examples or counterexamples rather than universal theorems. He argues that this isn't due to a special affinity for existential statements, but because these tasks align with AI's core advantages: vast knowledge and the ability to explore many unpromising branches of the search tree. The logical form of a problem doesn't determine whether it's an example or a theorem; context matters. For instance, Vinogradov's theorem is a universal statement, while Gluskin's result is an example, despite similar quantifier structures. AI's style of 'try many things until lucky' suits methods like testing ready-made examples, 'just do it' proofs, and probabilistic constructions. However, methods requiring intuition about promising directions—like using metavariables or successive approximation—may still favor humans. Gowers suggests that AI's lack of incentive to prune search trees, due to its speed, might be a barrier to achieving human-like intuition. A true sign of human-level AI would be a proof that is surprising, beautiful, and natural, like the capset solution.

## Counterpoints

- AI can also prove universal statements, but its strongest results are examples or counterexamples.
- The distinction between example and counterexample is context-dependent, not purely logical.
- AI's apparent lack of intuition might be overcome by scaling or different reward structures.
- It's hard to test whether AI's ideas are original or just retrieved from literature.
