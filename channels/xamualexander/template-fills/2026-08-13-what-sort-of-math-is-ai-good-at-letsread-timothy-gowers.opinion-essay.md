## Thesis

AI's mathematical strengths lie in finding counterexamples and examples through broad knowledge and brute-force search, while humans may retain an edge in conceptual, deep-probing proofs.

## Argument

Gowers observes that AI's most celebrated results, like the non-sofic group and Ramsey number bounds, are examples or counterexamples rather than universal theorems. This isn't because AI prefers existential statements—many theorems can be reformulated existentially. Instead, AI's advantage stems from two traits: vast mathematical knowledge and the ability to try many approaches quickly. This suits tasks with a probabilistic element, where success comes from trying many ideas until one works. Humans, by contrast, excel at conceptual arguments requiring deep probing. Gowers lists eight counterexample-finding methods, noting AI is well-suited to testing ready-made examples, 'just do it' proofs, and probabilistic methods. But methods like using metavariables or successive approximation require judging promising directions—a skill AI may lack. Gowers suspects AI's successes come from inefficient but fast search, and that a true sign of human-level math would be surprising, beautiful, natural proofs, like the capset solution.

## Counterpoints

- AI can also prove universal statements, but its strongest results are often counterexamples.
- Some counterexample methods require intuition about promising directions, where humans may have an edge.
- AI's apparent intuition might be emergent from scaling, but training data lacks examples of fruitful research directions.
- AI might lack incentive to prune search trees because it can afford brute-force exploration.
- It's hard to tell if an AI's idea is original or just retrieved from literature.
