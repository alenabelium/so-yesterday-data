## Thesis

AI's mathematical strengths lie in broad knowledge and brute-force search, making it good at finding counterexamples and examples, but humans may retain an edge in conceptual, deep-probing proofs that require intuition about promising.

## Argument

Gowers' analysis starts with the observation that AI's most celebrated results—like the non-sofic group and the Ramsey number bound—are counterexamples or examples, not universal theorems. He argues this isn't because AI has a special affinity for existential statements, since many theorems can be recast as existence claims. Instead, the real advantage comes from two AI traits: vast mathematical knowledge and the ability to try many approaches at high speed. This makes AI well-suited for tasks with a probabilistic element, where the best method is to try lots of ideas until one works. Humans, by contrast, are better at finding surprising, conceptual arguments that require digging deeper into a problem. Gowers lists eight common methods for finding counterexamples, noting that AI excels at testing ready-made examples, using the 'just do it' method, and applying probabilistic or typical-example approaches. However, methods requiring metavariables, proving the opposite, or successive approximation demand a greater ability to assess whether an approach is promising—a skill where humans may still have an advantage. He suggests that AI's lack of incentive to prune search trees, due to its speed, might be a barrier to developing this intuition. The ultimate sign of human-level mathematical ability would be a proof that is new, surprising, and beautiful, like the 2016 capset solution.

## Counterpoints

- AI can also prove universal statements, but its strongest results are often counterexamples or examples, not theorems.
- The distinction between an example and a counterexample is context-dependent, not purely logical, so AI's success isn't simply about existential claims.
- AI's apparent intuition might be an emergent property of scaling, and it could develop human-like intuition within a year or two.
- AI's successes might be due to methods that are extremely inefficient for humans, but its speed and knowledge mask the combinatorial explosion.
- It's hard to test whether AI has genuine intuition because a seemingly original idea might just be hidden in its training data.
