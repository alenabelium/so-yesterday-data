## Thesis

AI's mathematical strengths—broad knowledge and brute-force search—make it good at finding examples and counterexamples, but humans may retain an edge in conceptual, deep-probing proofs.

## Argument

Gowers observes that AI's most celebrated results are counterexamples or examples, not universal theorems. He argues this isn't because AI prefers existential statements, but because its strengths—broad knowledge and brute-force search—align with methods like testing ready-made examples, 'just do it' proofs, and probabilistic methods. These methods involve trying many ideas until one works, which suits AI's style. Conversely, methods requiring intuition about promising directions—using metavariables, proving the opposite, successive approximation—may favor humans, who can prune the search tree effectively. Gowers suggests AI's lack of incentive to prune, due to its speed, might be a barrier. He proposes that a true sign of human-level AI would be producing surprising, beautiful, and natural proofs, like the capset solution, which are hard to find by chance.

## Counterpoints

- AI can also prove universal statements, but the strongest ones are often examples or counterexamples.
- Some counterexample-finding methods require assessing promising directions, where humans might have an advantage.
- AI's apparent lack of intuition might be an emergent property that appears with scaling or different training rewards.
- It's hard to tell if an AI's original idea is truly original or just hidden in its training data.
