## Thesis

AI's mathematical strength lies in finding counterexamples and examples through broad knowledge and brute-force search, not in producing deep, conceptual proofs where human intuition still holds an edge.

## Argument

Gowers observes that AI's most celebrated results, like the non-sofic group and the Ramsey number bound, are examples or counterexamples rather than universal theorems. This isn't because AI has a special affinity for existential statements—many theorems can be recast existentially. Instead, AI's advantage stems from two concrete capabilities: vast mathematical knowledge and the ability to explore many unpromising branches of a search tree. These traits make AI well-suited for methods like testing ready-made examples, 'just do it' proofs, and probabilistic constructions, where success comes from trying many ideas until one works. Conversely, methods requiring a sense of promising directions—using metavariables, attempting the opposite, or successive approximation—may still favor humans, who can prune the search tree more effectively. Gowers suggests that if humans retain an edge, it will be in problems with deep, branching search trees where intuition is crucial. The true test of human-level AI would be a proof that is surprising, beautiful, and natural, not just correct.

## Counterpoints

- AI can also prove universal statements, but its strongest results are still counterexamples.
- The distinction between example and counterexample is context-dependent, not purely logical.
- AI might develop human-like intuition as an emergent property of scaling, though current models lack it.
- A seemingly original AI proof might just be a hidden argument from the literature.
- Different reward structures during training could make AI more human-like in its approach.
