## Thesis

AI's mathematical strengths lie in broad knowledge and brute-force search, making it good at finding counterexamples and examples, while humans may retain an edge in conceptual, deep-probing proofs.

## Argument

Gowers observes that AI's most notable successes, like the non-sofic group and Ramsey number results, were counterexamples or examples rather than universal theorems. This isn't because AI prefers existential statements—many theorems are existential in form—but because its methods align with certain types of example-finding. AI excels at tasks with a probabilistic element: trying many ideas until one works. This suits methods like testing ready-made examples, 'just do it' proofs, and probabilistic constructions. However, methods requiring intuition about promising directions—like using metavariables or successive approximation—may still favor humans. Gowers suggests that AI's lack of incentive to prune search trees, due to its speed and knowledge, might be a barrier. He proposes that a true sign of human-level AI would be producing surprising, beautiful, and natural proofs, like the capset solution, which are hard to find by chance.

## Counterpoints

- AI can also prove universal statements, but its strongest results are counterexamples.
- The distinction between example and counterexample is context-dependent, not purely logical.
- AI might develop human-like intuition as an emergent property of scaling.
- AI's successes might be due to inefficient but fast brute-force methods, not deep understanding.
