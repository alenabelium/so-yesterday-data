## Thesis

OpenAI's withdrawal of three AI-generated math papers over a single sign error is not a failure of AI mathematics but a sign of its health, and AI-generated proofs will soon be more reliable than human ones.

## Argument

The mechanism is the error itself. One sign error—the kind a sixth-grader makes—invalidated a single paper, and two other papers collapsed because they depended on it. That's not three failures; it's one pillar knocked out, taking three stories down with it. The withdrawal rate is under 1% of nearly 400 papers, and the error was caught by a human mathematician, not by the AI. But that's not a knock on the AI—it's a testament to the sheer volume of output. The real shift is that the hard part of mathematics has inverted. Traditionally, the proof was the hard part and translating the theorem into formal language was easy. Now, with Lean formal verification, the proof is the easy part—Lean checks it mechanically—and the translation from plain English to Lean is where the risk lives. That's a profound inversion, and it's why the community's skepticism is misplaced. The Hacker News Bayesian argument that a sign error raises the prior for more false proofs is wrong because it ignores the detection rate—we found this error fast, and we have AI tools to help find more. Human mathematics is riddled with errors; journals are full of them. Anthropic found an elliptic curve with rank 31 that contradicted a human-published paper. The difference is that AI-generated math is being checked at scale, with version control and formalization, while human math is checked slowly and imperfectly. ## Counterpoints

- Skeptics argue that a sign error indicates a high probability of other basic errors, but this ignores the detection rate and the fact that only one error was found, not three.
- Some claim that unverified proofs should not be released, but the host argues that releasing everything, even with flaws, is a net positive because it informs the community and accelerates progress.
- Critics say that AI-generated proofs will fall apart on closer inspection, but the host counters that Lean verification and AI-assisted checking make this unlikely, unlike the Mochizuki ABC proof.
- The objection that AI labs are hyping their products and thus should be held to a higher standard is acknowledged as fair, but the host argues that the benefits outweigh the risks.
- The concern that AI will eliminate the need for junior mathematicians is dismissed as a reason to halt progress; instead, the host predicts a shift to amateur mathematicians.
