## Thesis

0^0 is universally equal to 1 by definition, and the mathematical community's stubborn refusal to accept this is both amusing and unfounded.

## Argument

The mechanism is definitional: 0^0 = 1 is not a theorem to be proven but a convention baked into the foundations of mathematics. The Lean theorem prover, the highest source of truth in mathematics as of 2026, explicitly defines 0^0 = 1 for real, complex, natural, and integer numbers. This is not a matter of proof; it is a matter of definition. The Maclaurin series for e^x, which converges for all real x, implicitly requires 0^0 = 1 at x = 0, and Wolfram Alpha's refusal to compute this sum is an internal contradiction. The historical confusion traces to Cauchy, who used 0^0 as a symbol for indeterminate forms, not as a statement about the value of 0^0 itself. This conflation of definition with limit behavior has misled even Terence Tao, who in his 2025 paper 'Machine-Assisted Proof' incorrectly claims that 0^0 has different interpretations under different exponentiation concepts. The truth is simple: 0^0 = 1 by definition, and any attempt to 'prove' it, like Möbius's, is a fool's errand that only leads to error and embarrassment. The widespread disagreement stems from this fundamental misunderstanding, and it is hilarious that so many, including AI systems, have been trained to repeat the error.

## Counterpoints

- Some argue 0^0 is indeterminate because the limit of f(x)^g(x) as f and g approach 0 can vary, but this confuses the definition with limit behavior.
- Wolfram Alpha and many calculators return 'undefined' for 0^0, but this is a software implementation choice, not a mathematical truth.
- Terence Tao's claim that 0^0 has different interpretations under different exponentiation concepts is contradicted by Lean, which defines 0^0 = 1 across all number systems.
- The historical debate, including Möbius's flawed proof, led to the false conclusion that 0^0 is undefined, but the proof's failure does not invalidate the definition.
