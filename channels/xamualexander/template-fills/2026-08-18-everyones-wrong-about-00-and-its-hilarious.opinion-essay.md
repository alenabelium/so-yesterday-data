## Thesis

0^0 is equal to 1 by definition, and the mathematical community's stubborn insistence on treating it as undefined or indeterminate is both amusing and unfounded.

## Argument

The contradiction is right there in Wolfram Alpha. Ask it for the Maclaurin series for e^x, and it happily returns the sum from n=0 to infinity of x^n/n!, true for all x. But plug in x=0, and suddenly the sum 'does not exist due to a singularity.' The only reason is that Wolfram Alpha thinks 0^0 is undefined. That's not a mathematical fact; it's a bug in their implementation. The truth is simpler: 0^0 = 1 by definition. There is no proof, because definitions don't need proofs. The Lean theorem prover, the gold standard for verifying AI-generated proofs, defines it that way for real, complex, natural, and integer numbers. If you disagree, you're contradicting the highest source of truth in mathematics as of 2026.

The historical confusion traces back to Cauchy, who used the symbol '0^0' as a shorthand for indeterminate forms in limits. That was a notational convenience, not a statement about the value of 0^0 itself. But the symbol stuck, and calculus textbooks have been spreading the confusion ever since. Even Terence Tao, in his 2025 paper 'Machine-Assisted Proof,' repeats the error, claiming 0^0 has 'slightly different interpretations' across number systems. That's flat wrong. The limit argument is a red herring: when I ask what 0^0 is, I'm not asking about limits. The value is fixed by definition, regardless of any limiting process.
