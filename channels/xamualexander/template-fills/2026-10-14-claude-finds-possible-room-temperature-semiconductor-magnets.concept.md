## TL;DR

AI agents from Claude Opus 5.5 identified two candidate materials for room-temperature antiferromagnetic semiconductors, a class that could enable faster, denser computer memory. One candidate is a newly designed compound, the other was first synthesized in 1999 but overlooked. This discovery highlights AI's potential to accelerate materials science by finding overlooked literature and designing new materials.

### Define the Core Substrate
- **Anchor Timestamps**: ['00:02:37']
- **Claim**: The key to next-generation memory is a material that combines the spin-sorting of ferromagnets with the zero net magnetism of antiferromagnets. This 'Luttinger-compensated' class achieves zero net spin moment while allowing spin-up and spin-down electrons to be separated by energy, enabling spintronic data storage without stray magnetic fields.

## Evidence and caveats

The 1999 candidate belongs to the Prussian blue family and was originally studied for magnetic coupling under pressure; a 2008 study even plotted spin-sorted states but didn't note its Luttinger-compensated nature. The speaker cautions that the 1999 sample is imperfect (powder with water), and the two modeling methods disagree on how much water attenuates spin sorting. Neither band gap nor spin sorting has been measured experimentally. The designed compound's checkerboard pattern is thermodynamically unstable, making fabrication difficult. The speaker remains cautiously optimistic, drawing parallels to AI's early 'warm-up' contributions in mathematics that preceded a flood of results.
