## TL;DR

A new class of materials predicted by AI agents could combine the best of ferromagnets and antiferromagnets for spintronics. These 'Luttinger-compensated semiconductors' have zero net magnetism but sort electrons by spin, potentially enabling faster, denser computer memory. Two candidates have been identified, one of which was synthesized in 1999 but overlooked.

## The argument

### Define the Core Substrate
- **Anchor Timestamps**: ['00:02:37']
- **Claim**: The key to next-gen spintronics is a material that combines the spin-sorting ability of ferromagnets with the zero net magnetism of antiferromagnets. This is achieved in 'Luttinger-compensated materials,' where spin-up and spin-down atoms are in non-equivalent environments, allowing energy separation despite zero net moment.
- **Role**: definition

### Evidence: The 1999 Candidate
- **Anchor Timestamps**: ['00:13:26']
- **Claim**: A material first synthesized in 1999, a Prussian blue analog, was identified by AI agents as a Luttinger-compensated semiconductor. It was created for a different purpose and overlooked, but it retains magnetic order up to 376 K, making it a promising candidate for room-temperature spintronics.
- **Role**: evidence

### Counter: The Fabrication Challenge
- **Anchor Timestamps**: ['00:12:10']
- **Claim**: The designed candidate, yttrium-barium-manganese-iron-O5, requires a perfect checkerboard atomic arrangement that falls apart at high temperatures. Standard synthesis would likely yield a chaotic crystal, losing spin sorting, making it difficult or even impossible to create in a usable form.
- **Role**: counter

### Synthesis: The Path Forward
- **Anchor Timestamps**: ['00:17:47']
- **Claim**: The 1999 candidate's existence proves the concept is realizable, but its imperfect powder form shows a small residual magnetic moment. The next step is to recreate the material in perfect crystalline form and directly measure its spin sorting, which could lead to practical spintronic devices.
- **Role**: synthesis

## Evidence and caveats

The 1999 candidate, a Prussian blue analog, was first synthesized in 1999 and its spin sorting was described in a 2008 study, but it was never recognized as a Luttinger-compensated semiconductor. The AI agents predicted a band gap of 2.1 eV with spin-sorted windows of 2.6 eV for holes and 1.6 eV for electrons. The 1999 sample remained magnetically ordered up to 376 K (103°C above room temperature). However, the sample was a powder with water in pores, showing a residual magnetic moment of 0.125 Bohr magnetons per formula unit, whereas a perfect crystal would have zero. The two modeling methods disagree on how much water attenuates the spin-sorting effect. The designed candidate, yttrium-barium-manganese-iron-O5, has a predicted band gap of 2.35 eV with windows of 1.0 eV and 1.4 eV, but its fabrication is challenging due to the need for a perfect checkerboard pattern that disintegrates at ~950 K. The speaker remains cautiously optimistic, drawing parallels to AI's early contributions in mathematics.
