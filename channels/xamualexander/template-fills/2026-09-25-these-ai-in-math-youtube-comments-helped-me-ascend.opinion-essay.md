## Thesis

Fears that AI will make mathematicians obsolete are overblown; instead, AI makes mathematics more accessible and should be embraced as a tool for solving harder problems.

## Argument

The panic among mathematicians, exemplified by the 25 Fields medalists' declaration, is a reaction to threatened prestige and incentives, not a genuine crisis for the field. The real value of mathematics has always been in solving problems, not just producing proofs for their own sake. AI, like the calculator before it, will not stop mathematical research; it will raise the bar. Mathematicians should pivot to using AI to tackle harder problems and explore new areas, as seen in the collaborative AI-assisted proof of Medvedev's logic. The field will adapt, with new roles and possibly more mathematicians, even if the work becomes unrecognizable. The focus should be on what can be done with AI, not on resisting it.

## Counterpoints

- Some argue that mathematicians value conceptual understanding over problem-solving, but this is a recent narrative driven by AI fears; historically, proofs were the primary goal.
- The subjectivity of evaluating motivated explanations is a concern, but mathematics already relies on subjective judgments about importance and interest.
- AI could become a 'hard drug' providing diminishing returns and leading to addiction, as seen in software engineering, but this is not a reason to abandon it.
- There are fears that AI will solve all problems, making mathematicians obsolete, but this ignores the human role in interpreting and building on AI results.
- The lack of a plan for income distribution (e.g., UBI) in the face of AI is a valid concern, but it is a broader societal issue, not specific to mathematics.
