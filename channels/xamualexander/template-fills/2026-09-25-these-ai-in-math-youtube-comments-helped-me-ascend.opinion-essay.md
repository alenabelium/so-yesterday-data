## Thesis

Fears that AI will make mathematicians obsolete are overblown; instead, AI makes mathematics more accessible and should be embraced as a tool for solving harder problems, not resisted.

## Argument

The doom and gloom about AI replacing mathematicians is overrated. Mathematicians will continue to have employment, though the nature of the work may change. The real crisis is an identity crisis within the mathematical community, where problem-solving prowess has been overvalued at the expense of conceptual understanding. AI challenges this hierarchy, making frontier mathematics accessible to a wider audience, as seen when a civil engineer uses AI to 'touch' math concepts. This democratization is a positive development, not a threat. The 25 Fields medalists' letter against AI-assisted proofs is a reaction to losing prestige, not a genuine concern for understanding. Proofs remain objective, but the choice of which theorems are interesting is subjective, a fact that already exists in the field. Instead of resisting, mathematicians should ask how they can use AI to solve harder problems and make new innovations, just as the calculator didn't stop research but raised the bar. The future belongs to those who embrace AI as a collaborator, not those who cling to the past.

## Counterpoints

- Some argue that AI will solve all problems, making mathematicians' work pointless, but this ignores the joy of the process and the ability to raise the bar.
- The subjectivity of evaluating motivated explanations is seen as a problem, but proofs themselves are also subjectively judged for importance, as shown by the lattice theory scandal.
- There's a concern that AI-assisted proofs are not understandable by humans, but this is already accepted for the four-color theorem, and we can still extract insights from AI proofs.
- The idea that AI will lead to a loss of conceptual understanding is refuted by the example of Maxwell's equations, which were simplified by Heaviside, showing that understanding can follow proof.
- The fear that AI will make mathematics less prestigious is countered by the fact that other fields without objective proofs, like philosophy, still get funding, though less prestige.
