## Thesis

Fears that AI will make mathematicians obsolete are overblown; AI makes mathematics more accessible and should be embraced as a tool for solving harder problems, not resisted.

## Argument

The doom and gloom about AI replacing mathematicians is overrated. Mathematicians will continue to have employment, though the nature of the work will change, and possibly more people will enter the field. The real crisis is internal: academia has long valued problem-solving over conceptual understanding, and AI threatens that status quo. But this is a reason to adapt, not panic.

AI is making frontier mathematics accessible to the masses, breaking down the ivory tower. People who couldn't read math papers can now use AI to explore and even contribute. This is a positive development, not a threat.

The 25 Fields medalists' letter against AI-assisted proofs is a reaction to threatened prestige and ego, not a sound argument. They claim conceptual understanding is paramount, but this is a recent 180 from the traditional focus on proofs. The real motivation is protecting their status.

Instead of resisting, mathematicians should use AI to solve harder problems and compete in new ways. The calculator didn't stop mathematical research; AI won't either. The bar should be raised, and those who embrace AI will be the ones remembered in history.

Even the subjective nature of evaluating explanations is not a reason to abandon them. Other fields like astronomy and philosophy thrive with subjectivity. The key is to adapt incentives and reward conceptual contributions, not just proofs.

AI is also bringing people together, as seen in the collaborative work on Medvedev's logic. It's a tool for collaboration and discovery, not a threat.

## Counterpoints

- Mathematicians fear AI will solve the problems they enjoy working on, taking away the joy of the process.
- The evaluation of motivated explanations is subjective, unlike proofs which are objective.
- AI results might act like a hard drug, providing diminishing returns and leading to misery.
- There's a risk that AI-assisted proofs will be accepted without human understanding, leading to a loss of insight.
- The shift to valuing explanations could demote mathematics to a 'soft science' with less prestige and funding.
