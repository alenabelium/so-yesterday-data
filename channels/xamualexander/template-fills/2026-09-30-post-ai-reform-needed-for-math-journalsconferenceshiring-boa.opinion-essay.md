## Thesis

Mathematics must immediately reform its incentive structures—journals, conferences, and hiring boards—to reward clarity and understanding over raw proof production, treating all results as AI-proven to prevent dishonesty.

## Argument

The mechanism is the prisoner's dilemma. As Amit Harlove notes, mathematicians are rewarded for being first to solve significant problems, so they must use AI to stay competitive. This has led to an exponential surge in submissions—combinatorics papers nearly doubled since 2025, with similar trends in algebraic geometry and metric geometry. The result is a publication crisis: we are drowning in unreadable articles that prove complex results but fail to advance human understanding. The host agrees that the value of a paper should shift from merely recording results to improving collective understanding through clear presentation and proof refinement. He applauds Harlove's controversial stance that all results should be treated as AI-proven, regardless of actual authorship, to eliminate the incentive to lie about AI use. The host emphasizes urgency: hiring committees must update listings now, and journals must revise review guidelines to reject poorly written work. He warns that waiting for consensus is futile—academia moves too slowly, and the crisis is already here. The solution is to reward canonization, as Terence Tao describes, where proofs are refined into natural, understandable forms. This is the most valuable and least automatable part of mathematics, offering a path for human mathematicians to remain relevant and paid.

## Counterpoints

- Some argue that AI-proven results have zero value, even if verified, but the host counters that the process of refining and presenting proofs adds value.
- Others worry that treating all results as AI-proven is too radical, but the host argues it prevents dishonesty and is the only fair approach.
- Hiring committees face practical limits—hundreds of applicants and too many papers to read—but the host insists they must find new evaluation methods quickly.
- Some believe the crisis will resolve in five years, but the host predicts math departments will be 'smoldering craters' by then.
