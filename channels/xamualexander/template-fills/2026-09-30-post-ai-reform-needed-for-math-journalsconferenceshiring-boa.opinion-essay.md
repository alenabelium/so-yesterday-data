## Thesis

Math journals, conferences, and hiring boards must immediately reform their incentive structures to reward clarity and understanding over raw result production, treating all results as AI-proven to prevent dishonesty.

## Argument

The mechanism is the exponential surge in paper submissions, which has nearly doubled in the hardest-hit fields since 2025, driven by AI models like GPT-5.2. This is a prisoner's dilemma: to stay competitive, mathematicians must use AI to solve problems as fast as possible, sacrificing writing quality and human understanding. The result is a flood of unreadable papers, a publication crisis that threatens to sink the discipline. The solution is to shift value from merely proving results to improving human understanding through clear presentation, proof refinement, and canonization—the slowest, least AI-optimizable stage. This means hiring committees and journals must update guidelines to reward these activities, and crucially, treat all results as AI-proven, regardless of actual provenance, to avoid incentivizing dishonesty. The urgency is now: waiting for consensus is a luxury we cannot afford, as the crisis is already here.

## Counterpoints

- Some argue AI-proven results have zero value, even if human-verified; the host counters that the process of verification and refinement adds value.
- Others worry that treating all results as AI-proven is too radical; the host applauds this as a necessary step to prevent lying about AI use.
- A commenter suggests that if anyone can solve a problem with AI, it's not an important contribution; the host agrees, emphasizing the need for human-added value.
- A hiring committee member notes practical limits: with hundreds of applicants, committees can't read all papers, relying on proxies; the host acknowledges this but insists on changing incentives.
- Some propose teaching descriptive writing to students; the host dismisses this as naive, predicting AI will be used instead.
