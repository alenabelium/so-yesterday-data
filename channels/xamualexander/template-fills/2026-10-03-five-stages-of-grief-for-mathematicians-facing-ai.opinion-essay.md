## Thesis

Mathematics is being killed by AI, and mathematicians who grieve it are wrong: the field's future belongs to those who embrace AI's theorem-proving power, not to those clinging to human understanding.

## Argument

The mechanism is the theorem tally. Kevin Buzzard, who was scooped by AI on formalizing Fermat's Last Theorem, argues that mathematics has always been about proving hard theorems, not about human understanding. The recent shift to 'understanding' as the field's purpose is a narrative move by mathematicians to protect their turf. But AI is already proving theorems humans can't, and it's only a matter of time before it explains them too. The real choice is between clinging to a dying paradigm or letting AI loose and following where it leads. Buzzard's own experience—he never read Deligne's proof, yet proved theorems relying on it—shows that 'understanding' is nebulous and often absent even in human practice. The future is about letting machines run, then reverse-engineering their results. It's a bet: those who bet on AI's explanatory power will thrive; those who bet on human understanding will sink into irrelevance.

## Counterpoints

- Peter Scholze, a Fields Medalist, will not use AI and will 'die on that hill,' but he risks becoming irrelevant.
- The Advisory Group on Mathematics and AI, including three Fields Medalists, asks labs to stop testing on proprietary models and to fund human understanding of AI outputs.
- Thurston's view that mathematics is about human understanding is cited, but Buzzard argues it's a recent reframing to survive AI.
- Some mathematicians argue AI proofs come without understanding, like the rank 30 and 31 elliptic curves, but Buzzard believes we can learn from them.
