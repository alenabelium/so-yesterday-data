## Thesis

Mathematicians grieving AI's rise are clinging to a dead version of mathematics, and the field's future belongs to those who embrace AI rather than resist it.

## Argument

Kevin Buzzard's five-stage grief model maps the community's reaction: denial (the Association for Human Mathematics, with Peter Scholze vowing to never use AI), anger (Vlad Vlasic's posts), bargaining (the Advisory Group asking labs to fund human understanding), and depression (students and postdocs questioning their purpose). These stages are understandable but ultimately self-defeating. The 'human understanding' justification is a recent, transparent goalpost shift—prizes and jobs have always gone to those proving hard theorems, not to those who merely understand deeply. Thurston's essay is now wielded cynically, but even that framing collapses when AI soon explains proofs as well as it proves them. Buzzard's own career—formalizing Fermat's Last Theorem in Lean, only to be scooped by AI—shows the pace. He rightly calls for OpenAI to release its 100+ results, arguing that hiding them is censorship. The real opportunity is that AI excels at problem-solving but not yet at theory-building or conjecture formulation, leaving a natural boundary where humans will still be needed. The path forward is to let machines loose, learn from their outputs, and then push beyond where they stop.

## Counterpoints

- Peter Scholze's stance: he will never use AI, even if it makes him irrelevant.
- The claim that mathematics is about human understanding, not just theorem tallying.
- The Advisory Group's request that labs stop testing on proprietary models and fund human understanding.
- The argument that AI proofs lack explanatory insight, as with the rank 30/31 elliptic curves.
- The belief that exponential progress will continue indefinitely, countered by the infinity of mathematics.
