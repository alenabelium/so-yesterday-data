## Thesis

Mathematicians grieving AI's theorem-proving are clinging to a reframed 'human understanding' narrative that is both historically false and strategically doomed.

## Argument

The grief stages are real: denial via the Association for Human Mathematics, anger from figures like Vlad Vlasic, bargaining from the Advisory Group on Mathematics and AI, and depression among students and postdocs. But the 'human understanding' defense is a recent invention. Before AI, top journals and departments rewarded hard theorems, not understanding—Thurston's essay is now cynically weaponized. Even the understanding defense collapses: Kevin Buzzard admits he never read Deligne's proof, yet claims to understand his own work. And AI will soon explain theorems as well as prove them. The real value of mathematics is proving theorems, developing tools, and seeing where to go next—AI is already doing the first, and humans will reverse-engineer the rest. The optimal move is to let machines loose, then follow.

## Counterpoints

- Peter Scholze will not use AI and will 'die on that hill'—but he risks irrelevance.
- The Advisory Group asks labs to stop testing on proprietary models and fund human understanding—but labs have fiduciary duties.
- Thurston's view that mathematics is about understanding is sincere—but it's a recent narrative shift, not the historical norm.
- AI proofs come with no explanation—but humans can reverse-engineer them, as with Navier-Stokes.
- Exponential progress can't continue indefinitely—but mathematics is infinite, so machines will hit a wall.
