## Thesis

The Riemann hypothesis, P vs. NP, and Navier-Stokes have lost much of their original practical significance, becoming largely academic challenges whose real-world impact is far less than initially imagined.

## Argument

Trefethen's article, published in the November 2026 Notices of the AMS, was already outdated by the time it appeared, as OpenAI had solved Navier-Stokes in September and made progress on 3-SAT. This lag highlights the archaic nature of the peer-reviewed journal system, which cannot keep pace with the rapid developments in mathematics since mid-2026. The core argument is that these three Millennium Problems, while theoretically fascinating, have seen their practical importance diminish over time. For the Riemann hypothesis, the verification of trillions of zeros on the critical line has reduced the quantitative impact of a potential disproof on prime distribution to an imperceptible level. For P vs. NP, the practical gap between polynomial and exponential algorithms has narrowed due to typical-case efficiency and approximation methods, as seen with the simplex method and 3-SAT solvers. For Navier-Stokes, research suggests that any singularities would require such artificial and unstable initial conditions that they are unlikely to occur in real fluids, making the problem more theoretical than physical. The host agrees with Trefethen's assessment, adding that the recent AI-driven solutions have not yielded the practical applications originally envisioned, but have instead sparked a renaissance in mathematics, attracting new talent and attention.

## Counterpoints

- The Riemann hypothesis remains a pillar of mathematics, and its proof or disproof would be an earthquake, even if the quantitative consequences are small.
- P vs. NP is still a central organizing principle in theoretical computer science, and a breakthrough algorithm would be a real sensation.
- The Navier-Stokes problem is important for understanding fluid dynamics, even if engineers have never been bothered by it.
- Some might argue that the problems have not lost significance but have simply evolved, with new theories and methods arising from attempts to solve them.
