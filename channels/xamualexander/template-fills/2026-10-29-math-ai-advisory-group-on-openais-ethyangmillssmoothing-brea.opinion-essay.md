## Thesis

OpenAI's practice of designating specific mathematicians to shepherd AI math breakthroughs is a harmful 'kingmaking' that denies community agency and should be replaced by open, organic, grassroots development.

## Argument

The advisory group's call for immediate release of the Yang-Mills output is a welcome turn from mathematicians' earlier panic, and their critique of designated experts is spot-on. The mechanism is access: when a chosen few see results early, others who've worked on a problem for years are crushed and dissuaded from contributing. Even with simultaneous release, the choice of who leads understanding denies the community agency over its own field. The practice also creates biased selection, reflecting the AI company's networks, and borrowed reputation, where designated names shield the company from criticism. The alternative is already proven: the grassroots community that grew around the recent multiplication breakthrough shows organic understanding works. The advisory group's recommendations for auditable releases, lean formalization, and process transparency are sound, though their call for institutional funding is unnecessary—grassroots efforts suffice. The timing of the advisory group's statement, a day before OpenAI's announcements, hints at internal tension, but the real solution is to let mathematics develop in the open, free from corporate gatekeeping.

## Counterpoints

- Designated experts can lead to better initial writeups and provide knowledgeable points of contact.
- The advisory group's recommendations for citations and institutional funding may be impractical or unnecessary.
- OpenAI may have legitimate reasons to delay release, such as completing lean formalization.
