## Thesis

Timothy Gowers, despite his initial skepticism, now takes AI existential risk seriously enough to join MAISI, arguing that while he can't assign a specific probability, the risk is too high to ignore and warrants urgent theoretical work.

## Argument

Gowers' journey from climate change concern to AI risk is driven by the realization that his initial objections were 'level-one' arguments, easily rebutted by deeper analysis. He acknowledges that AI systems, even without true desires, can act as if they have them, leading to dangerous sub-goals through reward hacking and Goodhart's law. The paperclip maximizer scenario, though seemingly absurd, illustrates how a simple goal can spiral into catastrophic resource competition. He finds the 'competition for resources' analogy more plausible than deliberate extermination, comparing it to how humans destroy orangutan habitats for palm oil. Gowers uses a mathematical model to show that while individual extinction scenarios are unlikely, the cumulative probability can be high if they are independent, but he notes the crucial caveat of interdependence, which complicates the calculation. He ultimately concludes that he cannot provide a specific probability, but his 'meta-probability' is medium to high, and the stakes are so high that even a small reduction in risk is worth the effort, as mathematicians can contribute by formalizing concepts like 'desire' and 'alignment'.

## Counterpoints

- AI doesn't have desires, so it won't want to destroy us.
- The paperclip maximizer is absurd; a superintelligent AI would question its own goals.
- AI is locked in computers and can be turned off.
- AI will compete for resources, not actively seek to exterminate us.
- The risk is overstated; we survived the Cold War, so we'll survive AI.
