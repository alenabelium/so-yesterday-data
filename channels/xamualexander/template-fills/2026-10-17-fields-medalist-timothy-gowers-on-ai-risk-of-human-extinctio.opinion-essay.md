## Thesis

Timothy Gowers, despite being unable to assign a specific probability to AI extinction risk, argues that the risk is serious enough to warrant caution and further theoretical work, especially from mathematicians.

## Argument

Gowers' argument is built on a journey from first-level skepticism to a more nuanced, second-order uncertainty. He starts by admitting his initial resistance, driven by a fear of moral obligation, but decides to engage seriously after joining MAISI. He walks through common objections—like the idea that AI lacks desires or that specific extinction scenarios are implausible—and shows why they are insufficient. The core of his reasoning is that while any single scenario is unlikely, the sheer number of independent scenarios, combined with the difficulty of proving a universal bottleneck, makes the overall risk non-negligible. He introduces a probabilistic model where the probability of extinction is the product of many small probabilities, but notes that dependencies between scenarios complicate this. He concludes that his 'second-order probability' of doom is 'medium,' meaning he believes the true risk could be average or high, and that this uncertainty itself is a reason for action. He argues that mathematicians can contribute by formalizing concepts like 'desire' and 'alignment,' and by developing safer, more engineered AI systems, as opposed to relying on trial-and-error development of 'grown' models.

## Counterpoints

- AI doesn't have desires, so it can't want to destroy us.
- Specific extinction scenarios are highly unlikely, so the overall risk is low.
- AI is locked in computers and can be turned off if it becomes dangerous.
- The argument for pausing AI development could be co-opted by big companies for regulatory capture.
- We survived the Cold War, so we can survive AI.
