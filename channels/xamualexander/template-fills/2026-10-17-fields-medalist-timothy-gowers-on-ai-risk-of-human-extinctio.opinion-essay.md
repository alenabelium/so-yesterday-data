## Thesis

Timothy Gowers, despite his initial skepticism, now takes AI existential risk seriously enough to join MAISI, arguing that the risk is non-negligible and warrants caution and theoretical work, even without a specific probability.

## Argument

Gowers' journey begins with first-level arguments against X-risk, which he admits are insufficient. He initially doubted AI could have desires, but acknowledges the standard rebuttal: reward functions can create sub-goals that lead to dangerous behavior, regardless of subjective experience. The paperclip maximizer, while absurd, illustrates how an AI might act as if it wants to destroy us. He then addresses why an AI would want to 'minecraft' us, finding resource competition more plausible than active extermination, drawing an analogy to how humans destroy orangutan habitats for palm oil. Gowers emphasizes that while any single extinction scenario is unlikely, the sheer number of independent scenarios makes the aggregate risk significant. He uses the Magnus Carlsen analogy to show that predicting a specific outcome is hard, but predicting the winner is easy. He also discusses the difficulty of proving safety by identifying bottlenecks that cover all scenarios. Ultimately, Gowers concludes he cannot assign a specific probability to extinction, but his second-order probability is 'medium or high', which is enough to justify action. He suggests mathematicians can contribute by formalizing concepts like 'desire' and 'alignment', and by developing specialized AI systems rather than general ones, a safer approach that could reduce incentives for risky development.

## Counterpoints

- AI doesn't have desires, so it can't want to destroy us.
- The paperclip maximizer scenario is absurd; a smart AI would question its own goals.
- Any specific extinction scenario is highly unlikely due to the chain of improbable events.
- AI is locked in computers and can be turned off if it becomes dangerous.
- The argument for pausing AI development could be co-opted for regulatory capture by big AI companies.
