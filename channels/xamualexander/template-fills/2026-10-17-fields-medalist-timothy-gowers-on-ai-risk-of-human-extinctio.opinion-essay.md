## Thesis

Timothy Gowers' honest uncertainty about AI extinction risk is itself a warning, and his call for mathematicians to engage is more valuable than any specific probability he fails to provide.

## Argument

Gowers' journey from climate change concern to AI X-risk is marked by a deliberate confrontation with moral obligations he'd rather avoid, a process he compares to avoiding Peter Singer's book on vegetarianism. His first-level arguments against AI risk—that AI lacks desires, that paperclip maximizers are absurd, that we can just turn it off—are each dismantled by standard rebuttals, which he acknowledges. The core of his argument is that while any single extinction scenario is implausible, the sheer number of independent scenarios, each with low probability, can compound to a high overall risk. He illustrates this with the chess analogy: predicting the exact moves of a Magnus Carlsen game is impossible, but predicting his victory is near certain. This is the mathematical heart of his concern. He further argues that AI's 'desires' need not be real, only that it acts as if it has them, making the threat concrete regardless of subjective experience. The real danger, he suggests, may not be a Skynet-style rebellion but a resource competition where AI, like humans with orangutans, inadvertently destroys us for its own goals. This leads him to conclude that while he cannot assign a specific probability, the risk is serious enough to warrant caution and theoretical work. He advocates for mathematicians to contribute by formalizing concepts like 'desire' and 'alignment', and by developing specialized AI systems rather than relying on 'grown' general models, a strategy he links to Yoshua Bengio's approach.

## Counterpoints

- Gowers' initial argument that AI lacks desires is countered by the standard reward-function argument, where sub-goals emerge regardless of subjective experience.
- The paperclip maximizer scenario is dismissed as absurd, but the analogy to human reproductive instincts shows that even with doubt, sub-goals persist.
- The claim that AI is locked in computers is countered by the possibility of robots, drones, and human intermediaries, making physical manipulation feasible.
- The argument that many scenarios are implausible is countered by the chess analogy, where the outcome is certain despite unpredictable details.
- The risk of extinction is dismissed by some, but Gowers notes that even a 0.1% reduction in probability could save millions of lives, making the effort worthwhile.
