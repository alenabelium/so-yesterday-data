## Thesis

Timothy Gowers, despite his initial skepticism, now takes AI existential risk seriously enough to join MAISI, arguing that the risk is real and warrants caution and theoretical work, even without a specific probability.

## Argument

Gowers' journey from climate change concern to AI X-risk is driven by the realization that many smart people disagree with him and that he hasn't thought about it enough. He acknowledges his initial arguments were 'level-one'—the kind that elicit eye rolls from experts. He then walks through the standard rebuttals: AI can act as if it has desires, reward hacking leads to unintended sub-goals, and the paperclip maximizer scenario, while absurd, highlights the danger of 'grown' models we don't control. He concedes that any single extinction scenario is unlikely, but the aggregate probability could be high if scenarios are independent. However, he notes the independence assumption is too strong; common bottlenecks like robot fleets cap the cumulative probability. He finds the chess analogy (predicting Carlsen wins without predicting the game) compelling for why we can't dismiss X-risk just because specific scenarios are implausible. He also addresses the 'just turn it off' objection, listing counterarguments like AI controlling robots, hacking, and our increasing dependence. Ultimately, he concludes he can't assign a specific probability but estimates a 'medium' second-order probability, and he sees no way to reduce it to negligible. He argues mathematicians can contribute by formalizing concepts like 'desire' and by developing specialized AI systems, an indirect approach to safety that might reduce the incentive for dangerous 'grown' systems.

## Counterpoints

- AI doesn't have desires, so it won't want to destroy us. Gowers counters that AI can act as if it has desires, which is just as dangerous.
- The paperclip maximizer is absurd; a superintelligent AI would question its own goals. Gowers notes that sub-goals developed during training might not disappear even if the AI doubts its primary goal.
- We can just turn off AI if it becomes dangerous. Gowers lists counterarguments: AI could control robots, hack systems, or become so integrated that we can't shut it down.
- The risk is overblown; we survived the Cold War. The host counters that the Cold War survival was lucky and doesn't guarantee AI safety.
- Pausing AI development would benefit big companies via regulatory capture. The host notes this as a potential downside of Gowers' call for caution.
