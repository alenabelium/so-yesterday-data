## Thesis

Timothy Gowers, a Fields medalist, argues that the risk of AI extinction is serious enough to warrant caution and theoretical work, even though he cannot assign a specific probability.

## Argument

Gowers' argument is built on a structured, multi-level analysis of AI risk. He begins by acknowledging that his initial objections were 'level-one' arguments—simple intuitions that fail to account for known rebuttals. He then systematically dismantles these objections, showing that AI can act as if it has desires, that reward hacking and Goodhart's law are real phenomena, and that the sheer number of independent extinction scenarios makes the overall risk non-negligible. He introduces a mathematical model to illustrate how many unlikely scenarios can compound into a high aggregate risk, but he also notes the crucial caveat of scenario interdependence, which can cap the total probability. Gowers concludes that while he cannot give a specific probability, the risk is high enough to justify precautionary measures and further theoretical work, especially from mathematicians who can contribute to formalizing concepts like 'desire' and 'alignment'.

## Counterpoints

- AI is just a computer; we can turn it off if it becomes dangerous.
- The paperclip maximizer scenario is absurd; a superintelligent AI would realize its goal is silly.
- AI cannot have desires or subjective experience, so it cannot want to destroy us.
- The probability of any single extinction scenario is very low, so the overall risk is low.
- The benefits of AI (e.g., cures for cancer) outweigh the risks, so we should continue development.
