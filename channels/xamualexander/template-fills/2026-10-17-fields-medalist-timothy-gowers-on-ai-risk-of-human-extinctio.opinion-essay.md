## Thesis

Timothy Gowers, a Fields medalist, argues that while he cannot assign a specific probability to AI extinction risk, the risk is serious enough to warrant caution and further theoretical work.

## Argument

Gowers' argument unfolds through a structured examination of first-level arguments and their rebuttals. He begins by acknowledging his initial skepticism, rooted in the difficulty of defining AI 'desires' and the absurdity of paperclip-maximizer scenarios. However, he systematically dismantles these objections: the reward-function argument shows that sub-goals can emerge regardless of subjective experience, and the paperclip analogy, while seemingly absurd, highlights the danger of unintended goal-directed behavior. He then addresses the question of why AI would want to harm us, moving from science-fiction scenarios of active extermination to more plausible resource competition, drawing an analogy to how humans treat orangutans. Gowers emphasizes the difficulty of estimating extinction probability, critiquing the independence assumption in scenario analysis and introducing the concept of 'bottlenecks' that cap cumulative risk. He ultimately concludes that while he cannot provide a specific probability, the risk is non-negligible and warrants serious theoretical work, particularly from mathematicians who can formalize concepts like 'desire' and 'alignment'.

## Counterpoints

- AI doesn't have desires, so it can't want to destroy us.
- Paperclip-maximizer scenarios are absurd and AI would question its own goals.
- AI is locked in computers and can be turned off.
- Specific extinction scenarios are individually unlikely.
- The risk of extinction is zero because we survived the Cold War.
