## Thesis

Timothy Gowers, despite his initial skepticism, now takes AI existential risk seriously enough to join MAISI, arguing that the risk is non-negligible and warrants caution and theoretical work, even without a specific probability.

## Argument

Gowers' journey from climate change concern to AI risk is driven by the realization that many smart people disagree with him and that he had thought less about X-risk. He structures his thinking through levels of argument, acknowledging that first-level objections have rebuttals. He dissects the 'desire' argument, noting that AI need not have subjective experience to act as if it wants to destroy us, and that reward functions can lead to unintended sub-goals. He addresses the 'why would AI want to destroy us' question, finding the resource competition analogy more plausible than active extermination. He then tackles probability, showing that while individual scenarios are unlikely, the aggregate risk depends on independence and bottlenecks, making the argument complex. He concludes that while he cannot assign a specific probability, the risk is serious enough to warrant caution and further theoretical work, especially from mathematicians who can formalize concepts like 'desire' and 'alignment'.

## Counterpoints

- AI doesn't have desires, so it can't want to destroy us.
- The paperclip maximizer scenario is absurd; AI would question its own goals.
- AI is locked in computers and can be turned off.
- Any specific extinction scenario is highly unlikely.
- The risk is overblown; we survived the Cold War.
