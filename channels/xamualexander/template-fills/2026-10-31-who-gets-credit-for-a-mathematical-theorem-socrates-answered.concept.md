## TL;DR

Socrates' theory of anamnesis, from Plato's Meno, argues that all learning is recollection. By guiding an uneducated servant to a geometric proof through questions alone, Socrates shows knowledge is innate. This ancient idea offers a radical answer to modern debates about who deserves credit for AI-discovered theorems.

## The argument

### Define Anamnesis: Learning as Recollection
- **Anchor Timestamps**: ['00:00:02']
- **Claim**: Socrates introduces anamnesis, the idea that we never truly learn but only recollect knowledge our souls possessed before birth. This challenges modern assumptions about learning and sets the stage for a radical rethinking of intellectual credit.
- **Role**: definition

### Demonstrate Through Socratic Geometry
- **Anchor Timestamps**: ['00:03:19']
- **Claim**: Socrates guides a servant boy to discover that the diagonal of a square yields a square of double area. He never asserts anything, only asks questions, showing the boy's 'correct opinions' were latent and drawn out by questioning.
- **Role**: evidence

### Expose the Boy's Initial Ignorance
- **Anchor Timestamps**: ['00:05:38']
- **Claim**: The boy first guesses that doubling the side doubles the area, a naive error. Through questioning, he realizes his mistake and enters a state of productive confusion, which Socrates argues is necessary for genuine inquiry.
- **Role**: counter

### Synthesize: No One Gets Credit
- **Anchor Timestamps**: ['00:20:01']
- **Claim**: Neither the boy nor Socrates deserves credit for the theorem. The boy lacked knowledge initially; Socrates only asked questions. The knowledge was within the boy all along, so credit belongs to no one—a conclusion applicable to AI discoveries.
- **Role**: synthesis

## Evidence and caveats

The video walks through the Meno's geometry: starting with a 2x2 square (area 4), Socrates leads the boy to see that a square on the diagonal has area 8, illustrating the square root of 2. The host notes the dialogue's imprecise units ('feet' for square feet) and that we lack Plato's original diagrams, making the proof hard to follow in words alone. He also acknowledges anamnesis is 'completely contrary to modern philosophical ideas' but insists Socrates should be taken seriously. The argument extends to any proof, from simple to the classification of finite simple groups, suggesting all mathematical truths are latent in us, awaiting the right questions.
