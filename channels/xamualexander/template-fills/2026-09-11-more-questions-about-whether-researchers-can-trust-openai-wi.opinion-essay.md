## Thesis

OpenAI's handling of the Navier-Stokes controversy has irreparably damaged trust in its use of unpublished mathematical research, and this distrust will persist regardless of the company's denials.

## Argument

The core issue is not whether OpenAI actually used Buckmaster's unpublished work, but that the company's categorical denials are unverifiable and its behavior has been perceived as bullying. As one commenter notes, OpenAI's claim that it did not train on specific user data is 'a technical argument that is impossible to verify as an OpenAI outsider.' This lack of transparency, coupled with the demand to remove an Anthropic employee from the author list, has created a lasting impression of impropriety. The damage extends beyond mathematics: if OpenAI can be suspected of using private math conversations, it could also be using private therapy sessions or other sensitive data. Even if OpenAI's internal investigation is thorough, the 'genie is already out of the bottle'—conspiracy theorists will parse every word for ambiguity. This controversy forces mathematicians into a dilemma: use AI tools and risk having their work absorbed, or refuse and fall behind. The latter group will 'vanish into obscurity' because AI is becoming too powerful to ignore. The only potential solution is a competing AI company offering a no-training guarantee, but that merely shifts the trust problem from one company to another.

## Counterpoints

- OpenAI's categorical denial that specific user data was accessed, and the claim that it's impossible for Buckmaster's prompts to have influenced the system, are presented as strong evidence.
- The argument that OpenAI's models are capable enough to solve these problems without needing to steal work, as evidenced by progress on other Millennium problems.
- The suggestion that the controversy is partly driven by mathematicians' fear of job obsolescence, leading them to grasp at any straw to discredit AI.
- The observation that the model's solution was quite different from the mathematicians' approach, suggesting independent discovery.
