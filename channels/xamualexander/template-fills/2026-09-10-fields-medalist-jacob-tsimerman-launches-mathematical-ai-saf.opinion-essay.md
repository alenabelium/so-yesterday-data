## Thesis

Mathematicians, not subjective expert opinions, are the right people to formalize AI safety, but MAISI's funding and Bay Area logistics remain glaring unanswered questions.

## Argument

The pitch is that AI safety is drowning in subjective risk assessments—people say whatever moves stock prices—while we lack a rigorous definition of what 'safe' even means. Mathematicians are uniquely equipped to build that foundation, just as they did for physics and statistics. The institute's structure, modeled on the IAS, is designed to get mathematicians up to speed fast, with living documents shared before journal publication—a welcome break from the farcical 2-3 year review timelines that are useless in a field that changes every 2-3 weeks. The team, including Timothy Gowers, adds real credibility. But the elephant in the room is funding: the website is silent on who's backing this, and hiring 100 mathematicians in the Bay Area is 'somewhere between being and wow.' Big tech has obvious incentives to fund and influence this, which could undermine the whole enterprise. The core problems may also be provably intractable—Gödel's incompleteness theorem shows some things can't be proven from within a system—so the answers might be negative. Still, that's exactly the kind of rigorous analysis we need.

## Counterpoints

- Subjective risk assessments from experts and public figures abound, but they're just expressions of individual fears, not principled measures.
- Mathematical insights will be no panacea; regulatory frameworks, infrastructure hardening, and training techniques are still needed.
- The core problems of AI x-risk may turn out to be provably difficult or intractable, leading to negative answers.
- Who is funding this? The website has no information, and big tech companies have incentives to influence it.
