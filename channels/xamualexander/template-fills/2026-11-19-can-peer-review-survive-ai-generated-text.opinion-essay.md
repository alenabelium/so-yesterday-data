## Thesis

Peer review cannot survive AI-generated text; the system is already overloaded and will be replaced by AI-driven evaluation.

## Argument

The proposed fixes—AI detectors, stricter review protocols, and penalties—are attempts to patch an already broken system. The authors' own admission that 'expert judgment has been in decline for a long time' [00:00:01] undercuts their premise. They add more load to an overloaded system [00:01:34], and their 'first error' rejection policy is ineffective because high-quality AI models will eliminate technical errors [00:01:44]. The real value of expert assessment lies in judging relevance and innovation, not technical correctness [00:20:31]. AI will soon handle technical checks, making the proposed policies obsolete. The historical parallel to the Gutenberg press [00:08:55] shows that disruptive technologies don't end the world; they change how we work. We should adapt to a post-AI world rather than preserve a failing system. The article's own examples, like the Jacobian hypothesis counterexample [00:10:17], demonstrate that AI can completely replace human labor in some cases. The system is already failing: reviewers are unpaid, overworked, and will inevitably use AI [00:05:51]. The future is not about saving peer review but about moving to the next chapter in science [00:02:58].

## Counterpoints

- The authors argue that AI detectors are reliable and false positives are rare, but the host counters that this assumption is time-bound and will not hold as AI improves [00:12:32].
- The authors propose penalties for AI-generated reviews, but the host notes that reviewers are unpaid volunteers, making punishment impractical [00:22:24].
- The authors suggest that AI should complement rather than replace human effort, but the host points to the Jacobian hypothesis counterexample as a case where replacement is logical [00:10:17].
