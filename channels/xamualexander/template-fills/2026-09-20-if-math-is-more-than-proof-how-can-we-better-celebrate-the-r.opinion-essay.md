## Thesis

Mathematics must reform how it assigns credit to celebrate motivated explanations alongside proofs, but without objective standards for evaluating exposition, the field's meritocracy will collapse into tribal politics.

## Argument

The mechanism is the collapse of proof as a reliable proxy for merit. For centuries, proofs served as the metric for hiring and tenure because they were objective—if someone didn't care about math, they couldn't produce proofs. But AI now lets anyone generate proofs without understanding, undermining that proxy. Sanderson's proposal to credit 'motivated explanations' is appealing, but it's fundamentally subjective. There's no objective truth to stop a reviewer from rejecting a motivated explanation because the author isn't in their tribe. This isn't a hypothetical: the entire system of mathematical credit is built on objective verification, and removing that floor sends us into freefall. The problem is compounded by the fact that AI may soon excel at exposition too, making the proposed solution temporary at best. The real challenge is to find a way to maintain meritocracy—what you know, not who you know—while expanding what counts as valuable mathematical work. Without that, mathematics risks becoming just another humanities program where success depends on impressing the right professor.

## Counterpoints

- AI may soon be better at exposition than humans, undermining the proposed solution.
- The subjectivity of evaluating motivated explanations could lead to corruption and tribal politics.
- Rewarding textbooks and exposition could be gamed by AI-generated content.
- The proposal might not solve the underlying problem of assigning credit in a meritocratic way.
