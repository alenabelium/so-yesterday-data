## Thesis

Mathematics must reform how it assigns credit, but any shift toward celebrating motivated explanations will fail unless it solves the subjectivity problem that threatens to replace meritocracy with tribal politics.

## Argument

The mechanism is the collapse of proof as a proxy for both understanding and merit. For centuries, proofs served as the objective metric for hiring and tenure, but AI now lets anyone generate proofs without understanding, undermining that proxy. Grant Sanderson's proposal to credit 'motivated explanations' is appealing, but it introduces a fatal subjectivity: there is no objective truth to stop a reviewer from dismissing work because the author isn't in their tribe. This is the central problem we must solve before any reform can work. The stakes are existential. If we can't maintain an objective meritocracy, mathematics will become like the humanities, where success depends on sucking up to professors rather than on what you know. The field is already facing an existential crisis as outsiders see proof-generating machines as making mathematicians obsolete, and young students are afraid to enter. We need a cultural shift, but it must be built on objective standards, not just good intentions. The danger is that AI will eventually excel at exposition too, undermining the entire solution. We need to find a way to keep mathematics a true meritocracy, where it's what you know, not who you know, that matters.

## Counterpoints

- Subjectivity is intrinsic to human understanding, so we must accept it.
- AI will eventually be better at exposition than humans, making the proposal moot.
- The proposal is self-serving for Grant Sanderson, who has a non-traditional career.
- Tenure boards could use subjectivity to discriminate based on personal bias.
- Textbooks may become obsolete as learning moves to chatbots.
