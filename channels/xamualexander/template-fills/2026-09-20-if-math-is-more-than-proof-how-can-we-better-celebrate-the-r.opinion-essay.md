## Thesis

Mathematics must reform how it assigns credit to celebrate motivated explanations alongside proofs, but without objective standards, this reform risks collapsing meritocracy into tribal politics.

## Argument

The mechanism is existential: AI can now generate proofs without advancing human understanding, undermining proofs as a proxy for the true goal of mathematics and for meritocracy. Grant Sanderson's proposal to credit 'motivated explanations' is right, but it is dangerously subjective. As Alexander notes, 'there's no objective truth to stop the reviewer from saying that'—tribes form, and reviewers dismiss work from outsiders. Proofs worked for centuries because they were objective; exposition is 'free fall.'

Alexander agrees with the diagnosis: the field already treats exposition as 'fifth class or 10th class,' and the Princeton Companion to Mathematics, edited by Timothy Gowers, is a prime example of valuable work that required a Fields Medal to justify. But he challenges Sanderson's reading of Gowers—Gowers said he wouldn't have been *offered* the chance, not that he lacked freedom. The deeper issue is that credit is a social construct, and Thurston's 'noncredit producing activities' reveal who controls the credit: 'Who's dealing out the credit? And who made them experts?'

Alexander also warns that AI may soon excel at exposition too, undermining the proposed solution. He cites a commenter: 'Exposition may also become a solved problem.' If so, the reform is moot. Yet he agrees with the cultural urgency: young mathematicians are scared, and the field must act deliberately to attract them. ## Counterpoints

- Subjectivity: motivated explanations are inherently subjective, opening the door to tribal politics and corruption in hiring and tenure.
- AI may soon be better at exposition than humans, making the proposed reform obsolete.
- Timothy Gowers' quote is misread: he said he wouldn't have been offered the chance to edit the Princeton Companion, not that he lacked freedom.
- Thurston's essay ignores his own privilege—he received credit for results without complete proofs, a form of 'proof indigestion.'
- AI-generated proofs can be clear and good, so not every AI proof is an unsolved exposition problem.
