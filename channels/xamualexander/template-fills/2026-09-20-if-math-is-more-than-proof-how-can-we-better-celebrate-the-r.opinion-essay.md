## Thesis

Mathematics needs to reform how it assigns credit, but any shift toward subjective evaluation risks destroying the field's meritocracy unless objective standards are found.

## Argument

The mechanism is the breakdown of proofs as a proxy for merit. Historically, proofs served as an objective metric for hiring and tenure, keeping mathematics a true meritocracy. But now, AI can generate proofs without understanding, undermining this proxy. Grant Sanderson proposes celebrating 'motivated explanations' instead, which is a good idea in principle. However, the problem is that evaluating exposition is inherently subjective. Without objective standards, this reform could collapse into tribal politics, where reviewers favor their own tribe and discriminate against others. This is the central challenge: how to maintain meritocracy when the metric becomes squishy. The field needs to find a way to make motivated explanations 'nearly as verifiable as proof,' as Sanderson suggests, but this is easier said than done. The stakes are high: if mathematics can't solve this, it risks becoming like the humanities, where success depends on sucking up to professors rather than on objective merit.

## Counterpoints

- Subjectivity: evaluating motivated explanations is subjective and could lead to tribal politics and corruption.
- AI could also excel at exposition, undermining the proposed solution.
- The proposal might be self-serving, given Sanderson's non-traditional career in math communication.
- The difficulty of defining what constitutes a motivated explanation, as it's not binary like a proof.
