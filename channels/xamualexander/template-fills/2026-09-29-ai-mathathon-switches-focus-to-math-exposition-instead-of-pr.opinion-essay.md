## Thesis

The AI Mathathon's redesign toward exposition is a positive step, but it may be a trap if AI turns out to be better at explaining math than humans.

## Argument

The redesign is a genuine improvement. The original Mathathon, announced by Caltech undergrads, was met with an open letter from established mathematicians demanding its cancellation. Now, after a conversation between organizers and critics, the event has been reshaped around the theme 'old problems, new proofs.' This is thrilling because it shifts focus from solving new problems to finding alternative proofs and expositions of known results, like the four-color theorem or the ABC conjecture. The new format encourages participants to explore generalizations, new notations, and connections, which have historically been overlooked by grant makers and hiring committees. The event also requires full disclosure of AI usage and encourages open-source tools, a welcome change from the original sponsorship by OpenAI and Anthropic, which have been replaced by Terence Tao's Foundation for Science and AI Research and XTX Markets. This aligns with a broader community shift, following 3Blue1Brown's suggestion, toward valuing human conceptual understanding. However, there is a trap. Mathematicians, after being rudely awakened by AI's ability to prove theorems, are now retreating to the narrative that understanding and exposition are what matter. But if this Mathathon reveals that AI is also better at exposition, then mathematicians will have nowhere left to run. They are running out of castles to defend.

## Counterpoints

- The original Mathathon was attracting people more interested in resume polishing for industry jobs than pure math research.
- The change in theme won't alter the agendas of XTX Markets and SAIR.
- The chess analogy suggests that AI will help weaker mathematicians as much as the elites, dethroning the top names.
- Mathematics is not competitive in principle, unlike chess, so the analogy may not hold.
- The focus on exposition is a retreat from the real value of proving new results, as the moon landing analogy illustrates.
