## Thesis

The AI Mathathon's redesign toward mathematical exposition is a positive shift, but it exposes a trap: if AI proves better at exposition too, mathematicians will have no refuge left.

## Argument

The redesign is a genuine improvement. The original Mathathon, announced by Caltech undergrads, drew a massive open letter from established mathematicians demanding cancellation. Now, after a consensus-building conversation, the event has pivoted from proving new results to 'old problems, new proofs' — celebrating human understanding and exposition. This is a welcome change because it breathes new life into mathematics, rewarding the often-ignored work of generalization, notation, and connection-making. The shift also aligns with 3Blue1Brown's suggestion to value conceptual understanding, and it's telling that the community is finally following that lead. The funding change is equally significant: OpenAI withdrew after the Navier-Stokes controversy, and the organizers turned down Anthropic's remaining money. Now, Terence Tao's Foundation for Science and AI Research and XTX Markets are the lead donors, with a focus on open-source tools and full disclosure of AI usage. This is a more principled, community-driven approach. But here's the trap: mathematicians, rudely awakened by AI's theorem-proving prowess, are now retreating to the narrative that 'understanding' and 'exposition' are the true essence of math. Yet, this contradicts their historical behavior, which has always rewarded results, not explanations. If this Mathathon reveals that AI is also better at exposition — and the commenters' ideas about personalized proofs suggest that's plausible — then there's nowhere left to run. The castles are running out.

## Counterpoints

- The original Mathathon was attracting people more interested in resume polishing for industry jobs than pure math research.
- The funding shift to XTX Markets and SAIR won't change their underlying agendas.
- The chess-mathematics analogy is flawed because chess is finite and competitive, while mathematics is infinite and, in principle, not competitive.
- The 'moon landing' analogy for exposition is wrong; actually landing on the moon had a far greater impact than a failed journey with good feelings.
