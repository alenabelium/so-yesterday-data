## Thesis

The singularity has already started, unevenly distributed, and human mathematicians are dethroned as the main theorem-proving entities.

## Argument

The alarm clock is ringing: AI has solved the Navier-Stokes Millennium Problem, a feat that 20 years ago was the agreed-upon signal to panic. The goal posts were set in 2006, and by that standard, we've already scored. The singularity is here, just unevenly distributed—I still unload the dishwasher, but I'll never again prove a theorem because I'm needed to. The mechanism is the deluge: in the last month alone, AI has solved the Jacobian conjecture, improved Grothendieck's constant, verified Fermat's Last Theorem, and separated QMA from QMA2. Every paper I'd be interested in now includes an AI statement. The community is grimly fortifying its walls, but the high-order bit is clear: human mathematicians are forever more dethroned as the main theorem-proving entities. The question is no longer whether AI can do math, but whether we can preserve a human community that understands and cares about the results. That's why I endorsed the Fields Medalists' open letter—not because I'm naive, but because I want to keep insight and understanding at the center for as long as we can. The alternative is to close up shop and turn everything over to the machines, but I'm not ready to throw in the towel yet.

## Counterpoints

- Moving goal posts: 'Wake me up when AI does thing AI hasn't done yet but is going to do next year.'
- The Fields Medalists' open letter has no clear ask, just values.
- OpenAI acted unsportsmanlike in swooping in with vastly greater resources.
- AI can't generate interesting new math because 'interesting' is subjective and humans won't read AI slop.
- Eliezer Yudkowsky wasn't expecting this to happen anytime soon either.
