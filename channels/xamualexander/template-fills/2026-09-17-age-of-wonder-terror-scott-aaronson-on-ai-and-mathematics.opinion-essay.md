## Thesis

The singularity has already started, and human mathematicians are forever dethroned as the main theorem-proving entities on Earth.

## Argument

The mechanism is the sheer volume and speed of AI-discovered results. Navier-Stokes, a Millennium Problem, was solved with crucial AI contributions, and OpenAI burned $15 million in compute to produce a 166-page proof that no human has fully read. This isn't an isolated event; in the last month alone, AI has also produced a counterexample to the Jacobian conjecture, improved bounds for Grothendieck's constant, an AI-verified proof of Fermat's Last Theorem, and solutions to long-standing open problems in quantum complexity theory. The pattern is unmistakable: the goalposts set 20 years ago—AI solving major math problems—have been passed. The response from the mathematical community, like the Fields Medalists' open letter, is not about stopping AI but about preserving human understanding and community. The real question is whether we can keep insight and understanding at the center of mathematics, or whether we'll eventually close up shop and turn everything over to the machines. The alarm clock is ringing, and it's time to update with intellectual honesty.

## Counterpoints

- Some argue AI results are just 'AI slop' and that humans are still needed to direct AI toward interesting problems, but this misses that the AI is already solving problems humans care about.
- Critics say the open letter from Fields Medalists lacks a clear ask, but it's really about articulating the values worth preserving, not about stopping AI.
- Skeptics claim AI doom is an apocalyptic religion, but accepting the reality of AI solving major problems is now the epistemic bare minimum, not a matter of faith.
- Some say mathematicians will still be needed as verifiers and explicators, but this reduces their role and doesn't address the incentive problem for joining the community.
