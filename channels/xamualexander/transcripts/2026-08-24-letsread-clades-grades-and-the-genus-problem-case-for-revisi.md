**[00:53:49]** Limitations, disadvantages and additional considerations. The main limitation of the proposed expansion of the genus Homo is the loss of taxonomic information. Taxonomy serves not only to name biological groups, but also to convey information about their evolutionary history, morphology, and ecology. By grouping Australopithecus, Paranthropus, and Homo within the same genus, some of this information is inevitably hidden. In particular, most researchers would probably agree that, despite their shortcomings, the genera Homo and Paranthropus currently convey meaningful information about two sympatric lineages that followed markedly different evolutionary paths and exhibited significant anatomical and behavioral differences. As a result, many will perceive the taxonomy proposed here as a way of "throwing the baby out with the bathwater ." This criticism carries significant weight and will make it unlikely that this proposal will gain popularity. Although the proposals by Wood, Collard, and Tattersall introduce more genera or increase the size of major genera, they have the advantage of clearly recognizing what were clearly significant transitions in human evolution, including the evolution of large-brained and fully terrestrial Homo.

**[00:54:51]** One possible response to this criticism is that additional taxonomic ranks, such as subgenera or species groups, could be used to preserve information within the broader concept of Homo. For example, a subgenus could be applied to recent large-brained members of Homo, which are regularly distinguished as a clade and share clear behavioral and anatomical features, while taxa currently included in Paranthropus could be similarly included as a subgenus. A similar structure could be implemented using species groups, as is customary in modern primate taxonomy, which would allow for the preservation of significant biological and evolutionary differences. However, such approaches remain less satisfactory than the use of individual genera, which more clearly reflect these significant evolutionary, morphological, and ecological differences. **[00:57:43]** Attempts to ensure monophyly by splitting taxa would lead to an increase in the number of genera and likely to frequent taxonomic reassignments, rather than creating a stable and consistent classification. However, this approach is difficult to reconcile with genetic data from other primates and the instability observed in different phylogenetic analyses. **[00:58:37]** The problem lies not in its creation, which might be appropriate according to the principles of clade classification, but in its coexistence with the subsequent uncritical use of the gradational genus Australopithecus. Therefore, increasing the number of genera does not necessarily improve phylogenetic resolution or add more information within the current hominid and taxonomic system. The only clear alternatives are either to split Australopithecus into even more smaller genera, or, as proposed here, to recognize a single major hominin radiation over the past 4.5 million years.
