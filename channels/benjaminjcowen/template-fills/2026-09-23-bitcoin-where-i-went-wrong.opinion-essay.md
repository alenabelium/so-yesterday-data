## Thesis

Bitcoin's post-rate-hike rally proves that assuming rate hikes are immediately bearish for Bitcoin was wrong, and the market structure now demands deference to the higher high.

## Argument

The mechanism is historical data. I built a macro chain: energy up → yields up → Fed hikes → dollar up → Bitcoin down. Every link played out except the last. But when I checked the data, I found that in every prior rate-hike cycle, Bitcoin did not drop immediately after the first hike. In December 2015, the bottom was already in; in March 2022, Bitcoin actually went up initially. September hikes in midterm years (2018, 2022) also didn't cause immediate drops. So my assumption that a rate hike would force a repricing was not rooted in the data. That's where I went wrong.

Now I have to respect the market structure. Bitcoin set a higher high after the summer low, unlike the lower highs of 2018 and 2022. The burden of proof has shifted to the bears. I can't be deterministic about a lower low. If weakness comes in Q4, it could resolve as a macro higher low, especially if it follows a stock market correction. Historically, final weakness in midterm years comes after the midterms, not before. So I'm open-minded to a higher low, contingent on a stock correction. If the S&P only drops 10%, that's probably not enough to take Bitcoin to a new low.

## Counterpoints

- Some might say the rate hike is just a continuation of the previous cycle, not a new first hike, but the technical definition counts it as a new cycle since rates didn't return to zero.
- One might argue that Bitcoin's rally is just a temporary divergence and it will eventually drop, but the data shows that in past cycles, Bitcoin either rose or had already bottomed after the first hike.
- A skeptic might point to the FTX collapse in 2022 as a reason Bitcoin dropped after a rate hike, but that was an exogenous event, not a direct reaction to the hike.
