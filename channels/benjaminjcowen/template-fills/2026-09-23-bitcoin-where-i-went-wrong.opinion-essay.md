## Thesis

Rate hikes are not immediately bearish for Bitcoin, and my assumption that they were led me to wrongly predict a drop after the Fed's September 2026 hike.

## Argument

The mechanism is historical precedent. I built a macro chain—energy up, yields up, Fed forced to hike, dollar up—and concluded Bitcoin would reprice lower. But when I actually checked the data, I found that in every prior rate-hiking cycle since Bitcoin's existence, the first hike did not cause an immediate drop. In December 2015, the bottom was already in; in March 2022, Bitcoin initially went up. Even in September of midterm years (2018, 2022), Bitcoin did fine for a month or two after the hike. My error was assuming a rate hike would be immediately bearish without rooting that assumption in the data.

So now I defer to market structure. Bitcoin has printed a higher high, something it didn't do in the summer rallies of 2018 or 2022. The burden of proof has shifted to the bears. I'm open-minded to a higher low in Q4, but only if we get a stock market correction—and even then, a 10% S&P drop probably wouldn't be enough to take Bitcoin to a new low. The final weakness in midterm years has historically come after the midterms, not before. Until then, I defer to those who called this move correctly.

## Counterpoints

- One might argue that the current hike isn't the first in a cycle because rates didn't return to zero, but by technical definition it is, and history shows rates can rise again without going to zero.
- Some might say a rate hike should be bearish because it strengthens the dollar, but the dollar went up and Bitcoin went with it, showing a divergence from past patterns.
- A skeptic could point to the FTX collapse in 2022 as a reason Bitcoin dropped after a hike, but that was an exogenous event, not a direct response to the rate hike.
