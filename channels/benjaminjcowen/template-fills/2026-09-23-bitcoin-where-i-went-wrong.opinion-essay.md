## Thesis

My assumption that a Fed rate hike would immediately be bearish for Bitcoin was not rooted in historical data, and I was wrong to be so deterministic about it.

## Argument

The mechanism of my error was a macro chain that played out perfectly: energy prices rose, yields rose, the Fed hiked in September, and the dollar strengthened. But Bitcoin rallied anyway, setting a higher high. I had assumed a rate hike would force a repricing lower, yet the historical record shows the opposite. In the December 2015 and March 2022 rate-hiking cycles, Bitcoin either had already bottomed or initially rallied after the first hike. In September 2018 and September 2022, Bitcoin also did not immediately drop after the hike. The data suggests that if weakness follows a hike, it typically comes a month or two later, not immediately. I also ignored the market structure: Bitcoin formed its February low, then a summer low, and then rallied 50% off that low—a bigger move than in prior midterm years. The golden cross and the higher high were signals I should have respected. Now, the burden of proof has shifted to the bears. I defer to those who called this move correctly. If weakness appears in Q4, I will not assume it must be a lower low; it could be a macro higher low, especially if it coincides with a stock market correction. My mistake was being too deterministic, and I should have deferred to market structure rather than my macro narrative.

## Counterpoints

- One might argue that a rate hike should be bearish because it strengthens the dollar, but the data shows Bitcoin has not consistently dropped immediately after hikes.
- Some might say that this rate hike is not the first in a cycle, but the technical definition counts it as such, and historical September hikes in midterm years also did not cause immediate drops.
- A critic could point to the FTX collapse in 2022 as evidence that Bitcoin can drop after a hike, but that was a specific event, not a general pattern, and the drop did not reach a new low.
- One might argue that the stock market correction is inevitable, but 2010 shows that midterm years do not always have a late-September top, so a correction is not guaranteed.
