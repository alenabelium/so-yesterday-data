## Thesis

My assumption that a Fed rate hike would immediately be bearish for Bitcoin was not rooted in historical data, and I was wrong to be so deterministic about it.

## Argument

The mechanism of my error was a macro chain: I expected energy prices to rise, pushing yields up, forcing the Fed to hike in September, which would strengthen the dollar and send Bitcoin lower. Energy, yields, and the dollar all moved as I predicted, but Bitcoin rallied instead. The flaw was the final link: I assumed a rate hike is immediately bearish for Bitcoin, but the data shows otherwise. In the December 2015 and March 2022 rate-hiking cycles, Bitcoin did not drop immediately after the first hike; in one case the bottom was already in, and in the other it initially rose. Similarly, after the September 2018 and September 2022 hikes, Bitcoin did not immediately fall. My mistake was being deterministic when the evidence pointed to a range of outcomes. Now I defer to market structure: Bitcoin has set a higher high, and the burden of proof has shifted to the bears. I will not assume the next drop must be a lower low; it could be a macro higher low, especially if it aligns with a stock market correction. I also recognize that my past errors, like in 2023 when I said Bitcoin would not exceed $35,000, taught me to respect the market's message and listen to those who called the move correctly.

## Counterpoints

- One might argue that Bitcoin's rally is an anomaly, but historical data shows Bitcoin often does fine for a month or two after a rate hike in midterm years.
- Some might say the rate hike is not the first in a cycle, but the technical definition counts it as such, and Bitcoin has still risen.
- A skeptic could point to the FTX collapse in 2022 as a reason for caution, but that event was a unique shock, not a typical rate-hike response.
- Others might insist that a stock market correction is inevitable, but 2010 shows that midterm years do not always produce a late-September top.
