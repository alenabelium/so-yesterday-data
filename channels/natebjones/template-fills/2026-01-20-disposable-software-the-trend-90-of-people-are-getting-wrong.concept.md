## TL;DR

Disposable software is real—the cost of generating code has collapsed—but the discourse conflates two distinct phenomena: throwaway personal apps (positive democratization) and disposable features in enterprise products (a strategy that only works for developer audiences). The true constraint is attention, not software cost, and most enterprise customers buy reliability, not constant change.

## The argument

### Define Disposable Software as Cost Inversion
- **Anchor Timestamps**: ['00:02:05']
- **Claim**: Disposable software is not a philosophy but an economic description: when the cost of producing something collapses toward zero, it becomes disposable. The engineering bottleneck and capital requirement disappear, making software as cheap to produce as digital photos.
- **Role**: definition

### Two Categories: Throwaway vs. Enterprise Features
- **Anchor Timestamps**: ['00:04:27']
- **Claim**: Disposable software splits into two phenomena: throwaway personal apps (unambiguously positive democratization) and disposable features within enterprise products (the Cursor philosophy). The latter works for developers but fails for the 95% of the market that buys reliability.
- **Role**: definition

### Enterprise Customers Buy Reliability, Not Features
- **Anchor Timestamps**: ['00:09:00']
- **Claim**: Enterprise customers buy peace of mind—they want software they can ignore. Disposable software's constant change is the opposite of what they want. This is why enterprise contracts have SLAs and support lines; they are buying a 'ringable net,' not a moving target.
- **Role**: evidence

### Attention, Not Software Cost, Is the Real Constraint
- **Anchor Timestamps**: ['00:10:13']
- **Claim**: The argument that 'we'll vibe code our own Salesforce' ignores opportunity cost. Software is cheap to generate, but attention is not. Diverting highly paid builders from core business opportunities to maintain internal tools is a losing trade, and maintenance burden persists.
- **Role**: counter

### Winning Strategy: Reliability First, Then Proactive AI
- **Anchor Timestamps**: ['00:15:50']
- **Claim**: For enterprise software, the winning AI strategy is not to imitate Cursor's shipping velocity but to earn trust through reliability first, then extend into proactive AI agents that create value customers didn't know they were missing. Proactivity without trust is a liability.
- **Role**: synthesis

## Evidence and caveats

**Evidence:**
- Cursor's CEO used AI agents to build a web browser in one week (3M lines of Rust), vs. Chrome's 2-year development with elite engineers.
- 75% of Replit's customers never write code.
- AI-generated code introduces security vulnerabilities in nearly half of coding tasks.

**Caveats:**
- Disposable software works for developer audiences with high change tolerance, but even they complain about instability (e.g., Cursor forums).
- The speaker notes that proactive AI requires trust, and established players like Salesforce may be 'leaning over their skis' by pushing AI agents too fast.
- The speaker emphasizes that the strategy depends on context: 'Know which game you're playing.'
