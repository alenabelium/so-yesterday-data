## Thesis

The agent war is won not by form factor—computers, memory, background work—but by the quality of work each agent delivers, which is why OpenAI's Dots is worth $100 a month only if it produces outcomes you value.

## Argument

The mechanism is utility, not form. Every agent now ships with a computer, memory, and background work, but the underlying model and task-specific competencies differ. Muse excels at practical, low-intelligence chores like calls and purchases; Instinct targets travel and restaurant transactions; Dots is built for serious work, leveraging the context you've accumulated in ChatGPT. This is why OpenAI pairs Dots with its most powerful model, Astra, and why the real differentiator is the quality of work. The strategic bet is that more of your work stays in ChatGPT, feeding a flywheel of context that makes the agent smarter. Spaces and Pages extend this into a collaborative workspace, directly challenging Microsoft Office. The efficiency of the new GPT-6.1 Soul model is the unsung hero, delivering near-Astra performance at a fifth of the cost, letting you do more without exhausting plan limits. For $100, you're not just buying an agent; you're investing in a future where your AI context works for you, and the return is measured in time saved and outcomes achieved, not just features.

## Counterpoints

- Why pay $100 when Meta's Muse is free? Because form factor is copyable, but the quality of work—driven by model and context—is not.
- Dots doesn't automatically know everything from ChatGPT; it only accesses memories you choose to share, which is a privacy feature but also a limitation.
- Spaces and Pages assume an AI-native, flat organizational structure that's rare outside Silicon Valley, making adoption uncertain.
- Microsoft has a natural advantage with Copilot because of trust and integration, even if OpenAI's tech is more advanced.
- The $200 plan is getting a lower limit for the same price, which is unpopular but offset by the efficiency of GPT-6.1 Soul.
