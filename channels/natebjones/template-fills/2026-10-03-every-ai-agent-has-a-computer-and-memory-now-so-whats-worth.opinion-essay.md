## Thesis

The agent war is won by utility, not form factor: every AI agent now has a computer and memory, but the quality of work and task-specific competencies are what justify a $100 monthly subscription.

## Argument

The mechanism is utility. OpenAI's Dots, Meta's Muse, and Anthropic's Claude all offer agents with computers, memory, and background work, but the form factor is converging while the usefulness diverges. Dots is built for work, leveraging the context you've built in ChatGPT, and it shines in proactive, context-aware tasks like fixing a zombie meeting or adjusting launch materials when a feature slips. Muse is a free, practical personal assistant for calls and small purchases; Instinct is proactive around travel and restaurants. The real value is in the model behind the agent: Dots runs on Astra, the most powerful model, and the new GPT-6.1 Soul offers near-Astra quality at a fifth of the cost, making it the unsung hero for heavy users. OpenAI's broader strategy is to keep more work inside ChatGPT, with Spaces and Pages creating a collaborative workspace that competes with Microsoft Office. The outcome-focused shift—from activity to results—is the industry trend, and the opportunity for small teams and developers is to build plugins and services that deliver outcomes, not just activity.

## Counterpoints

- Why pay when anyone can copy a computer, memory, and background work? The answer is the quality of work the agent does for you.
- Spaces and Pages assume AI-native, AI-literate organizations, which are rare outside Silicon Valley; Microsoft has a trust advantage with Copilot.
- The $200 plan is getting a lower limit for the same price, which is unpopular; but GPT-6.1 Soul's efficiency may compensate.
- Agents like Dots focus too much on recent events for proactive suggestions; they need to evolve to distinguish what's truly important.
- The $500 Pro Max plan is only worth it if you have the skills to manage the speed; otherwise, it's a waste of money.
