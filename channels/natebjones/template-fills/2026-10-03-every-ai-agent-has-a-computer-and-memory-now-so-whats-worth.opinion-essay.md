## Thesis

The agent war is won by utility, not form factor: every agent now has a computer and memory, but what keeps you paying $100 a month is the quality of work it does for you.

## Argument

The mechanism is utility, not form factor. All agents are starting to look the same—OpenAI's Dots, Meta's Muse, and Grok's bot all offer a computer, memory, and background work. But just because the form factor converges doesn't mean the underlying model or task-specific competencies do. In practice, different agents excel at different jobs: Muse is great for practical, low-intelligence tasks like calls and small purchases; Instinct is proactive and travel-focused; Dots is built for serious work, leveraging the context you've accumulated in ChatGPT. This is where the real value lies—not in the packaging, but in the quality of the output. OpenAI's bet is that context becomes intelligent, and Dots is the first step in making that context work for you. The efficiency of the new GPT-6.1 Soul model is the unsung hero here, letting you do complex work without exhausting your plan limits. So, when deciding if $100 is worth it, don't just look at the feature set—ask if it saves you time or money. For me, it already has: Dots fixed a recurring weekend meeting and resolved a DMV dispute instantly. That's the outcome-focused future we're heading toward, and it's why I'd advise investing in the model, not the product.
