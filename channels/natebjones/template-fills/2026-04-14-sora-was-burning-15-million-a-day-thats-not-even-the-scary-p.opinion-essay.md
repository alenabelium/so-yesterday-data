## Thesis

The AI industry's hard constraint has shifted from training to inference economics, and the next 12 months will be defined by who can serve models profitably, not who can build the biggest cluster.

## Argument

The mechanism is cost-per-delivered-unit-of-revenue. Sora's shutdown, burning $15 million a day in inference against $2.1 million lifetime revenue, proves that serving models is now the bottleneck. This isn't a pricing problem; it's a structural shift from the training wall to the inference wall. Every AI product team must now ask: what does it cost to serve, and what do I get back? [[inference-cost]]

This economic lens also explains the first real ad dollars entering conversational AI. CRIO's integration with OpenAI's ad pilot showed 1.5x conversion rates from LLM referrals, collapsing the purchase funnel into a single context window. That threatens Google's $300 billion search ad business because conversational interfaces capture intent upstream, before the browser opens. The adtech layer, not the model provider, will fill that context, and $600 billion in ad budgets will follow. [[conversational-commerce]]

Meanwhile, the physical path to AI is closing. The White House's federal framework can't preempt local zoning moratoria in 12+ states or the Gulf conflict that turned data centers into kinetic targets. Asia is becoming the easiest place to build, and the center of gravity for compute is shifting there. [[data-center-geopolitics]]

The SaaS apocalypse is the same economics applied to software. Per-seat pricing collapses when 10 agents replace 100 reps, and Atlassian's first-ever decline in enterprise seat counts is the canary. ## Counterpoints

- Some argue AI video failure is just a product problem, but Sora's economics show it's an inference cost issue, not a go-to-market fix.
- Skeptics dismiss the 1.5x conversion data as small-sample, but the direction of the signal matters for where ad dollars migrate.
- Federal preemption of state AI laws is touted as easing regulation, but it can't override local zoning or utility commissions blocking data centers.
- Tech layoffs are often justified by AI, but inside boardrooms the narrative is overhiring and using AI as cover for cuts without real automation.
- The deploy-first vs. safety-first binary is too simple; the real question is how much control model makers retain over use, which will shape enterprise contracts.
