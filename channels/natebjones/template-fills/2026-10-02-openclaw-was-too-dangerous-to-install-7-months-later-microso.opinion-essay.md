## Thesis

Microsoft's Autopilot, built on OpenClaw, will reach far more workers than any rival AI this year, and the value it delivers depends on data access and user skill, not model intelligence.

## Argument

The mechanism is distribution. Microsoft's 450 million paid Microsoft 365 seats dwarf OpenAI's 9 million business users, and Autopilot is embedded in the tools people already use—Excel, Outlook, Teams. This isn't about being smarter; it's about being everywhere. The real leverage comes from the data it can access and how well you use it. The future is K-shaped: same tools, wildly different results. To land on the upward slope, you must master five practices. First, define what 'good' looks like—tell the AI the outcome you want, not just the task. Second, feed it the right context and verify it used the evidence; most failures are data-access problems, not intelligence problems. Third, make successful tasks repeatable—set triggers, schedules, and boundaries, and test each run. Fourth, match reasoning to complexity: don't use a cheap model for a nuanced decision just because both involve email. Fifth, obsess over improving the next run—find why a commitment was missed, fix the cause, and share what works. Microsoft's advantage is trust and existing relationships, but the real competition is about who understands the job well enough that we keep coming back to delegate work.

## Counterpoints

- A sufficiently powerful model could redesign workflows entirely, making Microsoft's process-heavy approach obsolete—Anthropic's guided agents are an example.
- Automatic routing may cost more tokens than just using a smart model, especially for complex new problems.
- Microsoft doesn't have its own frontier model; it relies on partners, which could limit its ability to compete on intelligence.
- Meta's enterprise push, leveraging its customer-relationship data, could challenge Microsoft's workplace dominance.
