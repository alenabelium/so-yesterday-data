## Thesis

Microsoft is bringing OpenClaw-derived agent technology to the enterprise as Autopilot, and the value it creates depends on data access and user skill, not model intelligence.

## Argument

The mechanism is distribution. Microsoft's 450 million paid Microsoft 365 seats dwarf OpenAI's 9 million business users, so Autopilot will reach far more working people than anything OpenAI or Anthropic releases this year. It's not better, but it's appearing everywhere—in Excel, Outlook, Teams—where you already work. The AI you have access to is about to start doing a different kind of work: not answering questions, but completing tasks while you do something else. What you do with it is what makes the difference.

Value comes from data access and user skill, not intelligence. Autopilot isn't very smart, but it can use your company's internal data—customer records, access permissions, assigned owners, decisions recorded in meetings. The opportunity Microsoft is selling is taking insights from inside your business and making them reusable. If an agent can use these connections, you spend less time remodeling your business for them.

Five practices determine which side of the K-shape you end up on. First, define what 'good' looks like. Give Autopilot a specific task aimed at something you care about, and tell it what 'good' looks like. Second, put the correct information in front of the model and check if it actually used it. Give it the current account plan, commitments, service data. Tell it which source takes priority when documents differ. Third, make a good task repeatable. ## Counterpoints

- Microsoft doesn't have its own field; a sufficiently powerful model can redesign workflows, making Microsoft's processes unnecessary.
- Autopilot's router may spend more tokens figuring out task complexity than just sending it to a smart model.
- A cheaper model may not meet client commitments, and the low model bill doesn't reflect the value of rework.
