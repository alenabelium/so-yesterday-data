## Thesis

Microsoft is bringing OpenClaw-derived agent technology to the enterprise as Autopilot, and the value it delivers will depend on data access and user skill, not model intelligence.

## Argument

The mechanism is distribution. Microsoft's 450 million paid Microsoft 365 seats dwarf OpenAI's 9 million business users, and Autopilot is embedded in the tools people already use—Excel, Outlook, Teams. This isn't about being smarter; it's about being everywhere. The real leverage comes from the data it can access and how well you use it. Five practices determine which side of the K-shape you land on. First, define what 'good' looks like—give the agent a specific task and tell it what a useful result is. Second, put the right information in front of the model and verify it actually used it. Third, make good tasks repeatable—set triggers and accountability. Fourth, match reasoning to complexity—don't use a cheap model for a hard problem. Fifth, obsess over improving the next run—fix the cause of failures and share what works. Microsoft's advantage is trust and existing relationships, but the real competition is about who understands the job well enough to keep you coming back.

## Counterpoints

- Microsoft doesn't have its own frontier model, so a sufficiently powerful model from Anthropic could redesign workflows more efficiently, making current processes obsolete.
- Autopilot's router may spend more tokens figuring out task complexity than just using a smart model, especially for novel problems.
- Meta's enterprise platform and data from customer relationships could challenge Microsoft's workplace dominance.
