## Thesis

Microsoft's Autopilot, built on OpenClaw-derived technology, will reach far more workers than any rival AI this year, and the value it delivers depends on data access and user skill, not model intelligence.

## Argument

The mechanism is distribution. Microsoft's 450 million paid Microsoft 365 seats dwarf OpenAI's 9 million business users, and Autopilot is embedded in the tools people already use—Excel, Outlook, Teams. This is why it matters more than any model release. The value isn't in the model's intelligence; it's in the data it can access and how well you use it. The future is K-shaped: same tools, wildly different outcomes. To land on the upward slope, you need five practices. First, define what 'good' looks like for a real task—tell the AI the standard, don't leave it unspoken. Second, give the model the right context and verify it used it; check the evidence tree. Third, make successful tasks repeatable—set triggers, schedules, and limits. Fourth, match reasoning to complexity—don't use a cheap model for a hard problem; separate routine prep from consistent interpretation. Fifth, obsess over improving the next run—fix the cause of failures, save what works, share with colleagues. Microsoft's advantage is trust and existing relationships, but it lacks its own frontier model. Anthropic's Claude, with its Excel and PowerPoint add-ins, could become the place people go to get work done, even while Microsoft keeps the documents. The real competition is over who understands the job well enough that we keep coming back to delegate work.

## Counterpoints

- The strongest argument against Microsoft winning is that frontier labs like Anthropic keep improving models, which can redesign workflows entirely, making Microsoft's process-based approach obsolete.
- Autopilot's router may waste tokens figuring out task complexity, potentially costing more than just using a smart model.
- Microsoft doesn't have its own frontier model, so it may lose the intelligence race even if it wins distribution.
