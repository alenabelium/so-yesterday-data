## Thesis

Microsoft's Autopilot, built on OpenClaw-derived technology, will bring agentic AI to hundreds of millions of workers, and the value it creates will depend not on model intelligence but on data access and user skill.

## Argument

The mechanism is distribution, not intelligence. Microsoft has 450 million paid Microsoft 365 seats, compared to OpenAI's 9 million business users. Autopilot is embedded in Excel, Outlook, Teams—where people already work. So the AI you have access to is about to start doing a different kind of work: completing tasks while you do something else. What you do with it is what makes the difference. This part doesn't come automatically.

First, define what 'good' looks like. Give Autopilot a specific task aimed at something you care about, and tell it what 'good' looks like. For example, ask it to identify outstanding customer obligations before a renewal conversation. Then check if the data it collected helps you act. Most people don't do this—they ask for a summary and leave the standard unspoken.

Second, put the correct information in front of the model and check if it actually used it. Give it the account plan, commitments, service records. Tell it which source takes priority when documents differ. Ask for an evidence tree. If the response is weak, check what information the tool had before deciding the model can't do it. Often the problem is access to information, not intelligence.

## Counterpoints

- Microsoft doesn't have its own frontier model; labs like Anthropic can redesign workflows more efficiently, potentially making Microsoft's processes obsolete.
- Autopilot's router may waste tokens figuring out task complexity, making it more expensive than just using a smart model.
- A cheaper model may not meet client commitments, and the low model bill IT receives doesn't reflect the value of rework.
- Meta's Enterprise Platform and Muse could challenge Microsoft by leveraging its customer-relationship data and distribution.
