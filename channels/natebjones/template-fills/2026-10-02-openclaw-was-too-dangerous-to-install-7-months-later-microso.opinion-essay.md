## Thesis

Microsoft's Autopilot, built on OpenClaw, will reach half a billion workers, and its value depends on data access and user skill, not model intelligence.

## Argument

The mechanism is distribution, not intelligence. Microsoft's 450 million paid Microsoft 365 seats dwarf OpenAI's 9 million business users, and Autopilot is embedded in Excel, Outlook, and Teams—where people already work. The agent is 'not very smart,' but it has credentials and a support contract, making it the AI your employer approves. The real differentiator is the K-shaped future: 5% of users get useful work done, while others get a fraction of the value. Five practices determine which slope you're on. First, define 'good'—show the model an example and explain what's useful, because asking for a summary leaves the standard unspoken. Second, provide context and verify evidence—give the model the account plan, commitments, and service data, then ask for an evidence tree and check if the source actually says what's claimed. Third, make tasks repeatable—set triggers, schedules, and limits, but watch for the failure mode of repeating a weak assignment. Fourth, match reasoning to complexity—separate routine prep from consistent interpretation, and consciously use stronger models for hard parts, because a cheap model that misses a commitment costs you rework time that IT's bill doesn't reflect. Fifth, obsess over improving the next run—fix the cause of errors, save fixes, and share what works. ## Counterpoints

- A sufficiently powerful model can find more efficient ways to prepare updates, eliminating the need for weekly reports and meetings that exist only to coordinate human activity.
- Anthropic's guided agents and Claude's Excel/Word add-ins could let companies keep Microsoft documents while delegating work to Claude, making another company the place we start for getting jobs done.
