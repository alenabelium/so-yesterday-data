## Thesis

By 2026, compounding AI advantages will make it the last chance for companies and individuals to catch up, as those who adapt fast will leave everyone else behind.

## Argument

The compounding gap is the core mechanism. Each breakthrough builds on the last: memory breakthroughs enable longer-running agents, which enable AI reviewing AI, which frees humans to focus on high-value judgment. This creates a flywheel where the fast get faster and the slow fall further behind. The power law of adoption will persist, with a few companies completely rebuilding workflows around agents while most add thin layers like email copilots. The stakes rise because these advantages compound, allowing disruptors to ambush slow incumbents and steal their customers in months. The workforce must retool at an unprecedented scale, learning to manage agent fleets and define work in engineering-shaped terms. The jet lag between cozy personal AI and governed work AI will be a major shift, and those who master the new skills will be incredibly valuable while others fall behind.

## Counterpoints

- Memory won't be perfect, but neither is human memory; tools like compression and markdown files will enable a reliable memory application layer.
- Recursive self-improvement raises fears of misalignment, but model makers will invest in alignment to keep the loop safe.
- Proactive AI risks becoming nagging, but the goal is high-precision, actionable interruptions with good taste.
- Some argue everyone is transforming in 2026, but the power law of adoption means most companies will barely change.
- Non-technical roles becoming engineering-shaped may seem daunting, but code will be accessible to everyone, not just engineers.
