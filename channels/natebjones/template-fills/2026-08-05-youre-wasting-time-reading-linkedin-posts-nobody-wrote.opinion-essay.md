## Thesis

AI-generated 'slop' is toxic to human attention and knowledge work; reclaiming authorship through a personal 'voice discovery' process is the only way to earn genuine human attention and restore clarity.

## Argument

The proliferation of AI slop on LinkedIn and in workplaces is not just annoying; it is a systemic drain on human attention. When someone uses AI to generate content without wrestling with it, they push the work downstream. The recipient pays the bill in hours spent checking, revising, and deciphering generic output. This is a failure of respect for other people's time and tokens.

The core technical issue is that AI models 'climb the same hill' over and over, converging on answers that are broadly rewarded as clear, confident, and professional. This creates a trough of sameness—identical sentence rhythms, headings, and dramatic contrasts. While useful for coding or standard tasks, this convergence is terrible for authorship, which requires a unique, non-standard perspective.

To fight this, we need a 'voice discovery skill.' This isn't about banning AI but using it to accelerate a deeply personal process of drafting and refining. By taking responsibility for the content we publish, we earn the most precious commodity: human attention. Agents may read anything, but humans choose who to listen to. Distinctive voice is the differentiator in a world of infinite, identical content. We must insist on conciseness, coherence, and clarity, treating AI as a tool inside the authorship process, not a replacement for the struggle that shapes our voice.

## Counterpoints

- Some anti-slop skills are actually good at catching familiar habits or simplifying coding tasks.
- AI tools are loved for their speed and ability to run fast.
- Universal checklists (like banning em-dashes) might seem like a quick fix for slop.
