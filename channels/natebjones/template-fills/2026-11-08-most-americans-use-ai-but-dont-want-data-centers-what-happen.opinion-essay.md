## Thesis

The November 2026 backlash against data centers will paradoxically strengthen big tech, push smaller AI players to the brink, and accelerate the global and orbital displacement of AI infrastructure.

## Argument

The mechanism is capital and time. When communities vote to block data centers, they don't kill demand—they delay supply. A neo-cloud that borrowed money, ordered GB300s, and signed a customer contract now faces a ticking clock: every month of permitting delay burns cash while depreciation and interest continue. Even with plenty of customers, a small player can run out of money before its facility opens. That's the paradox: the political defeat doesn't stop AI, it concentrates it. Big operators with existing capacity and deep pockets can absorb delays, buy distressed projects at fire-sale prices, and serve clients from other facilities. They gain pricing power and utilization advantages. Meanwhile, smaller competitors are forced to rent capacity from the very giants they're trying to beat—making compute access a lever of control.

But there's a second-order effect that cuts the other way. As Derek Thompson argued, slower construction gives demand time to catch up with supply, reducing the risk of a 2027 token glut. The industry may avoid a bubble precisely because it can't overbuild. That's cold comfort for the startups that go bankrupt, but it stabilizes the market.

Third, the search for buildable sites goes global. Developers will flock to places like Canada, Mexico, and parts of Asia that offer stable land, energy, and friendly politics. Jobs and spending shift overseas, and Americans end up renting AI services rather than owning the infrastructure. Finally, this uncertainty makes orbital computing more attractive.
