## TL;DR

AI is bifurcating the economy: in digital contestable markets, it commoditizes cognitive work and crushes mid-tier firms, while in physical, local, relationship-heavy markets, it lowers overhead without increasing competition. The framework's three layers—tokenizable cognition, judgment and accountability, and physical execution—determine where value and scarcity lie. Your strategic position and AI investment strategy depend on which layer your business operates in.

## The argument

### Define the Three Layers of Work
- **Anchor Timestamps**: ['00:04:48']
- **Claim**: AI collapses the cost of cognitive tasks expressible in language—drafting, summarizing, analyzing, coding—which is 'tokenizable cognition.' The second layer is judgment and accountability, where humans decide and own outcomes, and the third is physical execution, constrained by atoms and geography. These layers determine where AI creates abundance and where it creates bottlenecks.
- **Role**: definition

### Jevons Paradox Expands Cognitive Work
- **Anchor Timestamps**: ['00:04:48']
- **Claim**: When cognitive work becomes cheaper, we use more of it, not less—Jevons Paradox. Firms produce more customer segments, more AB tests, more reports, and more first drafts. The first-order effect is not AI replacing workers but enabling vastly more cognitive output, shifting humans to tasks within that expanded volume.
- **Role**: evidence

### Mid-Tier Firms Get Squeezed in Contestable Markets
- **Anchor Timestamps**: ['00:09:20']
- **Claim**: In digital contestable markets, mid-tier firms like a 50-person marketing agency are squeezed from below by lean AI-native teams and from above by giants with distribution moats. Their value proposition—producing cognitive work—is commoditized, and they can't match the cost structure of startups or the stickiness of platforms.
- **Role**: evidence

### Physical Markets Are Protected by Contestability and Baumol's Cost Disease
- **Anchor Timestamps**: ['00:11:47']
- **Claim**: In physical, local, relationship-heavy markets like plumbing, AI lowers overhead but doesn't increase contestability—you can't switch to a plumber in Hong Kong. Baumol's cost disease suggests that as AI makes other sectors more productive, wages rise economy-wide, making non-AI-productive services relatively more expensive, benefiting these firms.
- **Role**: synthesis

### Strategic Responses Depend on Your Layer
- **Anchor Timestamps**: ['00:13:58']
- **Claim**: For mid-tier digital firms, the viable paths are to get radically lean or move up the stack to judgment and accountability. For physical firms, focus on back-office efficiency, not transformation. AI-native startups must run toward distribution and second-layer bottlenecks, while giants must invest in internal talent and operational change to counter 'ankle biters.'
- **Role**: synthesis

## Evidence and caveats

The speaker uses the example of a 50-person marketing agency versus a three-person AI-native team and a giant media company to illustrate the squeeze on mid-tier digital firms. For physical businesses, he cites plumbing and dentistry, where AI automates scheduling and invoicing but doesn't make the market more contestable. He also references Baumol's cost disease with the orchestra example. Caveats: the speaker notes that AI's impact on judgment and physical execution is not yet a general-purpose unlock; robotics breakthroughs are more likely in factories than home services. He acknowledges that AI-native startups can still succeed if they have distribution or high switching costs, and that giants are not immune—Kmart's decline shows that even physical-layer advantages can be eroded by competitors like Walmart.
