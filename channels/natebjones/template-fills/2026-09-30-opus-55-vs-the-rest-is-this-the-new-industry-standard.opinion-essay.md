## Thesis

Opus 5.5 is the new industry standard because its task-level efficiency—not raw token counts—delivers more value per dollar than any previous model.

## Argument

The real metric isn't tokens per prompt; it's tokens per completed task. My 514-piece LEGO build, a complex visual and engineering challenge, consumed only 1% of my weekly Claude usage—about 50 cents of a $50 plan—versus $44 at API prices. That's the efficiency leap. Opus 5.5's pricing is 20% lower than Opus 5 and 60% lower than Fable 5.1, but the deeper win is fewer attempts, fewer corrections, and less back-and-forth. Anthropic claims typical workloads cost 40% less, and user reports from GitHub, Lovable, and Spotify corroborate this. This efficiency isn't just about cost; it's about headroom. With 99% of my budget left, I can iterate, explore, and take on bigger projects. The model's improved controllability—both in writing and visual work—means I can direct it precisely, and it follows through without resistance. This is a direct response to community feedback on Opus 5's writing issues, which Anthropic acknowledged and fixed. The broader story is recursive improvement: Anthropic says Claude writes 80% of its merged code, and labs use AI to build better AI, accelerating the release cycle to ~18 days. This feedback loop—users reporting issues, labs fixing them with AI assistance—is why Opus 5.5 feels like a leap. To measure this for yourself, run your own tasks with the same inputs and track total tokens, API-equivalent cost, and weekly usage. Compare across models. That's how you'll see where the real gains are.

## Counterpoints

- Your tasks aren't my tasks—efficiency varies by use case, so a single benchmark doesn't apply.
- Counting tokens too early is misleading; a great screenshot isn't the whole job—you need to measure the entire task.
- Opus 5.5's persistence can lead to long, token-wasting runs without clear stopping conditions.
- Writing quality is subjective; some may prefer the style of previous models like 4.6.
