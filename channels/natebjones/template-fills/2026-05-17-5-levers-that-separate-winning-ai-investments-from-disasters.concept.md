## TL;DR

Successful AI investment requires analyzing specific workflows rather than focusing on models or vendors. Leaders should apply five strategic levers—automate, build, buy, hire, and wait—based on workflow characteristics like repetition, complexity, and market maturity. A core principle is defining workflows in plain English before allocating capital to avoid failure.

## The argument

### Define the Workflow Loop
- **Anchor Timestamps**: ['00:02:45']
- **Claim**: AI investment is fundamentally about the shape of work, not the model. A workflow is the entire operating loop including inputs, allowed actions, output standards, and accountability, with the AI model being just one component.
- **Role**: definition

### Apply the Five Strategic Levers
- **Anchor Timestamps**: ['00:05:02']
- **Claim**: Every workflow can be addressed by one of five levers: automate (for routine, repetitive tasks), build (for unique, complex work with company-specific context), buy (for common work with mature market solutions), hire (to fill specific talent gaps), or wait (for lower-priority items to avoid premature investment).
- **Role**: definition

### Use the Investment Matrix
- **Anchor Timestamps**: ['00:21:18']
- **Claim**: Decisions are guided by a matrix plotting how specific the work is to the company against the maturity of the market solution. Common/mature work is a buy; specific/immature work is a build; common/immature work is prototype or wait.
- **Role**: evidence

### Enforce the Description Rule
- **Anchor Timestamps**: ['00:20:23']
- **Claim**: The critical constraint for any AI project is that you must be able to describe the work in plain English, including inputs, outputs, standards, and exceptions. If you cannot describe it, you cannot automate it or make sound investment decisions.
- **Role**: counter

## Evidence and caveats

The speaker cites Gartner's prediction that 40% of Agentic AI projects will fail by 2027 due to unclear business value and risk controls. Examples include accounts receivable teams having multiple distinct workflows (collections, invoice matching) rather than one problem, and the use of Finn by Intercom for customer support. Caveats include the difficulty of hiring for 'purple unicorn' AI talent and the risk of buying vendor solutions that don't fit specific workflows, as seen with the Harvey legal example. The speaker emphasizes that automation fails when exceptions hold the value, and building fails when executives cannot define 'good' output.
