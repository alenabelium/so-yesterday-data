## TL;DR

AI investment decisions fail when they start with models or vendors instead of the shape of the work. The five levers—automate, build, buy, hire, wait—let leaders match each workflow to the right capital allocation. The core rule: do not automate what you cannot describe.

## The argument

### Define the Workflow, Not the Model
- **Anchor Timestamps**: ['00:01:29']
- **Claim**: AI investment is not an AI question; it's a question about the shape of work. The model, vendor, and dashboard are all downstream. The unit of decision is the workflow—the entire operating loop—not a department or role.
- **Role**: definition

### Automate Only What Repeats and Is Cheap to Verify
- **Anchor Timestamps**: ['00:05:02']
- **Claim**: Automation is the easiest lever when work repeats, follows clear patterns, has definable exceptions, and can be checked cheaply. But don't automate when exceptions hold most of the value—that's where bad demos mislead buyers.
- **Role**: evidence

### Build When You Own the Secret Sauce
- **Anchor Timestamps**: ['00:07:56']
- **Claim**: Building is for workflows with company-specific context, edge cases, and standards. The hard part is defining what 'good' looks like—executives must be able to judge output quality, not just approve the build.
- **Role**: definition

### Buy Primitives, Not Whole Workflows
- **Anchor Timestamps**: ['00:11:00']
- **Claim**: Buying works when you can integrate a solution into your workflow quickly. Prefer primitives (like Stripe's) that stack into your builds, and only buy whole solutions (like Harvey) when there's an 80-90% overlap with your workflow shape.
- **Role**: synthesis

### Hire for Specific Gaps, Not Unicorns
- **Anchor Timestamps**: ['00:13:49']
- **Claim**: Hiring for a 'purple unicorn' is a losing game. Instead, define the human capability your workflows need in 6-12 months and hire for that missing piece. The workflow definition unlocks a clear job description.
- **Role**: counter

## Evidence and caveats

The speaker cites IBM ask HR and Intercom's Finn as examples of automation buys. He warns that vendors often demo routine cases while production traffic is full of exceptions, leading to low accuracy and buyer disappointment. For buying, he notes that primitives are easy to start with, but whole workflow solutions like Harvey require deep product knowledge. He also cautions that waiting is not about avoiding AI transformation but about prioritizing leverage; SQL query pools might be worth waiting on if they work fine. The investment matrix shows: common work + mature market = buy; common + immature = prototype or wait; company-specific + useful primitives = buy primitives and own the workflow; company-specific + thin market = build. Hiring cuts across all, and if no one can define the workflow, that's a signal to hire.
