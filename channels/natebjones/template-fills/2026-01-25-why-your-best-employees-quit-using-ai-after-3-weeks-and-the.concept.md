## TL;DR

A Microsoft study of 300,000 Copilot users found that AI excitement peaks at three weeks before most employees quietly stop using the tool. The problem isn't tool training (101) or technical implementation (401), but a missing middle layer of applied judgment: knowing which parts of work AI should handle, how to verify outputs, and how to integrate AI into workflows. This '201-level' gap is a management problem, not a tool problem, and it's where most productivity gains actually live.

- **Anchor Timestamps**: ['00:00:00']
- **Claim**: Microsoft tracked 300,000 Copilot users and found excitement peaked for three weeks before a crater of disappointment, after which most people quietly stopped using AI. In most organizations, this shows up as the 80/20 rule: 20% monthly active users, 80% dormant seats. The survivors figured out that AI isn't a tool skill—it's a management skill.

## Evidence and caveats

The Microsoft study of 300,000 Copilot users is the primary evidence for the 3-week trough and the 80/20 usage rule. The BCG and Harvard study provides the jagged frontier evidence: consultants using AI on tasks inside the frontier were 12% more productive and 25% faster, but on tasks outside it, they were 19 percentage points less likely to reach correctness. The speaker notes that the frontier moves and that experts typically know where the jagged boundaries are. The speaker also cites Simon Willis on the value of spending time with AI in one's domain, and Ethan Mollick's framing that the best AI users are good managers and teachers. Caveats: the speaker acknowledges that vocalized use cases are only partially correct and that real use cases often emerge when digging under the surface. The speaker also warns that the apprentice model is collapsing, leading to a judgment deficit that compounds over time.
