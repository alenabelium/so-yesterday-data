## Thesis

The internet is fragmenting into zones of access for AI agents, driven by security and business decisions, so consumers will face unequal access that a smarter model cannot overcome.

## Argument

The mechanism is security. Black October showed how AI makes hacking easier, so businesses now have reasons to restrict agents, not welcome them. Banks are already saying they can't tell an authorized agent from an attacker, because agent and human behavior look nearly identical. This forces a new infrastructure of authorization—protocols like Visa's bill-pay pilot, Stripe's Link, Cloudflare's signed agents, and Google's Agent Payments Protocol—where businesses decide who gets in. The result is a three-zone internet: a partnership zone with sanctioned access, a novice-actor zone where agents fumble through sites, and a forbidden zone where businesses like Amazon say no. This fragmentation is not a technical failure but a business choice, and it will reshape how we delegate tasks, shop, and trust our agents. The winners will be the trusted intermediaries who secure these connections, much like Amazon did for e-commerce in the 90s. But for now, consumers must be careful about where they send agents, and we need to communicate their limits honestly to non-technical users.

## Counterpoints

- A smarter model might solve agent access problems, but the host argues that a smarter model cannot unlock doors that a business has decided to close.
- Some might expect a single open internet for agents, but the host argues that security concerns and business interests will fragment access instead.
- Businesses might see agent access as purely beneficial, but the host notes that distinguishing authorized agents from attackers is nearly impossible, leading to restrictions.
