## TL;DR

AI labs are buying massive archives of corporate work records—emails, Slack messages, documents—to train agents, believing these traces capture intelligent work. But these records are often chaotic imitations of work, not the work itself, and they come disproportionately from failing companies. This raises urgent questions about who profits, what counts as work, and whether agents can ever learn real judgment from such data.

## The argument

### The Market for Work Records
- **Anchor Timestamps**: ['00:00:00']
- **Claim**: AI labs like Google are paying millions for corporate work records (e.g., Spirit Airlines' emails and Teams messages) to train agents, based on the theory that these traces can teach AI to do intelligent work. This creates a market where the company selling records gets paid, the broker gets a commission, but the employee whose work created the data gets nothing.
- **Role**: definition

### Records Capture Imitation, Not Work
- **Anchor Timestamps**: ['00:04:04']
- **Claim**: The records of work—messages, meetings, documents—are not the work itself. They are the chaotic byproducts of coordination and visibility, not the value creation. For example, resolving an invoice mismatch involves tacit knowledge and judgment that isn't captured in the 30 messages and three meetings it took to fix it.
- **Role**: evidence

### The Problem of Learning from Chaos
- **Anchor Timestamps**: ['00:05:21']
- **Claim**: If agents are trained on these records, they learn to imitate the chaos, not the underlying judgment. The valuable intervention—like noticing a credit was misapplied—is buried in a pile of routine messages. Agents can't distinguish what matters, so they learn to reproduce the imitation, not the work.
- **Role**: counter

### The Data Comes from Failing Companies
- **Anchor Timestamps**: ['00:09:40']
- **Claim**: The data sold to labs mostly comes from companies on the brink of bankruptcy, like Spirit Airlines, because they have no other use for it. This means agents are trained on examples of work that may not have been successful in the long run, further undermining the value of the data.
- **Role**: evidence

### The Real Work Is Coordination and Judgment
- **Anchor Timestamps**: ['00:18:41']
- **Claim**: The real work is in the coordination, judgment, and context-specific problem-solving that isn't captured in records. Agents can imitate the output, but they can't replicate the human ability to understand a specific company's context, evaluate issues for a specific customer, and solve them in a specific software environment.
- **Role**: synthesis

## Evidence and caveats

The speaker cites the Spirit Airlines auction where Google bid $10 million for 100 million emails and half a billion Teams entries, beating Mercor's $7.5 million bid. He notes that Mercor is also acquiring Deep Tune to build training environments, and that they pay experts to define success criteria for agent tasks. He acknowledges that some coordination in records is necessary, but argues that much is just the price of working within a particular company. He also mentions that employees may privately resist AI tools, with leaders estimating up to a third of teams passively sabotaging them. The speaker hedges that he believes in agents and has seen success stories, but insists that the value of an agent is in identifying repetitive work to automate, not in replacing the judgment required for intellectual labor.
