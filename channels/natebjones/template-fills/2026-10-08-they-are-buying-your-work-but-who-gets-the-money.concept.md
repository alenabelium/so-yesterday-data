## TL;DR

AI labs are buying archives of corporate work records—emails, Slack messages, documents—to train agents. But these records capture only the chaotic imitation of work, not the true value of intellectual labor. This raises urgent questions about what work really is, who profits, and how employees can defend their contributions.

## The argument

### The Market for Work Records
- **Anchor Timestamps**: ['00:00:00']
- **Claim**: AI labs like Google pay millions for archives of corporate work records—emails, Slack messages, documents—believing they can teach AI to do intelligent work. The seller gets paid, the broker gets a commission, but the employee whose work created the data gets nothing.
- **Role**: definition

### Records Capture Imitation, Not Work
- **Anchor Timestamps**: ['00:04:04']
- **Claim**: Work records show the coordination and communication around tasks, but not the actual value creation. The valuable intervention—like noticing a credit error—is buried in 30 messages and three meetings. Agents trained on this data learn to imitate the process, not the judgment.
- **Role**: evidence

### The Data Comes from Failing Companies
- **Anchor Timestamps**: ['00:09:40']
- **Claim**: The incentive to sell work records is strongest for businesses on the verge of bankruptcy, like Spirit Airlines. So the data agents learn from is mostly from failing companies, which may not even represent successful work. This biases the training data toward chaos and failure.
- **Role**: counter

### The Real Work Is Coordination and Judgment
- **Anchor Timestamps**: ['00:18:41']
- **Claim**: The real work is not in the messages or documents but in the coordination, judgment, and context-specific problem-solving that moves the business forward. Agents can imitate the visible traces, but they miss the tacit knowledge and decision-making that create value.
- **Role**: synthesis

## Evidence and caveats

The Spirit Airlines deal is the central example: Google bid $10 million for 100 million emails and half a billion Teams messages, beating Mercor's $7.5 million. Mercor also acquires training environments, paying experts to define success criteria—a hard problem. The speaker notes that work records are 'chaotic imitation' and that agents trained on them may produce 'simulation of work' that is 'entertaining, expensive, and of little value.' He also mentions that employees privately resist AI tools, with leaders estimating up to a third of teams passively sabotaging them. The speaker hedges that he believes in agents but stresses that their value lies in handling repetitive tasks, not replacing judgment.
