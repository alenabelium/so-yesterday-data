## TL;DR

AI labs are buying massive archives of corporate work records, like Spirit Airlines' emails and Teams messages, to train agents. But these records capture only the chaotic imitation of work, not the true value of intellectual labor. This disconnect raises urgent questions about what work really is and who profits from its data.

## The argument

### The Market for Work Records
- **Anchor Timestamps**: ['00:00:00']
- **Claim**: AI labs like Google are paying millions for corporate work archives (e.g., Spirit Airlines' emails) to train agents, believing these records teach intelligent work. But this theory is flawed because it mistakes the chaotic imitation of work for the real thing.
- **Role**: definition

### The Imitation Problem
- **Anchor Timestamps**: ['00:05:21']
- **Claim**: Archives record both valuable interventions and mundane coordination side-by-side, so agents can't distinguish what matters. They learn to imitate the entire process, not the essence of the work, leading to a simulation of work that is expensive and low-value.
- **Role**: evidence

### The Last-Hope Data Problem
- **Anchor Timestamps**: ['00:09:40']
- **Claim**: The data sold to labs mostly comes from failing companies like Spirit Airlines, whose records reflect bankruptcy-era chaos, not successful work. This biases the training data toward failure, undermining the entire enterprise.
- **Role**: counter

### The Real Work Is Coordination
- **Anchor Timestamps**: ['00:18:41']
- **Claim**: The real work is in coordination, judgment, and context—not in the messages. Agents that imitate Slack chatter produce reports with nothing to report. True value comes from agents that structure data and augment human judgment, not replace it.
- **Role**: synthesis

## Evidence and caveats

The Spirit Airlines deal is a concrete example: Google bid $10 million, beating Mercor's $7.5 million, for 100 million emails and 500 million Teams entries. Mercor also acquires training environments to define success criteria. The speaker notes that employees privately resist AI tools, with leaders estimating up to a third of teams passively sabotaging them. Caveats: the deal is not final (hearing October 14), and the speaker admits his examples are anonymized personal experiences, not universal. He also acknowledges that some data brokers like Grepped package small business data for $20k–$5M, but he is skeptical of the value.
