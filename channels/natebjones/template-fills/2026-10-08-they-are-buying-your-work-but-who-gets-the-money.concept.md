## TL;DR

AI labs are buying archives of corporate work records—emails, Slack messages, documents—to train agents, but these records capture only the chaotic imitation of work, not its true value. This raises urgent questions about who profits from your labor and what counts as real work in the age of AI. Understanding this market is crucial for anyone whose job involves intellectual work.

## The argument

### The Market for Work Records
- **Anchor Timestamps**: ['00:00:00']
- **Claim**: AI labs like Google are paying millions for corporate work archives (e.g., Spirit Airlines' emails and Teams messages) to train agents, believing these records can teach AI to do intelligent work. This market is based on a flawed assumption that work is fully captured in digital traces.
- **Role**: definition

### The Imitation Problem
- **Anchor Timestamps**: ['00:05:21']
- **Claim**: Work records include both valuable interventions and mundane coordination, making it impossible for agents to distinguish real work from imitation. The example of resolving an invoice dispute shows that the critical insight is buried in a mess of messages, and agents trained on this data will learn to imitate the chaos, not the value.
- **Role**: evidence

### The Last-Hope Data Problem
- **Anchor Timestamps**: ['00:09:40']
- **Claim**: The companies most likely to sell their work records are those on the brink of bankruptcy, like Spirit Airlines. This means the data agents learn from comes predominantly from failing companies, skewing the training data with examples of unsuccessful work and reinforcing the imitation problem.
- **Role**: counter

### The Need for a New Definition of Work
- **Anchor Timestamps**: ['00:14:11']
- **Claim**: To navigate this landscape, we must redefine work as the ability to increase profits or reduce costs, not as a collection of messages or documents. This reframing empowers employees to articulate their true value and resist the reduction of intellectual labor to optimization problems.
- **Role**: synthesis

## Evidence and caveats

The speaker cites the Spirit Airlines auction (Google's $10M bid vs. Mercor's $7.5M) and the sheer volume of data (100M emails, 500M Teams entries) as evidence of the market's scale. He also mentions Mercor's approach of paying experts to define success criteria, highlighting the difficulty of proving intellectual work. Caveats include the speaker's admission that the deal is not yet final (hearing on Oct 14) and his acknowledgment that some coordination in work records is necessary, not just noise. He also notes that employees may privately resist AI tools, with leaders estimating up to a third of teams passively sabotaging implementation.
