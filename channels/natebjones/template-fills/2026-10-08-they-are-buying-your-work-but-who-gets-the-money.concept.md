## TL;DR

AI labs are paying millions for corporate work archives—emails, Slack messages, and documents—to train agents. But these records capture only the chaotic imitation of work, not the true value of intellectual labor. This raises urgent questions about what work really is, who profits from its data, and how employees can defend their contributions.

## The argument

### The Market for Work Records
- **Anchor Timestamps**: ['00:00:00']
- **Claim**: AI labs like Google are buying vast archives of corporate work records—emails, Slack messages, documents—to train agents. The prevailing theory is that these records can teach AI to do intelligent work. But this theory is flawed because it conflates the chaotic imitation of work with the actual value of intellectual labor.
- **Role**: definition

### The Imitation Problem
- **Anchor Timestamps**: ['00:04:04']
- **Claim**: Work records capture the visible traces of work—messages, meetings, documents—but not the tacit knowledge and judgment that make work valuable. For example, resolving an invoice discrepancy involves understanding context and making decisions that aren't fully captured in the data. Agents trained on such records will learn to imitate the process, not the underlying value creation.
- **Role**: evidence

### The Data of Last Hope
- **Anchor Timestamps**: ['00:09:40']
- **Claim**: The companies selling their work records are often on the verge of bankruptcy, like Spirit Airlines. This means the data agents learn from is predominantly from failing companies, which may not represent successful work practices. The incentive to sell is highest when the data has no other perceived value, leading to a skewed training set.
- **Role**: counter

### The Real Work Is Coordination
- **Anchor Timestamps**: ['00:18:41']
- **Claim**: The real work lies in coordination, judgment, and achieving business outcomes—not in the individual messages or documents. Agents that imitate work may produce outputs that look good but lack the underlying understanding of context and purpose. The value of an agent is in automating repetitive tasks, not in replicating the full complexity of intellectual labor.
- **Role**: synthesis

## Evidence and caveats

The speaker cites the Spirit Airlines auction where Google bid $10 million for 100 million emails and 500 million Teams messages, beating Mercor's $7.5 million bid. He also mentions Grepped, a data broker packaging small business data for $20,000 to $5 million. He notes that employees often privately resist AI tools, with leaders estimating up to a third of teams passively sabotaging them. He cautions that agents trained on such data may produce 'simulations of work' that are 'incredibly entertaining, quite expensive, and of little value.' He also acknowledges that some companies like Glean offer transparency into work data, but their effectiveness is limited by the messiness of the underlying data.
