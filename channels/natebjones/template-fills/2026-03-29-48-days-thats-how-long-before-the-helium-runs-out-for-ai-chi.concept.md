## TL;DR

A missile strike on Qatar's Ras Laffan complex has cut off a third of global helium supply, a gas with no substitute in chipmaking. This disruption, plus LNG price spikes, threatens memory and logic chip production for years. The crisis could reshape geopolitics, favoring China's domestic chip ambitions.

## The argument

### Helium is the irreplaceable gas in chipmaking
- **Anchor Timestamps**: ['00:00:00']
- **Claim**: Helium is essential for EUV lithography and leak testing in fabs, with no substitute. Its unique properties—small atomic size and thermal conductivity—make it critical for temperature control during plasma etching and for detecting vacuum leaks. Advanced fabs consume up to 20,000 cubic meters per month, and purity must be 6N (99.9999%).
- **Role**: definition

### Qatar's shutdown creates a 48-day clock
- **Anchor Timestamps**: ['00:02:14']
- **Claim**: Ras Laffan produced 33% of global helium, now offline due to missile damage. Liquid helium in ISO containers vaporizes in 35-48 days, so stranded shipments are lost. Qatar Energy admits 14% of capacity is permanently damaged, with reconstruction taking up to 5 years. The planned Helium 4 plant is delayed, adding future pressure.
- **Role**: evidence

### LNG price spikes compound the crisis
- **Anchor Timestamps**: ['00:11:22']
- **Claim**: Beyond helium, LNG supply disruptions raise energy costs for East Asian fabs, which import most of their energy. These higher input costs will pass through to chip prices, affecting data center and consumer electronics costs. Europe is also vulnerable due to its reliance on LNG imports, while the US has some domestic cushion.
- **Role**: evidence

### Geopolitics may favor China's chip stack
- **Anchor Timestamps**: ['00:14:53']
- **Claim**: The crisis accelerates China's push for energy independence via the Power of Siberia 2 pipeline and domestic helium production. If China secures cheap energy and helium, it can scale its native chip fabrication, gaining a cost advantage in AI inference. This structural shift exposes South Korea and Taiwan, which lack domestic helium and remain dependent on Qatar.
- **Role**: synthesis

## Evidence and caveats

The speaker cites specific data: Qatar produces 2.4 billion standard cubic feet of helium per year (33% of global supply). South Korea imported 2/3 of its helium from Qatar in 2025. SK Hynix and Samsung, the top memory makers, are exposed. TSMC holds only 11 days of LNG reserves. Helium spot prices have doubled, and contract surcharges are up 30%. The speaker hedges: fabs will not shut down completely; they will operate at lower capacity or slower, causing delays and price increases. Public statements from SK Hynix and TSMC claiming they are fine are met with skepticism. The speaker notes that the US Federal Helium Reserve ended sales in 2023, and Russia's supply is offline due to war. Qualification of new suppliers at 6N purity takes months or years. The speaker also cautions that the conflict timeline is unpredictable, and repair of the complex is harder than the Saturn 5 rocket.
