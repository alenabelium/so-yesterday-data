# Most jobs are going away

## Executive Summary

The video argues that the vast majority of modern jobs are based on 'derived demand,' where human labor is merely an incidental means to an end rather than the core value, making these roles highly vulnerable to automation by AI and robotics. While a small segment of 'essential demand' exists—categorized as presence, provenance, liability, and affinity—it represents only about 5.5% of current employment and cannot absorb the displaced workforce due to physical limits on human attention and time. Consequently, even if wealth increases and luxury spending rises, the structural bottleneck of finite human attention ensures that most jobs will disappear rather than transform into new human-centric roles.

## Key Points

- Labor's share of national income has been declining for decades, falling below 53% in developed economies, signaling a long-term shift toward a post-labor economy where capital captures value over labor [00:01:41](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=kBZNi8zPUTQ&t=101).
- Most jobs rely on 'derived demand,' meaning the outcome (like a house or health) is valued, but the human input is incidental and will be replaced if machines can deliver it better, faster, cheaper, and safer [00:02:41](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=kBZNi8zPUTQ&t=161).
- Essential demand, which preserves jobs, consists of four components: presence (physical embodiment), provenance (human authorship), liability (accountability), and affinity (identity attachment), but this accounts for only ~5.5% of current work [00:11:04](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=kBZNi8zPUTQ&t=664).
- The 'essential' job market is constrained by the scarcity of human attention and time; even with doubled leisure hours, the power law of the attention economy means only a tiny fraction of people can monetize these roles effectively [00:29:03](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=kBZNi8zPUTQ&t=1743).
