# Why people HATE automation

**[00:00:01]** The night the machines broke. Nottinghamshire, 1811, and the first great fight against automation. On a cold November night in 1811, in the villages north of Nottingham, a group of men moved quietly in the darkness. Some smeared their faces with soot. Some carried the hammer. They knew what houses they were looking for and what machines. These were hosiery masters, skilled workers who made stockings and lace on hand looms. Many of them rented these machines and worked from home, receiving payment for each product . And that winter they starved. Britain was at war with Napoleon. Trade routes to Europe and America were blocked. Bread was expensive, orders were lacking, and wages were falling. During these difficult times, working methods in the industry have changed. Some hosiers, merchants who sold finished goods, began using wide looms to knit large cloths, which were then cut and sewn into cheap stockings—so- called "scraps." These " trimmings" were of poor quality. They were dissolving. But they were cheap and could be made by workers without professional training for less pay. For the masters, this was not progress. This was a violation of the agreements. So they started breaking the machines. The raids were disciplined.

**[00:01:35]** Men chose specific machines in specific workshops, often those owned by employers who paid less than the set rates. Neighboring machines sometimes remained untouched. Later, letters appeared, signed by a mysterious commander—Ned Ladd or General Ladd. Or, with a touch of theatricality, King Ladd. Such a person did not exist. Ladd was a legend, a name borrowed from a story about an apprentice who, years before, had smashed a lathe in a fit of rage. But it was very difficult to arrest a leader who did not exist. In early 1812, the movement spread north. In Yorkshire, shearers, highly paid craftsmen who processed woolen cloth with huge hand shears, were confronted with shearing machines that did the job much faster. In Lancashire, weavers watched as power looms appeared in cotton mills . The complaints were different in detail, but the same in spirit. The way of life and the income that supported it were being destroyed from above. In Yorkshire, the shearers' main weapon was a heavy sledgehammer, which they called an Enoch.

**[00:02:56]** The name came from Enoch Taylor, a blacksmith from Marsden, whose firm, which he ran with his brother James, manufactured many of the same shearing machines that the workers hated . Hammers and machines came from the same forge. The shearers turned this irony into a grim slogan. Enoch made them, and Enoch must break them. The government's response was force. Thousands of soldiers were sent to the troubled counties. Spies and informants were paid to infiltrate groups. And in February 1812, Parliament made breaking machine tools a crime punishable by death. Not everyone in Westminster agreed with this. On February 27, the 24-year-old poet stood up in the House of Lords to deliver his first speech. His name was George Gordon, Lord Byron. He traveled extensively and wanted his fellow peers to understand what he saw at home. “I crossed the theater of war on the peninsula,” he said. "I have been in some of the most oppressed provinces of Turkey , but never under the most despotic infidel governments have I seen such abject poverty as I saw upon my return in the very heart of a Christian country." The bill was passed anyway. That spring, the conflict escalated into real violence. In April 1812, a large band of Luddites attacked the Rawfolds factory in West Yorkshire, where the owner, William Cartwright, had fortified the building and was waiting with armed soldiers.

**[00:04:43]** The attack failed. Two attackers were fatally wounded. A few weeks later, another factory owner, William Gorsfall, was shot dead on his way home from the market. The reckoning came in January 1813 at a special court convened in York. When the trials were over, 17 men were sentenced to hang. Others were sent to penal colonies in Australia. The executions were public, and the message was unmistakable. After that, the movement died down, although it flared up again in the following years. Trade eventually resumed after the war. Salary—no. Within a generation, Lancashire weavers, once among the highest-paid workers in England, found themselves among the poorest. Here's the part we usually get wrong. Today, "Luddite" is an insult. This means a person who is afraid of technology, who does not understand it, who wants to stop time. But true Luddites knew their machines well. Many of them had been working on machines for decades. They were not against technology as such. They opposed specific uses of technology . One that lowered wages, degraded quality, and ignored the customs that governed their craft. One of their songs directly expressed this demand. She called for full-time work at the old price, established, as the song went, by custom and law. This is not the language of people who hate technology. This is the language of people who agree on who gets the profits.

**[00:06:28]** They lost these negotiations . They lost the battle with hammers in the dark against the army and parliament, which had already decided whose interests mattered. Every decision to introduce a new machine is also a decision about the fate of people. Whose skills will depreciate, whose earnings will increase, and whose voice will carry weight ? In 1812, employers made this choice alone, with the army behind them . This part of the story is usually overlooked . Two centuries later, we are having the same argument again. The cars have become different. They write, think, draw, and drive. But the underlying issues have hardly changed. Who owns the new tools? Who decides how to use them? And when productivity increases, who gets the bonus? The Luddites were the first to raise these questions. They should be remembered for what they did , not ridiculed for their defeat.
