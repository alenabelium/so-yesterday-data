It is that the post-labor economy is simply communism under a new name. This objection is often raised by people who grew up in the Soviet Union or Eastern Europe. And this story matters. If you or your parents lived under that system, any plan to reorganize the economy might sound like the beginning of something terrible. This fear is understandable. When the Soviet Union forcibly united farms into collective farms in the early 1930s, the ensuing famine claimed millions of lives. So when a person with such experience hears the words " public wealth fund," it may seem like the first steps back to the USSR . This reaction is n't really about facts. It is important to be precise in comparisons. Marxism, socialism, and communism are not exactly the same thing. Marxism is the theory of history and economics of Karl Marx. Socialism and communism are the names that 20th-century states gave to systems built on its ideas. These systems had three main features in common. The first was state ownership of the means of production. The state owned farms, factories, and mines. The second was centralized planning. In the Soviet Union, the state planning committee decided what to produce, in what quantities, and how much it would cost. And the third was the treatment of everyone as a financial ward of the state. Your job, your housing, and your income—all came from the government, and there was nowhere else to go. Marx and Engels themselves spoke about this . In the " Communist Manifesto " they wrote that the theory of communists can be reduced to one sentence: " Abolition of private property." The post-labor economy is built around the opposite goal. One of its main design principles is to avoid dependence on government payments.

In 1970, about 8% of Americans' personal income came from government transfers . Today, this figure is around 19%.
