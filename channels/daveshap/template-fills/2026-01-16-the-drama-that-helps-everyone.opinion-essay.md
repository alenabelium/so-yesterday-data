## Thesis

The AI industry's revolving door of talent and public drama is a net positive for humanity, functioning as a modern journeyman system that cross-pollinates expertise and prevents dangerous monopolies.

## Argument

The recent departures from Meera Murati’s Thinking Machines, including her CTO allegedly defecting to OpenAI, exemplify a beneficial pattern often dismissed as toxic drama. While Sam Altman thrives on this attention-seeking spectacle, the underlying mechanism is economic and structural: there is no intellectual property moat in AI. Research is predominantly open-source, and California’s ban on non-compete agreements ensures that top talent can circulate freely between firms like OpenAI, Google DeepMind, and Meta.

This circulation mirrors the historical journeyman system. Just as apprentices traveled between masters to learn diverse techniques, AI researchers move between companies, absorbing different cultural values, research approaches, and intuitions. This cross-pollination raises the tide for everyone. Without this talent flow, we risk a monopoly akin to Nvidia’s 90% dominance in AI accelerators, where lack of competition stifles price reduction and quality improvement. The 'boomerang' effect and talent poaching ensure that expertise is not siloed but distributed, ultimately accelerating progress for humanity rather than serving as a zero-sum game for corporate advantage.

## Counterpoints

- Sam Altman’s OpenAI manufactures and broadcasts drama for attention, creating a 'clown car' perception that distracts from substantive progress.
- Companies may use clawback provisions for vested shares to punish defectors, creating a 'skevy' deterrent despite legal non-compete bans.
- Some competitors like XAI or Grok have limitations (e.g., lower coding scores) that suggest not all 'defectors' bring equal value.
- The 'fake accounts' on social media complicate the verification of who actually defected, adding noise to the signal.
