## Thesis

The talent poaching and 'boomeranging' drama in AI startups is beneficial for humanity because it circulates intellectual capital across companies, raising the tide for everyone.

## Argument

The recent departures from Meera Murati's Thinking Machines, with alleged defections to OpenAI, exemplify a broader, healthy trend in the AI industry. While Sam Altman thrives on this drama for attention, the underlying mechanism is the lack of a true intellectual property moat. Most research is published open-source by giants like Google and Meta, and California’s ban on non-compete agreements legally prevents companies from locking in talent. This creates a 'revolving door' where top engineers and researchers move freely between firms. This circulation functions like the historical journeyman system: practitioners learn different approaches and cultural values from various 'masters' (leaders like Altman, Musk, or Karpathy) before planting those insights in new organizations. This cross-pollination prevents monopolies on expertise and accelerates collective progress. Just as competition in hardware drives down prices and improves quality, this intellectual socialization ensures that humanity benefits from a diverse, well-rounded cohort of AI experts rather than a single 'one-trick pony' dominating the field. The net effect is that talent poaching increases the total intellectual capital of humanity.

## Counterpoints

- Sam Altman loves drama and uses it for attention, which can feel like 'clown car' behavior.
- There are still trade secrets and competitive advantages in specific areas like coding or math.
- Companies may try to claw back vested shares to punish defectors, which looks 'skevy'.
- Fake accounts on social media can distort the perception of who is actually defecting.
