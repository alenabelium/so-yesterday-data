## Thesis

The revolving door of talent between AI labs is a net positive for humanity because it cross-pollinates expertise and prevents monopolies, despite the distracting drama.

## Argument

The recent departures from Mira Murati's Thinking Machines, with alleged defections to OpenAI, exemplify a structural benefit often masked as scandal. The core mechanism is the absence of an intellectual property moat in AI. Research is predominantly open-source, published by universities and giants like Google and Meta, rendering trade secrets less defensible than in other industries. Furthermore, California’s ban on non-compete agreements legally enables this circulation. Talent moves like 'boomerangs' are standard in tech, akin to the historical journeyman system where practitioners learn from multiple masters to gain a well-rounded education. This socialization allows experts to carry cultural values and technical intuitions across organizations, raising the tide for everyone. Without this circulation, we risk a single-vendor monopoly, such as Nvidia’s 90% dominance in AI accelerators, which stifles competition. By contrast, the current ecosystem of OpenAI, XAI, Google DeepMind, and Meta fosters the competition necessary to drive down prices and increase quality, ultimately benefiting humanity more than any single entity's secrecy could.

## Counterpoints

- OpenAI’s drama is driven by Sam Altman’s attention-seeking behavior and desire for status, not just organic talent movement.
- Clawing back vested shares from departing founders looks 'skevy' and punitive, punishing people for leaving to create AGI.
- Some companies, like Anthropic, maintain tighter information control, suggesting that not all labs embrace this open circulation.
- Fake accounts on social media may exaggerate the extent of defections and internal drama at Thinking Machines.
- While Nvidia dominates accelerators, the lack of competition in hardware could still harm humanity despite model-level competition.
