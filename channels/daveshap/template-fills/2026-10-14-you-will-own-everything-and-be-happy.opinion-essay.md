## Thesis

The post-labor economy will end human effort as the bottleneck to wealth, but only a radical redistribution of ownership—not wages—can secure democratic consent for automation.

## Argument

The first wave is cognitive hypersufficiency: machines that read, write, design, and reason 24/7. The second is labor hypersufficiency, when robots bring that intelligence into the physical world. The Empire State Building took 7 million hours of human labor; a million robots working 24/7 can do that in 7 hours. Energy won't hold us back either—every doubling of solar capacity drops panel prices by 20%, and fusion may soon appear. So if work and energy are no longer constraints, what is? Consensus, democracy, legitimacy, and permission. Nothing of this magnitude gets built without the consent of the people who have to live with it. And that consent is already disappearing: local resistance blocked or delayed $130 billion of data center projects in the first three months of 2026 alone. About half of Americans are more concerned than inspired by AI; less than one in ten say the opposite. Seven in ten expect AI to leave the country with fewer jobs. They're afraid of losing income and of a world where a handful own everything. And they're right to be worried. History shows the top reaps first: during Britain's Industrial Revolution, labor productivity rose 46% but real wages only 12%—Engels' pause. So the post-labor economy must rescue us. People don't need promises; they need to see the path to their own success. Wages are the current hand pump—you work, you get paid. But wages have been losing ground for decades: from 60-66 cents of every dollar earned going to workers after WWII, down to below 53 cents by 2026. Imagine 10 cents or 5.
