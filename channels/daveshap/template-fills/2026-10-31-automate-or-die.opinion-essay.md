## Thesis

The automation imperative—the structural pressure on every organization to cut labor costs—is now binding, and it will force mass layoffs regardless of individual intent.

## Argument

The mechanism is structural, not moral. Every organization, public or private, has a mandate for effectiveness. In the public sector, budget pressure forces it; in the private sector, shareholder primacy and market competition do. Milton Friedman's doctrine makes it a fiduciary duty to cut redundant staff. The market punishes virtue: a CEO who refuses automation puts the company at a disadvantage. This works like a ratchet—once one firm automates, competitors must follow. Two more forces amplify it: high wages make labor a target (John Hicks), and the tax code taxes labor at 25% but software at 5%. For decades, three constraints held this imperative back: capability, cost, and risk. AI has now overcome the first two for many office jobs and is starting on the third. So Microsoft's voluntary layoffs and the Dutch government's cuts are not exceptions—they are preemptive moves. The Black Queen hypothesis ensures others will follow. The systemic problem is that individual rationality creates collective ruin: each company cuts costs, but together they erode the earnings their customers live on. Governments are no different—every civil servant laid off is a lost salary in the economy. You cannot shame or vote this away; you must change the incentives themselves.

## Counterpoints

- It's not because these corporations are evil or going bankrupt, nor because of one bad earnings report.
- High salaries do not protect workers—they make them a prime target for automation.
- The automation imperative cannot be removed by shame or vote; only changing incentives works.
