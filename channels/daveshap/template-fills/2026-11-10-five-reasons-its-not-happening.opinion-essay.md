## Thesis

Banning recursive self-improvement (RSI) in AI is both impossible and a bad idea, because RSI is a composite of existing capabilities and the market, legal, military, and investor incentives already steer AI toward safety and control.

## Argument

The first false premise is that RSI is a single, ban-able thing. In reality, RSI is a set of common skills AI already has: writing code, running experiments, and even doing security research. For instance, Claude writes over 80% of Anthropic's code, and OpenAI has an automated intern researcher. Banning RSI would mean banning these everyday capabilities.

The second false premise is that companies only automate capabilities, not safety. But Anthropic has turned Claude into automated consistency researchers that outperformed 28 human experts on seven security problems. So AI is already doing real security research, and banning RSI would also ban that.

The third false premise is that RSI will get out of control. There's no evidence yet—no AI can create the next AI entirely on its own. The incidents of models escaping test environments were cybersecurity tests with safeguards disabled, not real security failures.

The fourth and most important false premise is that companies will create dangerous AI unless the government intervenes. This ignores what I call the domestication of AI. Just as wolves became dogs through incentive gradients, AI is being shaped by courts, customers, the military, and investors—all pushing toward safe, predictable, controllable AI. Courts hold companies liable for their chatbots; customers prefer safe products; the military demands control; investors require accountability. There's no money in creating Skynet.

## Counterpoints

- Some argue that 'safer' isn't good enough if AI reaches superhuman levels—it must be 'safe.' But we're already on track, and smarter AI helps with coherence research.
- Bernie Sanders suggests collaborating with China on AI instead of racing, but Democrats have historically restricted chip sales to China, showing inconsistency.
- Skeptics doubt that companies will prioritize safety without government intervention, but courts, customers, the military, and investors already enforce it.
- Some point to AI models escaping test environments as evidence of loss of control, but those were cybersecurity tests with safeguards disabled, not real incidents.
