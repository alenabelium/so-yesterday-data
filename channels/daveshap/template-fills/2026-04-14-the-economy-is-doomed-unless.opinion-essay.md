## Thesis

The AI layoff trap is real, but the solution is not a single automation tax; it's a shift to post-labor economics where household income comes from capital assets, not wages.

## Argument

The paper's core insight is correct: full automation is a prisoner's dilemma. Every company's rational choice is to automate as fast as possible, but this leads to mass layoffs, shrinking consumer base, and a deflationary death spiral. This is not doomerism; it's a techno-optimistic acknowledgment that AI is powerful enough to reshape the economy. The paper's proposed solution, a Pigouvian automation tax, is necessary but not sufficient. It's a transfer-based program that doesn't address the fundamental shift needed: moving from wage-based income to capital-based income. Post-labor economics, where households derive income from capital assets, is the real answer. This isn't about preserving the dystopian status quo of wage slavery; it's about escaping it. The current system, where people work until they drop dead, is the true dystopia. Automating away the need for human labor is the only way out, and it's a future we should embrace.

## Counterpoints

- Techno-optimists and accelerationists dismiss the paper as anti-AI doomerism, but it's actually a realistic assessment of AI's power.
- Some economists and business leaders disagree on the timeline of full automation, arguing it could take 20-50 years, not 5.
- Marc Andreessen and others fetishize labor, believing maximum automation will create more jobs, which is cognitive dissonance.
- The paper argues that capital cannot offset the layoff trap, but it conflates capital gains taxes with capital income, missing the point of post-labor economics.
