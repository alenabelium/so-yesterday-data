## Thesis

AI FOMO is best managed by recognizing we're early, accepting others will win bigger, and redirecting anxious energy into productive positioning rather than zero-sum competition.

## Argument

The first step is to break FOMO into its four components: money, status, opportunity, and security. Each drives anxiety, but they're not all equal. The money FOMO is the most honest — seeing someone like Pete Stigler build OpenClaw and land a seven-figure job at OpenAI stings, especially when you've been working on cognitive architectures for years. Status is harder to shake because it's wired into our primate brains; we're always aware of who's rising and who's falling. Opportunity and security layer on top, amplifying the fear that we're missing our shot or that we won't be safe.

My first coping strategy is reminding myself we're still very early. When I started my channel with GPT-3 experiments, I had 5,000 subscribers; when ChatGPT launched, I hit 100,000 in nine months. That growth came from being positioned early, and there are still new opportunities like post-labor economics that haven't manifested yet. The second is accepting that some people will win bigger than me — there are gaming channels that pivoted to AI and blew up faster because they had a decade of production quality. I can't control that, so I focus on what I can: developing skills, creating content, and positioning myself well, whether that's financially or by living a low-cost life in a small town.

Third, I stay grounded by looking at history and my own family's struggles.
