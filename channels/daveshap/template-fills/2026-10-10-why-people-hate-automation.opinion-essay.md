## Thesis

The Luddites were not anti-technology but opposed specific uses of machinery that lowered wages, degraded quality, and ignored craft customs, and their core questions about who owns and benefits from new tools remain relevant today.

## Argument

The Luddites knew their machines intimately; many had worked on them for decades. They were not against technology as such but against specific uses that lowered wages, degraded quality, and ignored the customs governing their craft. Their songs demanded full-time work at the old price, established by custom and law—the language of people negotiating who gets the profits, not of people who hate technology. They lost those negotiations, losing with hammers in the dark against an army and a parliament that had already decided whose interests mattered. Every decision to introduce a new machine is also a decision about the fate of people: whose skills depreciate, whose earnings increase, whose voice carries weight. In 1812, employers made this choice alone, with the army behind them. Two centuries later, we are having the same argument again. The machines are different—they write, think, draw, and drive—but the underlying issues have hardly changed. Who owns the new tools? Who decides how to use them? And when productivity increases, who gets the bonus? The Luddites were the first to raise these questions. They should be remembered for what they did, not ridiculed for their defeat.

## Counterpoints

- The common insult that Luddites were afraid of technology and wanted to stop time, which the host refutes by noting they knew their machines well.
- The government's view that machine-breaking was a crime punishable by death, which the host implies was a choice to side with employers.
- The eventual economic outcome that weavers became among the poorest, which the host uses to show the stakes of losing the negotiation.
