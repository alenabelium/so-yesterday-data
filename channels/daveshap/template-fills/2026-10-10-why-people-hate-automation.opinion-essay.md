## Thesis

The Luddites were not anti-technology but opposed specific uses of machinery that lowered wages, degraded quality, and ignored craft customs—questions that still define today's automation debates.

## Argument

The Luddites' rebellion was a targeted response to economic exploitation, not a fear of machines. In 1811, hosiery masters broke wide looms that produced cheap, low-quality 'scraps,' undercutting their wages and craft standards. They left other machines untouched, aiming at employers who violated set rates. This was a negotiation about who benefits from productivity gains, not a rejection of progress.

By 1812, the movement spread to Yorkshire shearers facing shearing machines and Lancashire weavers confronting power looms. Their slogan, 'Enoch made them, and Enoch must break them,' highlighted the irony that the same forge produced both tools of production and destruction. The government's response—military force, spies, and the death penalty—revealed whose interests were prioritized. Lord Byron's speech in Parliament, describing the workers' abject poverty, was ignored.

The Luddites lost, and wages never recovered; within a generation, weavers were among the poorest. But their core questions persist: Who owns new tools? Who decides their use? Who gets the productivity bonus? Today's automation raises the same issues, and the Luddites should be remembered for raising them, not ridiculed for their defeat.
