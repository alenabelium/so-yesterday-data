## Thesis

The Luddites were not anti-technology; they opposed specific uses of machinery that lowered wages, degraded quality, and ignored craft customs, and their core questions about who owns and benefits from new tools remain relevant to modern.

## Argument

The Luddites of 1811-1812 are often dismissed as technophobes, but they were skilled workers who understood their machines intimately. They targeted specific machines used by employers who paid below set rates, not technology in general. Their complaints were about wage cuts, quality degradation, and the violation of craft customs. The government's response—military force, spies, and making machine-breaking a capital offense—showed whose interests mattered. The Luddites lost, and within a generation, weavers went from among the highest-paid to the poorest. Today, automation raises the same questions: who owns the new tools, who decides their use, and who gets the productivity bonus? These are not anti-technology questions but questions about power and distribution of gains.

## Counterpoints

- The common view that Luddites were afraid of technology and wanted to stop time.
- The argument that technological progress inevitably benefits everyone in the long run.
- The idea that resisting automation is futile because it will happen anyway.
