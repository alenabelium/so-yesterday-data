## Thesis

The Luddites were not anti-technology but opposed specific uses of machinery that lowered wages, degraded quality, and ignored craft customs, and their core questions about who owns and benefits from new tools remain unresolved today.

## Argument

The Luddites' rebellion was a negotiation over the terms of technological change, not a rejection of machinery itself. In 1811, skilled hosiery masters broke wide looms that produced cheap, poor-quality 'scraps,' undercutting their wages and craft standards. They targeted specific machines in specific workshops, often those owned by employers paying below set rates, leaving others untouched. This was a disciplined protest against a violation of agreements, not a blind fear of innovation.

The movement spread to Yorkshire shearers facing shearing machines and Lancashire weavers confronting power looms. Their complaints varied but shared a core: the way of life and income that supported it were being destroyed from above. The shearers' slogan, 'Enoch made them, and Enoch must break them,' highlighted the irony that the same forge produced both the machines and the hammers used to destroy them. This was not the language of people who hate technology; it was the language of people negotiating who gets the profits.

The government's response—troops, spies, and the death penalty for machine-breaking—showed whose interests mattered. Lord Byron's speech against the bill, describing the 'abject poverty' he witnessed, was ignored. The Luddites lost their battle, and within a generation, Lancashire weavers went from among the highest-paid workers to among the poorest. Their defeat was not a refutation of their concerns but a demonstration of the power imbalance in technological decisions.

## Counterpoints

- The common insult 'Luddite' implies fear of technology, but true Luddites knew their machines well and opposed specific uses, not technology itself.
- Some argue the Luddites were simply resisting inevitable progress, but the video counters that every technological decision involves choices about who benefits and who loses.
- The government's view that machine-breaking was a capital crime was opposed by Lord Byron, who argued the real issue was the abject poverty caused by these changes.
