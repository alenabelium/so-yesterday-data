## Thesis

The Luddites were not anti-technology; they opposed specific uses of machinery that lowered wages and degraded quality, and their core questions about who owns tools and who gets the productivity bonus remain relevant to modern automation.

## Argument

The Luddites' rebellion was a precise, targeted response to economic exploitation, not a fear of machines. In 1811, hosiery masters broke specific wide looms used to produce cheap, low-quality 'scraps' that undercut their wages and craft standards, while leaving other machines untouched. Their actions were a negotiation about the terms of technological adoption, demanding 'full-time work at the old price, established by custom and law.' The government's brutal response—soldiers, spies, and the death penalty for machine-breaking—revealed that the real conflict was about power and who decides the fate of workers. Byron's speech in Parliament, highlighting the 'abject poverty' he witnessed, underscored the moral stakes, but the state sided with employers. The aftermath, where Lancashire weavers fell from the highest-paid to the poorest, shows the consequences of losing that negotiation. Today, as AI writes, thinks, and drives, the same questions persist: who owns the new tools, who decides their use, and who gets the productivity bonus? The Luddites' legacy is not a cautionary tale against technology but a reminder that every automation decision is a choice about whose interests matter.
