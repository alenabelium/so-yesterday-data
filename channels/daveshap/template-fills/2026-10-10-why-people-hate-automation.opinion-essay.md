## Thesis

The Luddites were not anti-technology; they opposed specific uses of machinery that lowered wages and degraded quality, and their questions about who owns and benefits from new tools remain central to today's automation debates.

## Argument

The Luddites' rebellion was a targeted response to economic exploitation, not a rejection of progress. In 1811, hosiery masters broke wide looms that produced cheap, low-quality 'scraps,' undercutting their wages and craft standards. Their raids were precise—they hit machines owned by employers who paid below set rates, leaving others untouched. This was a negotiation about the terms of work, not a war on innovation.

The same pattern repeated in Yorkshire, where shearers smashed shearing machines that threatened their livelihoods, and in Lancashire, where weavers faced power looms. Their slogan, 'Enoch made them, and Enoch must break them,' highlighted the irony that the same forge produced both the tools and the hammers—a recognition that technology's impact depends on who controls it.

The government's response—military force, spies, and the death penalty for machine-breaking—revealed whose interests mattered. Lord Byron's speech in Parliament condemned the poverty he witnessed, but the bill passed anyway. The Luddites lost, and within a generation, weavers went from among the highest-paid to the poorest workers.

Today, the machines are different—they write, think, draw, and drive—but the underlying questions remain: Who owns the new tools? Who decides how they're used? And when productivity increases, who gets the bonus? The Luddites were the first to raise these questions, and they should be remembered for that, not ridiculed for their defeat.
