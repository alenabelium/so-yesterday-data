## Thesis

The Luddites were not anti-technology; they opposed specific uses of machinery that lowered wages, degraded quality, and ignored craft customs, and their core questions about who owns tools and who gets the productivity bonus remain.

## Argument

The Luddites' rebellion was a negotiation over the terms of technological change, not a rejection of it. In 1811, hosiery masters broke wide looms that produced cheap, poor-quality 'scraps,' undercutting their wages and craft standards. They targeted specific machines in specific workshops, sparing others, and their songs demanded 'full-time work at the old price, established by custom and law.' This was the language of people who understood the machines intimately and objected to how they were deployed, not to the technology itself.

The government's response—troops, spies, and the death penalty for machine-breaking—revealed whose interests mattered. Lord Byron's speech in Parliament, describing the 'abject poverty' he witnessed, was ignored. The Luddites lost, and within a generation, once-highly-paid weavers were among the poorest in England. That outcome was not inevitable; it was a choice made by employers with the army behind them.

Today, the machines are different—they write, think, draw, and drive—but the underlying issues are unchanged. Who owns the new tools? Who decides how they are used? And when productivity increases, who gets the bonus? These are the questions the Luddites first raised, and they should be remembered for asking them, not ridiculed for their defeat.
