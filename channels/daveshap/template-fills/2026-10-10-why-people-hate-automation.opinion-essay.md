## Thesis

The Luddites were not anti-technology but opposed specific uses of machinery that lowered wages, degraded quality, and ignored craft customs—questions that remain relevant today.

## Argument

The Luddites knew their machines well; many had worked on them for decades. They opposed specific uses of technology that lowered wages, degraded quality, and ignored craft customs. Their demands were about who gets the profits, not about hating technology. In 1812, employers made these choices alone, with the army behind them. Today, the same issues arise with AI: who owns the tools, who decides their use, and who gets the productivity bonus. The Luddites raised these questions first and should be remembered for that, not ridiculed for their defeat.

## Counterpoints

- The common objection is that Luddites were simply afraid of technology, but they were skilled workers who understood the machines they broke.
- Another objection is that automation always leads to progress, but the Luddites' concerns about wage depression and quality degradation proved valid in the long run.
