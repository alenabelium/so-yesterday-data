## Thesis

The Luddites were not anti-technology; they opposed specific uses of machinery that lowered wages, degraded quality, and ignored craft customs, and their core questions about who owns and benefits from new tools remain central to today's.

## Argument

The standard story of the Luddites as simple machine-haters is a distortion. The men who broke looms in 1811 were skilled hosiery masters who knew their machines intimately; they targeted specific workshops where employers paid below the set rates, leaving neighboring machines untouched. Their grievance was not with technology itself but with the way it was deployed—wide looms producing cheap 'scraps' that undercut quality and wages, violating the customs that governed their craft. This pattern repeated across Yorkshire and Lancashire, where shearers and weavers faced machines that deskilled their labor and destroyed their livelihoods. The Luddites' slogan, 'Enoch made them, and Enoch must break them,' captures the irony: the same forge produced both the machines and the hammers used to smash them. Their demands, as expressed in their songs, were for 'full-time work at the old price, established by custom and law'—a negotiation over the distribution of gains, not a rejection of progress. The government's response—troops, spies, and the death penalty—shows that the conflict was never about technology; it was about power. Today, as AI writes, thinks, and drives, we face the same questions: who owns the new tools, who decides their use, and who gets the productivity bonus? The Luddites lost their battle, but their questions remain unanswered.

## Counterpoints

- The common insult 'Luddite' implies fear of technology, but the real Luddites were skilled workers who understood their machines for decades.
- Some argue the Luddites were simply resisting inevitable progress, but the transcript shows they opposed specific uses that lowered wages and quality, not technology per se.
- The government's framing of machine-breaking as a capital crime ignored the economic desperation and the employers' unilateral decisions.
