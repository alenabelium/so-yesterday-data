## Thesis

The Luddites were not anti-technology but opposed specific uses of machinery that lowered wages, degraded quality, and ignored craft customs—questions that remain central to today's automation debates.

## Argument

The Luddites' rebellion was a negotiation over the distribution of productivity gains, not a rejection of machines. In 1811, hosiery masters broke wide looms that produced cheap, low-quality "scraps," undercutting their wages and craft standards. They targeted specific machines in specific workshops, often those of employers paying below set rates, leaving others untouched. Their slogan, "Enoch made them, and Enoch must break them," underscored their familiarity with the technology—they opposed its specific application, not the tool itself.

The government's response was force: troops, spies, and a law making machine-breaking a capital crime. Lord Byron's 1812 speech in the House of Lords, comparing the poverty he saw in England to that in Turkey, was ignored. The movement was crushed, and 17 men were hanged. Yet the Luddites' questions—who owns new tools, who decides their use, and who gets the productivity bonus—were never answered. Within a generation, Lancashire weavers went from among the highest-paid workers to the poorest.

Today, the machines are different—they write, think, draw, and drive—but the underlying issues remain. The Luddites are remembered as fools, but they were the first to raise these questions. Their defeat was not a refutation of their concerns but a choice about whose interests mattered. The same choice is being made now, often without the workers who will be affected having a voice.
