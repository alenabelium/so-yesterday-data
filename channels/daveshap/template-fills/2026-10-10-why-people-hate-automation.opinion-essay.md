## Thesis

The Luddites were not anti-technology; they opposed specific uses of machinery that lowered wages, degraded quality, and ignored craft customs, and their core questions about who benefits from automation remain relevant today.

## Argument

The Luddites knew their machines well. Many had worked on them for decades. They were not against technology as such. They opposed specific uses of technology. One that lowered wages, degraded quality, and ignored the customs that governed their craft. Their song called for full-time work at the old price, established by custom and law. This is not the language of people who hate technology. This is the language of people who agree on who gets the profits. They lost these negotiations. They lost the battle with hammers in the dark against the army and parliament, which had already decided whose interests mattered. Every decision to introduce a new machine is also a decision about the fate of people. Whose skills will depreciate, whose earnings will increase, and whose voice will carry weight? In 1812, employers made this choice alone, with the army behind them. This part of the story is usually overlooked. Two centuries later, we are having the same argument again. The cars have become different. They write, think, draw, and drive. But the underlying issues have hardly changed. Who owns the new tools? Who decides how to use them? And when productivity increases, who gets the bonus? The Luddites were the first to raise these questions. They should be remembered for what they did, not ridiculed for their defeat.

## Counterpoints

- The common insult that Luddites were afraid of technology, but they knew their machines well and opposed specific uses, not technology itself.
- The claim that Luddites were anti-progress, but they demanded full-time work at the old price, established by custom and law, showing they were negotiating over profits.
- The assumption that automation inevitably benefits everyone, but history shows that within a generation, Lancashire weavers went from among the highest-paid to among the poorest.
