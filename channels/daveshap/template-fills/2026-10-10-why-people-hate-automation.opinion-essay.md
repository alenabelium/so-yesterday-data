## Thesis

The Luddites were not anti-technology but opposed specific uses of machinery that lowered wages, degraded quality, and ignored craft customs, and their core questions about who owns and benefits from new tools remain unresolved in modern.

## Argument

The Luddites knew their machines well. Many had worked on them for decades. They were not against technology as such; they opposed specific uses that lowered wages, degraded quality, and ignored craft customs. Their songs demanded full-time work at the old price, established by custom and law—language of people negotiating who gets the profits, not of technophobes.

The movement was disciplined and targeted. In 1811, they broke specific machines in workshops of employers who paid below set rates, leaving neighboring machines untouched. In Yorkshire, shearers used hammers from the same forge that made the machines they hated, turning it into a grim slogan: 'Enoch made them, and Enoch must break them.' This was not mindless destruction but a calculated protest against economic displacement.

The government's response was force: thousands of soldiers, spies, and a law making machine-breaking a capital crime. Lord Byron's speech in Parliament, describing the abject poverty he witnessed, was ignored. The trials ended with 17 hangings and penal colonies. The movement died, but the underlying issues did not.

Two centuries later, we have the same argument. The machines now write, think, draw, and drive. But the questions are unchanged: Who owns the new tools? Who decides how to use them? And when productivity increases, who gets the bonus? The Luddites raised these questions first. They should be remembered for what they did, not ridiculed for their defeat.
