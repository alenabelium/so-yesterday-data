## Thesis

The Luddites were not anti-technology; they opposed specific uses of machinery that lowered wages and degraded quality, and their core questions about who owns and benefits from new tools remain unresolved in today's automation debates.

## Argument

The common story of the Luddites as technology-haters is wrong. In 1811-1812, Nottinghamshire hosiery masters broke wide looms not because they feared machines, but because those looms produced cheap 'scraps' that undercut their craft and wages. They targeted specific machines in specific workshops, often those of employers paying below set rates, leaving others untouched. Their demands were about preserving custom and law, not stopping progress. The same pattern repeated in Yorkshire and Lancashire, where shearers and weavers opposed machines that deskilled their trades and destroyed their livelihoods. The government's response—troops, spies, and the death penalty—shows that the conflict was never about technology itself but about power and who decides the terms of change. Today, as AI writes, thinks, and drives, the underlying questions are unchanged: who owns the new tools, who decides their use, and who gets the productivity bonus? The Luddites lost their negotiation because employers and parliament had already chosen whose interests mattered. We face the same choice now, and the outcome is not predetermined.

## Counterpoints

- The common objection that Luddites were simply afraid of technology and wanted to stop time is explicitly rejected; they were skilled workers who knew their machines well.
- Some might argue that technological progress inevitably benefits society in the long run, but the video counters that the Luddites' concerns about wage depression and quality degradation were real and their losses were permanent.
- The video acknowledges the government's view that machine-breaking was a crime punishable by death, but frames this as a choice to side with employers, not a neutral enforcement of law.
