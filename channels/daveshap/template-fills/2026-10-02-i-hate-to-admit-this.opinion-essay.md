## Thesis

AI and the post-industrial economy will force people to abandon 'slave morality' and redefine their identity beyond service and duty.

## Argument

The shift begins with a simple observation: many people define their entire identity by the services they provide to others. That is 'slave morality'—a term from Nietzsche, though he popularized rather than invented it. Slave morality means your moral structure and identity are built on duty, on owing your time and labor to society or a company. The inner 'Panopticon' of society enforces this: it tells you that if you're not being productive at 1:30 on a Wednesday, you're doing something forbidden. It tells you that elderly men playing golf are somehow failing because they're not earning money. But who said you have to be at work? Who said you have to make money? These values come from certain segments of society, but you don't have to accept them.

The reason this matters now is that the old rules were a survival strategy. When you lack material security, you must play by the rules to survive. You need a job to pay for housing, food, and quality things. That creates a real threat of compulsion. But AI, automation, robotics, and the post-industrial economy are changing the game. Imagine a future where everyone has passive income from universal basic income, universal basic capital, citizen dividends, or sovereign wealth funds—say, $60,000–70,000 a year without lifting a finger. At that point, you have material security that no person, organization, or structure can take away. You owe no one anything. The challenge then becomes psychological: we must rid everyone of this internalized 'slave morality' and realize that we don't owe anyone anything. We must free ourselves

## Counterpoints

- The term 'slave morality' is offensive and has not survived well over time.
- Some might say that if you can't control your dog, how can you control AI?
- Nietzsche is generally a philosopher I criticize, but this one idea is sound.
