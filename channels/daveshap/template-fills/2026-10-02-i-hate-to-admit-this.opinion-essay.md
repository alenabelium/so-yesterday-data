## Thesis

AI and the post-industrial economy will force people to abandon 'slave morality'—defining identity through service and duty—and embrace self-determined values.

## Argument

The mechanism is economic. Currently, material insecurity compels people to play by society's rules, internalizing a moral judgment that they owe their time and labor to companies or society. This is Nietzsche's 'slave morality': building your entire moral structure and identity on duty. But as AI, automation, and robotics decouple financial security from work—through mechanisms like universal basic income, universal basic capital, citizen dividends, or sovereign wealth funds—this compulsion disappears. Imagine earning $60,000–70,000 a year without lifting a finger. At that point, you owe no one anything, and no person, organization, or structure can take that away from you. The challenge then becomes psychological: we must rid everyone of this internalized 'slave morality' and realize that we don't owe anyone anything. This is a fundamental shift in ontology—your mental model of the world—because your identity can no longer be defined by the services you provide to others. Instead, you must determine your own values and decide for yourself what makes sense, which is 'master morality.' The transition won't be automatic; it will require a conscious shedding of these duty-based structures. As I observed with the elderly men playing golf on a Wednesday afternoon, even those who have earned their freedom may still feel they're 'missing out' because the Panopticon of society tells them they should be productive. The future demands we break that internalized gaze.

## Counterpoints

- The term 'slave morality' is offensive and has not survived well over time.
- Some might argue that without work, people will lack purpose or structure.
- Critics might say that universal basic income is unrealistic or unaffordable.
