## Thesis

AI and the post-industrial economy will force people to abandon 'slave morality' and adopt self-determined values.

## Argument

The mechanism is economic. Today, material insecurity compels us to play by society's rules: we owe our time to employers, and our identity is built on service and duty. This is what Nietzsche called 'slave morality' — a moral structure where you don't establish your own values, but derive them from serving others. The 'Panopticon' of society internalizes this judgment, making us feel guilty for not being productive, even when we're free to choose otherwise.

But AI, automation, and the post-industrial economy will decouple financial security from work. Imagine receiving $60,000–70,000 a year from universal basic income, citizen dividends, or sovereign wealth funds — without lifting a finger. At that point, you owe no one anything. No person, organization, or structure can take your material security away. The threat of compulsion disappears.

This forces a shift in ontology — your mental model of the world. You must shed the internalized duty and service that defined your identity. You have to realize: 'I don't owe anyone anything.' And you must free yourself from that structure of obligations. This is the master morality: determining your own values, deciding for yourself what makes sense.

I've been an ardent critic of Nietzsche, but this one idea — which he popularized, not invented — is sound. It's not about being offensive; it's about recognizing that the moral framework we've internalized was a survival strategy for a world of scarcity. That world is ending.

## Counterpoints

- The term 'slave morality' is offensive and has not survived well over time.
- People will feel guilty or 'forbidden' for not being productive, even when they have financial security.
- Some critics say: 'Dave can't even handle his dog; what makes you think we can control AI?'
