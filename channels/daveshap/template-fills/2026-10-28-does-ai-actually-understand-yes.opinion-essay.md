## Thesis

AI systems genuinely understand, as demonstrated by Bloom's Taxonomy and Ashby's Law, and denying this is a category error.

## Argument

The dispute hinges on the definition of "understand." Bloom's Taxonomy, developed by educators in 1956, defines understanding by observable behavior, not internal feelings. The revised taxonomy lists six levels: remembering, understanding, applying, analyzing, evaluating, and creating. AI achieves all six: it remembers (scoring 61% on a hard humanities exam), understands (AI tutor doubling learning at Harvard), applies (gold medal at the International Mathematical Olympiad), analyzes (finding 54 of 63 vulnerabilities in DARPA challenge), evaluates (agreeing with experts 80% of the time), and creates (AlphaEvolve breaking a 1969 matrix multiplication record, and Claude agents writing a 100,000-line C compiler).

Ashby's Law of Requisite Variety and the Conant-Ashby theorem state that a good regulator must be a model of its system. AI systems control complex tasks—writing 80% of Anthropic's code, folding laundry with novel manipulators—and even build internal world models, as shown in the Othello experiment. These are objective, verifiable demonstrations of understanding.

Kurtzer's objection reduces to equating understanding with consciousness, a category error. He admits AI has functional capabilities but insists on conscious realization as the criterion. This is solipsistic and unverifiable, as Turing noted. The feeling of understanding is unreliable—the illusion of explanatory depth shows humans overestimate their own understanding.
