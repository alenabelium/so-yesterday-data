## Thesis

Bernie Sanders' proposal to seize 50% equity in AI labs is unconstitutional, economically dangerous, and prone to political capture, rendering it a failed shock tactic rather than viable policy.

## Argument

The proposal is a one-time expropriation of wealth disguised as a sovereign wealth fund, targeting only visible frontier labs like OpenAI and Anthropic while ignoring the actual cash-flowing winners: hyperscalers (Microsoft, Google, Amazon) and infrastructure providers (Nvidia, TSMC). This narrow focus invites capital flight, forcing founders and talent to foreign jurisdictions, and violates the Takings Clause by imposing a stock transfer with inadequate compensation. The moral argument that public commons funded the research is undermined by the mechanism: active government control via board seats and vetoes creates an unresolvable conflict of interest where the state is simultaneously regulator, customer, and owner. History shows that when governments hold controlling stakes, they distort markets and risk capture by successive administrations seeking to raid the fund for political ends, as seen in the failure of central planning models globally.

The viable alternative follows the Santiago Principles: passive index ownership with strict exposure limits (max 10% per security) and no voting rights. Revenue should come from ecosystem-wide levies—compute taxes, data center levies, and windfall taxes on monopoly rents—rather than forced equity transfers. Payouts must be automatic and formulaic, not discretionary. A recommended distribution includes 40% direct AI dividends to citizens, 25% worker transition (though post-labor economics suggests this is a temporary fix), 15% public infrastructure, 10% local grid compensation for data center costs, and 10% reinvestment.
