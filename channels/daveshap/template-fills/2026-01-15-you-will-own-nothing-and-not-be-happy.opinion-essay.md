## Thesis

Technofeudalism is the default trajectory of AI-driven civilization, but a progressive wealth tax funding sovereign wealth funds can redirect this toward technoabundance.

## Argument

Shapiro identifies five attractor states for the AI era: extinction, collapse, technofeudalism, stagnation, and technoabundance. He dismisses extinction and civilizational collapse as unlikely due to robust modern institutions and the resilience of societal memory, though ecological collapse remains a risk. He argues that "nothing ever happens" is a cognitive bias (normalcy bias) contradicted by historical evidence of constant technological disruption.

The core argument is that technofeudalism—a high-tech, low-life cyberpunk dystopia—is the current default trajectory. This stems from neoliberalism’s decades-long systematic undermining of labor power in favor of corporate power. The genre of cyberpunk emerged in the 1980s as a reaction to Japanese economic ascendancy and corporate corruption, codifying anxieties about wealth concentration and the privatization of everything. Today, we are already in the early stages: labor has decoupled from productivity, wages are flat, and elites capture governance through a sclerotic "manager class" and deep state.

To pivot to technoabundance (a Star Trek-like future of post-scarcity), Shapiro proposes a progressive wealth tax as the minimum viable intervention. He argues that strong property rights are not immutable laws of nature but social constructs that can be reformed. With AI obviating traditional intellectual property moats (citing AlphaFold’s free release of 200 million proteins), the focus must shift to physical wealth concentration. ## Counterpoints

- Extinction via AI or bioweapons is unlikely because robust modern institutions and global safety research prevent self-inflicted catastrophe.
- Civilizational collapse is a myth; modern society is too robust and interconnected to truly cease existing like ancient civilizations.
- Stagnation ('nothing ever happens') is a result of normalcy bias, ignoring historical evidence that technology always disrupts labor markets.
- Revolution is not the answer; democratic institutions have 'meta-governance' mechanisms to evolve without the centuries of instability seen in authoritarian regimes.
