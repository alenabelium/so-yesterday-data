## Thesis

Post-labor economics is the coming regime where human labor is no longer a binding constraint on output, and household income must shift from wages to transfers and capital to avoid a deflationary death spiral.

## Argument

The mechanism is the automation imperative. Every microeconomic decision to use AI instead of hiring a human is rational—better, faster, cheaper, safer—and it compounds. The printing press enhanced output by 150-400x; electric lights cut illumination costs by 200-1000x. AI is the latest general-purpose technology, pervasive and constantly improving, so it will subsume cognition, dexterity, strength, and social-emotional labor. The result is a deflationary death spiral: companies automate, wages disappear, household income drops, aggregate demand collapses, and the tax base dries up. The market only requires supply and demand of goods and services—labor is not in the matrix. Household income drives 70-80% of GDP, and wages drive 82% of household income. If wages vanish, the only remaining buckets are transfers and capital. Transfers alone fail because 80-85% of federal revenue comes from income and payroll taxes—the government goes broke too. The solution is a capitalist-friendly mix: sovereign wealth funds like Alaska's Permanent Fund and Norway's, employee ownership trusts (ESOPs/EOTs), and baby bonds. Simulations show median household income could reach $140k, with effective spending power of $300k due to deflation. This is not socialism—it's path-dependent, market-friendly, and politically viable. The exception is embodied human experience: concerts, sports, accountability—the 'throat to choke'—will keep some human demand permanent.

## Counterpoints

- What compels AI companies to share their capital? The economic answer is presently not much, but we're already a demand-constrained economy—billionaires need consumers to keep the circulation going.
- Why isn't this socialism/communism? Because it's designed to be politically viable and capitalist-friendly, using proven policies like the Alaska Permanent Fund (created by a Republican governor) rather than abolishing private property.
- What if automation causes security failures or setbacks? Short-term setbacks are how the hive mind learns—every failure mode becomes a new design spec, and the economic incentive to continue is enormous.
- Could humans revert if infrastructure collapses? Yes, but it would be painful—we're already dependent on technology like air conditioning and Haber-Bosch agriculture, and a full reset would require a civilization-scale event.
