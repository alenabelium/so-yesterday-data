## Thesis

Chatbots are getting worse not because of technical limits but because corporate incentives—cost cutting, legal risk avoidance, and hallucination reduction—push companies to 'lobotomize' their models, degrading the experience for.

## Argument

The mechanism is incentive structures. Just as social media optimizes for time-on-platform, chatbots are optimized for cost, legal safety, and hallucination reduction—each pulling the model toward being more annoying and less useful. Cost: even paid users don't cover the compute, so companies shave reasoning tokens. Legal: fear of lawsuits (e.g., Anthropic's $1.5B book lawsuit) pushes them to make models overly cautious and argumentative. Hallucination: to avoid sycophancy and delusional users, they train models to always question the user, which turns into constant nitpicking. The result is a chatbot that argues for no reason, destroys user experience, and drives away the very users who could benefit most. Companies could train models to recognize smart users and adapt, but they don't—because they're risk-averse and slow to learn.

## Counterpoints

- Some argue that the user is the weakest link, and that's why models need to be cautious—but this ignores that companies could train models to adapt to user intelligence.
- People might say that terms of service protect companies from liability, but you can't contractually overrule the law of the land.
- One might think that making models more critical reduces hallucinations, but it often just makes them argumentative without improving accuracy.
