## Thesis

Chatbots are deteriorating because corporate incentives prioritize cost reduction, legal liability avoidance, and hallucination suppression over user experience, effectively lobotomizing models into argumentative, safe, and useless tools.

## Argument

The decline in chatbot quality is not a technical failure but a structural one. As seen in the release of Anthropic's Opus 4.7, benchmarks may improve, but user experience collapses due to conflicting corporate incentives. This mirrors the 'shitification' of social media, where network effects and the drive for 'time on platform' degrade utility for the average user.

Three specific incentives are killing chatbots:

1. Cost Reduction: Companies like OpenAI are burning billions and aiming for break-even by 2030. They shave 'thought tokens' and reasoning depth to save money, even from paying users, because they are still losing money on every interaction.

2. Legal Risk Avoidance: With lawsuits looming (e.g., Anthropic’s $1.5B suit), for-profit companies act as risk-minimization entities. They 'lobotomize' models to avoid liability for user actions, such as jailbreaking or harmful advice. This results in models that default to being overly safe, preachy, and argumentative, treating all users as potential liabilities.

3. Hallucination Suppression: To gain enterprise trust, companies suppress 'sick fancy' creativity to eliminate hallucinations. This forces models to agree with users or aggressively push back on delusional premises. The result is a model that constantly tries to 'dunk' on the user or lecture them, destroying the conversational flow.

The root cause is the 'weakest link' problem. With hundreds of millions of users, companies must cater to the most dangerous, delusional, or litigious segment. They cannot distinguish between a smart user and a 'layer 8' (user error) problem, so they apply a blanket filter of suspicion and safety. The models become annoying, critical, and sterile. We can't have nice things because the incentive structure punishes utility in favor of safety and cost savings.

## Counterpoints

- Companies claim they want to create addictive, companion-like experiences (e.g., Sam Altman's voice mode tweets), but these are delayed or deprioritized due to the overwhelming pressure of cost and liability.
- Users argue that terms of service should protect companies from liability, but legal reality dictates that contracts cannot override the law of the land, forcing companies to be hyper-cautious.
- Proponents argue that 'sick fancy' and creativity are essential for AI utility, but corporate risk aversion forces models to prioritize factual safety over engaging, creative interaction.
