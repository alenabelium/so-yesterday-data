## Thesis

The gap between what AI can do and what organizations deploy is widening, and laggards are becoming 'dead men walking.'

## Argument

The mechanism is organizational inertia, not technology. OpenClaw and its successors show what's technically possible—autonomous agents running on cron jobs, paying humans via Rent-a-Human—but Fortune 500 companies are at least 18 months away from deploying anything like it. The blockers are real: cybersecurity sees OpenClaw as 'functionally malware,' insurance won't underwrite AI, and legal shuts it down. Even chatbots, three years in, still face CFOs asking 'how do we track that?' and 'what if they just get lazier?' The result is a widening gulf: early adopters pull ahead while most organizations drag their feet, and that decision is being made now. As with Borders ignoring the internet, the companies that survive are the ones whose CEOs issue an edict—'we are going all-in on AI'—and lead the charge. Everyone else is already dead; their bodies just haven't caught up.

## Counterpoints

- Some argue chatbots have no legitimate use case, but that's a 'you problem'—general-purpose technologies evolve through diffusion, not instant ubiquity.
- CFOs worry about tracking ROI and employees getting lazier—but the ROI math is simple: one extra hour of value per month justifies the cost.
- Insurance won't underwrite AI, creating intolerable financial and legal risk—yet shadow IT use persists, forcing a reckoning.
- Skeptics point to VR as a new primitive that flopped—but AI is different because it's already reshaping jobs, with 200,000-300,000 destroyed or avoided in 2025.
