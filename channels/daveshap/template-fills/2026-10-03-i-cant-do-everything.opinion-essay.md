## Thesis

AI's growing capability creates a 'problem of choice' where human time, attention, and executive function—not model intelligence—become the true bottleneck, forcing deliberate minmaxing.

## Argument

The mechanism is psychological: as models like Opus 5.5 and Soul 6.1 get more capable, the option space explodes, triggering analysis paralysis. You can now theoretically do anything—write a book, animate a manga, research endlessly—but each output demands your executive function to verify, use, and enjoy it. That function is not fungible; delegating to bots just accumulates files unless you engage. The bottleneck shifts from quota (most mortals no longer run out) to your metabolic clock and sleep-wake cycle. This mirrors the quarter-life crisis: realizing you can do anything but not everything. The solution is minmaxing—concentrating AI use on three or four high-value goals rather than spreading thin. Quality over quantity is the TL;DR. But this isn't a call to pure productivity; you must consciously align with your values, asking if this project feeds your higher-order values or just indulges a billion ADHD interests. AI can now indulge every whim, which is why some experience 'AI psychosis'—the fire hose of slop overwhelms judgment.

## Counterpoints

- You can delegate to multiple AI agents to parallelize work, but verification and enjoyment still require non-fungible executive function.
- There is merit in just fussing around and challenging yourself, not everything must be productive.
- Some people still run out of quota (e.g., multiple accounts), but most mortals no longer face that limit.
