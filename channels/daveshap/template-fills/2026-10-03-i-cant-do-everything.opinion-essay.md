## Thesis

AI's expanding capability makes human attention and executive function the true bottleneck, forcing a deliberate minmaxing of AI use toward a few high-value goals rather than spreading effort thin.

## Argument

The mechanism is psychological and practical: with models like Opus 5.5, quota is no longer the constraint—the constraint is your own time, mental bandwidth, and the executive function required to verify and enjoy outputs. Delegating to AI agents doesn't solve this because reviewing results still demands your attention; otherwise, you're just accumulating files and wasting tokens. This mirrors the 'quarter life crisis' millennials faced in their 20s, where realizing 'I can do anything' collides with 'I can't do everything.' The lesson from that crisis is to minmax—optimize your AI use around a few specific traits or goals, like a tabletop RPG character, rather than trying to be well-rounded. Quality over quantity is the TLDR: ask whether you'll actually read, publish, or enjoy the output. However, this doesn't mean only doing productive things; you can still fuss around, but you must be conscious of whether that time aligns with your higher-order values. For those with ADHD or a billion interests, AI can indulge every whim, which is why some people experience 'AI psychosis'—the tool amplifies the problem of choice.

## Counterpoints

- You can delegate to AI agents, but verification and enjoyment still require your executive function.
- There's merit in just seeing if you can do something, even if it's not productive.
- You decide your own values, but you must be conscious about how you spend your time.
