## Thesis

As AI models become more capable, the true bottleneck in creative and productive work shifts from the technology to the human's limited time, attention, and executive function, forcing a deliberate 'minmaxing' of AI use.

## Argument

The problem of choice is becoming acute as models like Opus 5.5 and Astra make nearly any project feasible. The mechanism is psychological: more options lead to analysis paralysis and FOMO, but the real constraint is not quota or intelligence—it's the human's metabolic and cognitive limits. Even with delegation, the executive function required to verify and enjoy results is not fungible. This mirrors the quarter-life crisis, where realizing 'you can do anything but not everything' forces a pivot toward minmaxing. The practical implication is a shift from quantity to quality: instead of spreading effort across a sprawl of projects, focus on a few high-value goals that align with your values. This requires conscious value alignment, especially for those with ADHD, to avoid the AI psychosis of indulging every interest. The challenge is not technical but existential: deciding what you actually care about and are willing to invest your finite time in.

## Counterpoints

- There is merit in just seeing if you can do something, challenging yourself, or fussing around, even if it's not productive.
- You can delegate to AI agents, but the bottleneck remains your own executive function and time to enjoy the results.
- Some people still run out of quota, but for most, the bottleneck is not the account but human attention and energy.
