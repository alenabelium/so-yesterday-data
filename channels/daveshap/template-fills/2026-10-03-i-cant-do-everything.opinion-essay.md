## Thesis

AI's growing capability creates a 'problem of choice' where human time, attention, and executive function—not model intelligence—become the true bottleneck, forcing deliberate minmaxing.

## Argument

The mechanism is psychological: as models like Opus 5.5 and Astra get more capable, the option space explodes, triggering analysis paralysis and FOMO. But the real constraint isn't quota or intelligence—it's your metabolic clock and your ability to verify and enjoy the output. Delegation to AI agents doesn't solve this because executive function is non-fungible; you still have to look at the results and decide what to do with them. This mirrors the quarter-life crisis: you can do anything, but you can't do everything. The lesson is to minmax—put all your time, energy, and tokens into three or four high-value goals rather than spreading thin. Otherwise, you get a 'slop fire hose' of half-finished projects. The models will only get smarter, but you remain the bottleneck, so the strategic move is conscious value alignment, not chasing every possibility.

## Counterpoints

- Some people still run out of quota, but most mortals don't anymore.
- There are still plenty of things AI can't do well, and some tasks need a thousand times more quota.
- You can delegate to multiple AI agents, but verification and enjoyment still require your executive function.
