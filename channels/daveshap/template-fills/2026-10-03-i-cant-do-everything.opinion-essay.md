## Thesis

AI's growing capability creates a 'problem of choice' where human time, attention, and executive function—not model intelligence—become the true bottleneck, forcing deliberate minmaxing.

## Argument

The mechanism is the psychological 'problem of choice': as models like Opus 5.5 and Soul 6.1 get more capable, the host finds himself able to pursue any project—books, manga, animation—but mentally bogged down by the options. The bottleneck isn't quota or intelligence; it's the non-fungible executive function required to verify and enjoy results. Delegating to agents just accumulates files unless you engage with them, making it 'pointless token spend.' This mirrors the 'quarter life crisis' millennials faced in their 20s: realizing you can do anything but not everything, limited by time, education, money, and relationships. The host argues for 'minmaxing'—deliberately focusing AI use on a few high-value goals aligned with your values, rather than spreading effort thin. Quality over quantity is the TLDR. He acknowledges the merit of playful experimentation but insists on conscious value alignment: 'Is this how I want to be spending this time?' For those with ADHD or a billion interests, AI can indulge every whim, which he suspects drives 'AI psychosis.' The solution is not to do everything but to choose what you actually care about and will use.

## Counterpoints

- You can delegate to multiple AI agents to handle different projects simultaneously.
- There's merit in just seeing if you can do something, challenging yourself, fussing around.
- You decide your own values, so why not pursue many interests?
- Some people still run out of quota, so the bottleneck might be account limits for them.
