## Thesis

As AI models grow more capable, human time, attention, and executive function—not AI capability—become the true bottlenecks, forcing a deliberate 'minmaxing' of focus onto a few high-value goals.

## Argument

The mechanism is psychological and practical: the 'problem of choice'—more options, more mental bog-down—hits harder as models like Opus 5.5, Astra, and Soul 6.1 make nearly any project feasible. You can delegate execution to agents, but verification and enjoyment demand non-fungible executive function. Accumulating files without engaging them is 'pointless token spend.'

This is a replay of the 'quarter life crisis'—realizing you can do anything but not everything, limited by time, education, money, and relationships. AI and automation are creating a new quarter-life crisis, but the lesson is the same: you have one life, one year per year, no Hermione time-turner. So you must minmax.

The TLDDR: quality over quantity. Focus on what you actually care about—will you read, publish, or enjoy this? But there's merit in just fussing around; you decide your own values. The key is consciousness: is this how you want to spend this day? Does it satisfy higher-order values? For ADHD minds with a billion interests, AI can indulge every whim—which is why some get 'AI psychosis.' The bottleneck is you: intellectually, metabolically, and in your sleep-wake cycle.

## Counterpoints

- You can delegate to agents like Grockbot or ChatGPT, but verification and enjoyment still require non-fungible executive function.
- There are still things AI can't do well, and some tasks would need a thousand times more quota to complete.
- Some people still run out of quota, but most 'mortal humans' no longer do—the bottleneck has shifted.
- There is merit in just seeing if you can do something, even if it's not productive; you decide your own values.
