## Thesis

AI's growing capability creates a 'problem of choice' where human time and executive function, not model intelligence, become the true bottleneck, forcing a deliberate minmaxing of AI use.

## Argument

The mechanism is psychological and practical. As models like Opus 5.5 and Soul 6.1 get more capable, the option space explodes—you can write a book, animate a manga, or research anything. But the bottleneck shifts from quota to your own executive function: verifying results, enjoying them, and integrating them into your life takes non-fungible human time. This is the 'problem of choice'—analysis paralysis from too many options. The quarter-life crisis analogy is apt: you realize you can do anything but not everything, and you must minmax. Minmaxing means deliberately allocating your finite time, energy, and tokens to a few high-value goals that align with your values, rather than spreading thin across a sprawl of projects. Quality over quantity is the TL;DR. The alternative is 'slop'—a fire hose of low-value output that accumulates as pointless token spend unless you actually use it. Even with delegation to multiple agents, the bottleneck remains your attention and verification time. So the strategic response is conscious value alignment: ask if a project satisfies your higher-order values, not just if it's possible. This is especially acute for ADHD minds, where AI can indulge every interest, leading to what some call 'AI psychosis.' The lesson is to be deliberate, not to abandon exploration—there's merit in fussing around—but to ensure your AI use serves your chosen priorities.

## Counterpoints

- You can delegate to multiple AI agents to parallelize work, but verification and enjoyment still require your executive function.
- There's merit in just seeing if you can do something, challenging yourself, or fussing around—not everything must be productive.
- Some people still run out of quota (e.g., multiple accounts), but for most, quota is no longer the bottleneck.
