## Thesis

As AI models grow more capable, the true bottleneck shifts from model intelligence to human time, attention, and executive function, forcing a deliberate 'minmaxing' of AI use toward a few high-value goals.

## Argument

The mechanism is psychological and practical. With models like Opus 5.5, the option space explodes—you can write a book, animate a comic, or research anything. But the bottleneck isn't the account or even intelligence; it's your metabolic clock and your executive function. Delegation doesn't solve it because verifying and enjoying results still requires your attention. This is the 'problem of choice' on steroids, and it's creating a new 'quarter life crisis' for AI users. The lesson from that crisis applies directly: you can do anything, but you can't do everything. So you must minmax—put your time, tokens, and cognitive resources into the three or four projects that give the most bang for your buck. Quality over quantity is the TL;DR. But this isn't a call for pure productivity; you can still fuss around, as long as you're conscious about whether it aligns with your higher-order values. For those with ADHD or a billion interests, AI can become a fire hose of slop, indulging every whim—which is why some people get AI psychosis. The solution is to be deliberate about what you actually care about and will actually use.

## Counterpoints

- You can delegate to multiple AI agents, but verification and enjoyment still require your executive function.
- There's merit in just seeing if you can do something, even if it's not productive—but you must be conscious of your values.
- Some people still run out of quota, but for most, the bottleneck is no longer the account.
