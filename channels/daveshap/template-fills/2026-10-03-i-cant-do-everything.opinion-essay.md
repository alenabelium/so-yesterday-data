## Thesis

AI's growing capability creates a 'problem of choice' where human time, attention, and executive function—not model intelligence—become the true bottleneck, forcing deliberate minmaxing.

## Argument

The bottleneck has shifted. With models like Opus 5.5, quota is no longer the constraint for most people—the constraint is your own executive function. You can delegate the writing, the research, the fanfic, but reviewing and enjoying the output is non-fungible. Accumulating files without engaging them is just pointless token spend. This creates a new kind of FOMO: the fear of not using every option, of falling behind if you don't exploit every capability. But the real question is not 'can I?' but 'should I?'—and that requires conscious value alignment. The quarter-life crisis analogy is apt: you can do anything, but you can't do everything. You have one life, one year per year. So you must minmax around what you actually care about. Quality over quantity. That's the TL;DR. And this isn't a call to only do productive things—there's merit in fussing around. But you have to be conscious about whether the time spent satisfies your higher-order values. For those with ADHD and a billion interests, AI can indulge every whim, which is likely why some people experience AI psychosis. The answer is not to do less, but to choose deliberately.

## Counterpoints

- Some people still run out of quota—like those with multiple accounts—but most mortals don't anymore.
- There are still things AI can't do well, and some projects would need a thousand times more quota.
- There's merit in just seeing if you can do something, even if it's not productive.
