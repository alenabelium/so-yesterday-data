## Thesis

AI's growing capability creates a 'problem of choice' where human time and attention, not model intelligence, become the true bottleneck, forcing deliberate minmaxing.

## Argument

The mechanism is psychological: as models like Opus 5.5 and Soul 6.1 get more capable, the option space explodes, triggering analysis paralysis. You can now imagine writing a book, animating a comic, or building a game—but each project demands your executive function to verify, enjoy, and use the output. That's not fungible; you can't delegate the act of caring. So the bottleneck shifts from quota and intelligence to your metabolism and sleep-wake cycle. This mirrors the quarter-life crisis: you realize you can do anything but not everything. The solution is minmaxing—concentrating your tokens, time, and cognitive resources on three or four high-value goals rather than spreading thin. Quality over quantity becomes the TLDDR. But this isn't a call to pure productivity; you can fuss around for fun, as long as you're conscious about whether it aligns with your higher-order values. For ADHD minds, AI can become a fire hose of slop, indulging every interest whether or not it's a good idea—that's the real danger.

## Counterpoints

- You can delegate work to multiple AI agents, but verification and enjoyment still require your executive function.
- There's merit in just seeing if you can do something, even if it's not productive—play is valid.
- You decide your own values, but you must be conscious about how you spend your time relative to those values.
