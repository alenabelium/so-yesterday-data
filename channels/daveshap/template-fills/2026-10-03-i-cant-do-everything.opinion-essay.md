## Thesis

As AI models become more capable, human time, attention, and executive function—not AI capability—are the true bottlenecks, forcing a deliberate 'minmaxing' of AI use toward a few high-value goals.

## Argument

The mechanism is psychological: the 'problem of choice'—more options lead to analysis paralysis. With models like Opus 5.5, Astra, and Soul 6.1, the host realizes he could do anything, but only has so many hours and mental bandwidth. Delegation to AI agents doesn't solve the bottleneck because verifying and enjoying results requires non-fungible executive function. Accumulating files without using them is 'pointless token spend.' The host draws a parallel to the 'quarter life crisis'—the realization that you can do anything but not everything—and argues AI is creating a similar crisis. The solution is 'minmaxing': deliberately focusing AI use on a few high-value goals rather than spreading effort thin. This means prioritizing quality over quantity and being conscious about whether a project satisfies higher-order values. Even for ADHD individuals with a billion interests, AI's ability to indulge every interest can lead to 'AI psychosis' if not managed. The host acknowledges there's merit in playful experimentation, but insists on conscious value alignment.

## Counterpoints

- You can delegate to AI agents, but the bottleneck remains executive function for verification and enjoyment.
- Some people still run out of quota, but most mortal humans don't anymore.
- There's merit in just seeing if you can do something, but you must be conscious about your values.
