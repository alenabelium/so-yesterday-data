## Thesis

As AI automation concentrates wealth, the key economic question becomes who owns capital, and the answer must be distributed ownership as a birthright, not a privilege.

## Argument

The mechanism is ownership. When machines produce more wealth, profits flow to shareholders, and the richest 10% own almost 90% of corporate shares, while the bottom 50% own just half a percent. Raising wages won't fix this because automation reduces the need for workers; only owners get paid regardless of employment. So the solution isn't higher pay—it's broader ownership. Four models exist. Public wealth funds, like Alaska's Permanent Fund and Norway's sovereign fund, give everyone a share automatically, but citizens lack daily control and payouts depend on politics. Production cooperatives, like Mondragon, give workers democratic control and limit pay ratios, but they're hard to scale in capital-intensive industries and concentrate risk. Employee ownership, via ESOPs and trusts like John Lewis, aligns workers with value creation, but again concentrates risk—losing both job and savings if the company fails. Startup capital, like Connecticut's child bonds, gives young people a stake from birth, but amounts are small and take decades to matter. None alone is sufficient, but together they outline an economy where property ownership is a birthright, not a privilege. If machines do more work, the rest of us are better off having a share in them.

## Counterpoints

- Raising wages won't work because automation reduces the need for workers; only owners get paid regardless of employment.
- Public funds give everyone a share but citizens lack daily control and payouts depend on politics.
- Cooperatives are difficult to establish in industries requiring huge capital and concentrate risk.
- Employee ownership concentrates risk—losing both job and savings if the company fails.
- Startup capital amounts are small, the wait is long, and the program only covers a portion of the population.
