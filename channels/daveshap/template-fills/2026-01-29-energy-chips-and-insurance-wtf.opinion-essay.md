## Thesis

The real barriers to AI acceleration in 2026 are physical and structural—energy, supply chains, and insurance—not philosophical debates about safety.

## Argument

The mechanism is thermodynamic. The average US grid interconnection wait is 7 years, and data center demand is projected to hit 134 gigawatts by 2030—that's the hard stop. High-bandwidth memory has replaced GPUs as the supply chain bottleneck, with Nvidia booking over 50% of all chip-on-wafer-on-substrate capacity. The most surprising friction is insurance: policies with absolute AI exclusions because insurers can't price AI risk, which outweighs public debates about safety and ethics. These are mundane, physical constraints, not philosophical ones. The market will sort out chips and memory, but energy needs state intervention and regulatory help. The 'digestion phase' from 2026 to 2028 is about catching up with reality—grid interconnects, transformers, HBM, and insurance policies—not about bigger models. The future belongs to those who master the physical world: grid permits, fab capacity, energy generation. Focus on atoms, not arguments.

## Counterpoints

- People say AI safety is a barrier, but it's just noise—serious researchers and policy people don't take it seriously anymore.
- Some argue data will run out, but synthetic data is rising and humans learn from less than 1% of the data AI uses.
- Investors worry about ROI and a bubble, but everything is sold out—unmet demand, not speculation.
- Regulation like the EU AI Act is a compliance wall, but it's not slowing AI globally—it's just pushing startups elsewhere.
- Nuclear power is often cited as a solution, but it's too late—small modular reactors won't be ready in time.
