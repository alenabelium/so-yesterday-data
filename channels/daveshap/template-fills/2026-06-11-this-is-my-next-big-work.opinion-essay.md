## Thesis

Rights are only effective when backed by credible threats, necessitating a new realist theory of civic equilibrium and nonviolent general strikes before labor's leverage disappears.

## Argument

The keystone of civilization—human labor—is eroding due to automation and AI, severing the elite dependence on human contribution. Historically, rights were secured through 'double bilateral dependence': a mutual hostage situation where the state offered protection and prosperity in exchange for labor, taxes, and military service. This created a 'credible threat'—the ability to halt production and destroy value—which forced elites to grant concessions when the cost of repression exceeded the cost of concession.

As labor becomes 'redundant biomass,' this leverage vanishes. We face 'mass irrelevance,' not just technofeudalism. Idealist frameworks like Germany’s constitutional dignity clauses lack teeth without enforcement mechanisms. We must engineer a new veto power through nonviolent resistance. History shows nonviolent resistance (53% success rate) is twice as effective as violent revolt (26%). The threshold for success is low: campaigns sustaining 3.5% active participation never fail. We must leverage our current high labor force participation to demand structural changes—tax resistance, general strikes, and dispersed ownership stakes—before automation makes us ungovernable and economically useless.

## Counterpoints

- Violent resistance is viable because Americans have guns.
- Labor will never fully disappear, so leverage will remain.
- Elites will voluntarily grant universal basic capital and property rights expansions.
