## Thesis

Automation eliminates most jobs not through dramatic layoffs but through silent attrition—vacancies that are never filled—and this invisible process is the most worrisome because it neutralizes our early warning systems.

## Argument

The mechanism is attrition. A company with 1,000 employees loses about 10% each year to retirement, moves, or career changes. If software absorbs the routine work and the firm simply stops replacing those who leave, no one is fired and no headlines appear. After one year, headcount drops to 900; after two, 810; after three, 729—a 27% reduction without a single layoff. The press release cites efficiency and natural turnover, and the local newspaper stays silent. Hundreds of potential hires look elsewhere, often unaware the door has closed. Multiply that across the entire economy, and you get a structural shift that never triggers a public debate.

This pattern is invisible to conventional metrics. The unemployment rate can stay low while the quality of opportunities declines. Mass layoffs make headlines, but the main event is the hiring that never happens. Economists Acemoglu and Restrepo frame this as a race between the displacement effect—machines taking over tasks—and the recovery effect—new tasks that humans excel at. For four decades after WWII, the two were balanced. Their research suggests displacement has been outpacing recovery in recent decades. The ATM story shows the lag: tellers survived the 1980s and 1990s because cheaper branches meant more branches, but smartphones then triggered a 30-year gradual decline. Telephone operators faced a similar fate, with displaced workers suffering lasting earnings losses and younger cohorts pushed into low-paying jobs.

## Counterpoints

- The recovery effect: new jobs always appear, but no one can promise in advance what they will be, where, or for whom—they may require different skills and pay.
- The ATM example: tellers didn't disappear immediately, suggesting automation can be benign—but smartphones later triggered a 30-year decline.
- The unemployment rate stays low, so some argue there's no problem—but the quality of opportunities can decline while the rate remains stable.
