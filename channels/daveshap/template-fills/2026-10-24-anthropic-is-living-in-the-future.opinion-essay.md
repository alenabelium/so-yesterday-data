## Thesis

Anthropic's Claude code team has moved to a machine-checking-machine code review process, and this model will become the standard that every company must adopt to stay competitive.

## Argument

The mechanism is the shift in the scarce resource. Anthropic's team realized that human attention, not code, is the bottleneck. So they redesigned their review process to optimize for automated checks, with humans only stepping in for high-risk decisions. The result: pull requests merged more than doubled, while the cancellation rate dropped by over 40%. This isn't a new idea. The principle of machines checking machines is as old as double-entry bookkeeping, error-correcting codes, and TCP checksums. Claude Shannon formalized it in 1948 with information theory. The AI community is an epistemic bubble, unaware that engineering and finance have used this for centuries. The future is clear: agents will have their own org chart, with agents doing work, supervising, managing, coordinating, and auditing. We already see security agents monitoring networks and scanning code. The implications for your company are threefold. First, coding's value moves to defining what to build and designing checks. Second, efficiency requires building a safety net before opening the fire hose. Third, the org chart flattens, with humans controlling purpose, trust, and responsibility. The real climax: a fully automated technical stack leads to a fully automated business stack, with more checks than ever, all automatic. The differentiator is whether you intentionally redesign your process around this model.

## Counterpoints

- Anthropic is grading its own homework; no independent verification of the numbers.
- Anthropic spends far more on tokens than most companies would want to.
- The code review product only works with GitHub.
- GitHub now allows counting Copilot approvals toward required approvals, which conflicts with Anthropic's rule that agent approval never counts as human approval.
- Most companies lack the ability to recover from failed deployments quickly, so they can't afford to reduce human review.
