## Thesis

The singularity will feel boring because human neurology normalizes rapid change so quickly that transformative shifts in AI, energy, and robotics fade into mundane infrastructure rather than dramatic events.

## Argument

The host argues that the singularity is already here but feels unremarkable due to the 'jagged frontier' of AI capabilities and our brain's rapid self-model updates. We are witnessing the 'fire hose of code,' where AI writes software faster than human teams, yet this doesn't alter daily life because we are merely building the substrate for AI dominance. In hindsight, all computational history is just the lead-up to running AI, with 99% of future cycles dedicated to it.

The physical changes are equally invisible. Robotics will dominate logistics and agriculture, not as visible humanoid heroes, but as invisible warehouse staff. Energy production will shift entirely to power AI workloads, making residential power trivially cheap, which triggers Siobhan's paradox: cheaper energy leads to more consumption (e.g., home greenhouses) rather than visible spectacle. Space infrastructure, like Dyson swarms, will offload entropy to space, remaining unseen by the public.

Crucially, human neurology prevents us from feeling the magnitude of these changes. Our 'normalcy bias' updates our mental model within weeks, making exponential progress feel linear and boring. We adapt to better batteries, faster code, and personalized medicine as the new normal. Furthermore, physics may be running out of surprises. We might be past the golden age of fundamental discoveries, facing a sigmoid curve where intelligence cannot overcome physical limits on energy, distance, and time. ## Counterpoints

- Sam Altman's 'gentle singularity' concept, which suggests a slow, non-disruptive transition rather than a sudden takeoff.
- The possibility that thermodynamic or photonic computing could drastically increase efficiency, altering the energy consumption narrative.
- The historical precedent of physics enabling 'impossible' leaps, such as the exponential increase in global bit rate over the last 200 years.
- The potential for mind uploading or faster-than-light travel, though the host dismisses these as currently physically impossible.
- The view that AI is merely a tool for writing more code, rather than a fundamental shift in the purpose of computation.
