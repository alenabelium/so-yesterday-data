## Thesis

AI accelerates creative burnout by removing natural rate-limiters and satisfying novelty too quickly, leaving humans as the sole bottleneck in a culture obsessed with maximum productivity.

## Argument

The central thesis is that AI doesn't just change how we create; it changes the *pace* at which we exhaust our creative wells. Historically, creative output was gated by friction: learning instruments, scheduling bands, or waiting for approval processes. These natural stopping points allowed the 'well' of inspiration to refill through lived experience. AI removes these gates. When you can generate four albums in weeks via Suno, you drain a lifetime of inspiration instantly, leading to a year-and-a-half of creative silence.

This phenomenon extends to productivity. In white-collar work, AI makes the human the bottleneck. Without structural forbiddances like budget approvals or colleague availability, the default operating system of 'hustle culture' takes over. We burn out because we are no longer limited by external constraints but by our own internal drive to maximize output.

Furthermore, AI kills the joy of consumption by eliminating surprise. When you generate exactly what you want, whether it's a choose-your-own-adventure story or bespoke music, the novelty wears off rapidly. The thrill of creative content lies in the unexpected—the high-entropy input from the outside world. AI regresses to the mean, satisfying curiosity but starving the need for genuine novelty. As seen in declining AI chatbot usage, the initial excitement fades when the tool becomes too predictable. We need external friction and surprise to sustain long-term engagement, not just infinite, frictionless generation.

## Counterpoints

- Commercial artists apply skills differently than personal expression, so they may not experience this burnout.
- The author acknowledges this might be specific to his ADHD brain and high need for novelty.
- New form factors like agents may restart the novelty cycle, similar to the iPhone replacing Blackberries.
- Derivative iteration is valued in some cultures (e.g., Japanese anime) where refinement matters more than novelty.
