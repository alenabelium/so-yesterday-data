## Thesis

The backlash against data centers is not about environmental harm but about inequality, as extractive infrastructure funnels wealth to Silicon Valley while providing minimal local jobs and undermining democratic governance.

## Argument

The current data center buildout represents the most aggressive capital infrastructure expansion since the 1880s railroad boom, yet the opposition is fundamentally about who owns the future. While environmental impacts like water usage and carbon footprint are often exaggerated—agriculture consumes far more water, and hyperscalers drive renewable energy adoption—the real grievance is economic extraction. A billion-dollar data center creates only 40-100 permanent jobs compared to a Walmart’s 300, decoupling massive investment from local employment. Furthermore, state subsidies and tax abatements socialize costs while privatizing gains; for instance, Virginia lost $1.6 billion in tax revenue while residents saw no benefit, though local counties gained. This creates a governance crisis where shell companies and critical infrastructure designations bypass local zoning, allowing tech giants to operate with impunity. The opposition is structurally identical to NIMBYism but substantively about inequality: communities view these facilities as modern robber baron enterprises that extract value and return it to Wall Street, leaving locals with grid strain and noise but little prosperity. The solution requires ending tax abatements, mandating grid independence, and ensuring citizen dividends so communities share in the digital wealth they host.

## Counterpoints

- Data centers consume a tiny fraction of water compared to agriculture and golf courses.
- Hyperscalers are the world's largest buyers of green power, driving decarbonization.
- AI optimization saves more carbon than training models emit.
- Local residents may see lower property taxes due to data center tax revenues.
- Construction jobs provide a temporary economic boost during the build phase.
