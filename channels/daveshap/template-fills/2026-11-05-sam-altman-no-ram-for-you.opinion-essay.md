## Thesis

Sam Altman's claim that personal computers waste scarce DRAM is economically illiterate and self-serving; the market's price signals, not executive decrees, should decide who gets scarce memory.

## Argument

The mechanism is the price signal. When DRAM becomes scarce, its price rises, telling everyone to consume less and producers to make more. Altman dismisses this as 'bad' because memory makers overbuilt in the past, but that's the market working—profits are the signal, new fabs are the response. The real problem is time, not failure. Even in a future with superintelligence and billions of robots, there will always be scarcity, so distribution must be decided by something. Altman's answer is authoritarian: he'd order RAM out of MacBooks and into data centers, which conveniently serve his own $1.4 trillion data center buildout. But the market is a sorting algorithm that uses money as the signal—amoral, not immoral, and fairer than any single planner. The Soviet Union couldn't track 24 million goods; no CEO can either. And the market's amorality is its strength: it lets everyone's willingness to pay speak, rather than one person's morality. So who gets the RAM? The price decides, not Sam Altman.

## Counterpoints

- Altman's expected-value argument: putting all GPUs and memory in data centers for cancer research might have higher expected value than gaming consoles or YouTube.
- The market's cure for high prices is high prices, but Altman says it's 'bad'—memory makers have been burned by past overbuilding and are slow to respond.
- Markets can be manipulated, as shown by the 1999-2002 DRAM price-fixing scandal, so they're not automatically fair.
