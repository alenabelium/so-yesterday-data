## TL;DR

The Fisher-Clark model splits the economy into extractive, transformational, and service sectors, but a fourth sector is emerging where value is created with minimal human labor. Past attempts to define a fourth sector failed by categorizing occupations instead of value creation. This automated sector could dominate GDP within decades, shifting economic power from labor to asset ownership.

## The argument

### Define the Fourth Sector by Value Creation
- **Anchor Timestamps**: ['00:07:56']
- **Claim**: The real fourth sector is value created with minimal human involvement—automation, AI, and robots. Unlike the first three sectors, which all required human creators, this sector's defining trait is non-human workers. This definition draws a clear line around services as the last human sector.
- **Role**: definition

### Past Fourth and Fifth Sectors Were Categorical Errors
- **Anchor Timestamps**: ['00:05:31']
- **Claim**: Proposals for a fourth sector (trade/finance or intellectual work) and a fifth (healthcare/education or managers) merely subdivided the service sector by occupation and rank. They classified jobs instead of how value is created, repeating the same categorical error. Even newer ideas like the attention or relational economy remain service-sector activities.
- **Role**: counter

### The Doxa of Labor as a Necessary Input
- **Anchor Timestamps**: ['00:06:43']
- **Claim**: Economists' assumption that human labor is always part of the economy is 'doxa'—so basic it goes unquestioned. The textbook equation (output = f(capital, labor)) collapses when labor is zero, but science fiction and automation already imagine economies without significant human intervention. Dropping this assumption reveals the fourth sector.
- **Role**: evidence

### Measuring the Shift: Labor Share and New Metrics
- **Anchor Timestamps**: ['00:10:54']
- **Claim**: GDP doesn't distinguish human vs. machine value, but the labor share of national income fell to 52.8% in Q2, the lowest since 1947. As the automated sector grows, GDP alone won't reflect human well-being. Two new metrics are proposed: household income from asset ownership vs. wages/benefits, and income from publicly owned common wealth.
- **Role**: synthesis

## Evidence and caveats

The speaker uses historical pie charts: in 1381 England, extraction was ~45%, transformation ~30%, services ~26%; by 1851, services were already the largest by value (~50%) despite transportation being the largest employer. Today, US extraction is ~1%, transformation <20%, services ~80%. The speaker notes sectors never disappear—they only shrink as fractions. Caveats: the speaker acknowledges economists dislike science fiction, and the 75-90% automated share within decades is a projection, not a certainty. The speaker also hedges that some believe it could approach 99% in the long run, which he agrees with eventually.
