## Thesis

Universal Basic Income alone is insufficient because it only expands the transfer pipe, leaving households vulnerable to automation-driven job loss; policy must instead expand capital ownership.

## Argument

The vulnerability isn't about income levels—it's about income structure. The Rivers family earns $78,000, but 96.7% of it flows through the salary pipe. When Sam's pharmacy installs an automated dispensing system, their income collapses by 31%, and the capital pipe—just $500 a year—covers only two days of expenses. The Fed's $400 emergency question shows this isn't an edge case: four in ten households can't cover an unexpected expense without borrowing or selling something. For them, security isn't net worth; it's the distance between paychecks.

Contrast that with Margaret and Tom, a retired couple earning $70,000 from Social Security and a $800,000 portfolio. They're not rich—they're diversified. A new machine can't touch their income because it rests on two pillars, neither of which is work. That's the structural difference that matters.

So when evaluating any policy, ask three questions: What channel does it expand? Who gets access? And will it work in a world where fewer people are paid for their work? Raising the minimum wage expands the salary pipe for those who still have jobs. Child support expands transfers. But a sovereign wealth fund, an employee stock ownership plan, or a children's capital program expands the capital pipe—the only one that grows by itself through compound interest while you sleep.

UBI, by design, only expands the transfer pipe. It keeps families dependent on a channel that doesn't compound and doesn't build resilience. The real fix is to give households a stake in the machines that replace them—not just a check to soften the blow.
