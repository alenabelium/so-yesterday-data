## Thesis

Universal Basic Income alone cannot protect households whose income is almost entirely labor-based; policy must expand capital ownership channels instead.

## Argument

The mechanism is structural, not just financial. The Rivers family earns $78,000 a year, but 96.7% of it comes from salaries. When Sam's pharmacy installs an automated dispensing system, their income drops 31% overnight, and their $500 annual capital income covers only two days of expenses. Their $8,000 savings evaporate in five weeks. This is the reality for most working-age families: one pipe full, two nearly dry.

Contrast this with Margaret and Tom, a retired couple. Their $70,000 income splits between Social Security and a $800,000 portfolio. No machine can fire them. They are not rich—they are diversified. The difference is not wealth; it's structure.

So when evaluating any policy, ask: which channel does it expand? Raising the minimum wage helps only those still employed. UBI expands transfers, but transfers are a lifeline, not a foundation. The only channel that grows by itself is capital—through compound interest. A sovereign wealth fund, employee stock ownership, or a children's capital program expands that third pipe.

The arithmetic is unforgiving: to replace Sam's $23,400 salary at 4% return, the Rivers need $585,000 in assets. Most families will never get there by saving alone. That's why ownership is concentrated. The policy goal must be to give every household a stake in capital, not just a monthly check.
