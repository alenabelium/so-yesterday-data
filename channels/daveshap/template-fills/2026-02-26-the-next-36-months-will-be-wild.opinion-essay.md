## Thesis

The next 36 months will be WILD as recursive self-improvement collapses the timeline for AGI, creating an automation cliff defined by vanishing entry-level jobs rather than mass layoffs.

## Argument

The convergence of predictions from Altman, Huang, and Amodei places AGI in the 2027-2028 window, driven by a super-exponential doubling of machine autonomy that is shortening to 90-day cycles. Recursive self-improvement requires five components: algorithmic research, data generation, code writing, model training, and model evaluation. The first three are largely solved, with AI matching gold-level IMO performance and generating sufficient synthetic data. The remaining two—training and evaluation—are the current bottlenecks, but they are infrastructure and process problems, not fundamental scientific barriers.

The industry is locked in a "terminal race condition" with a point of no return; sunk costs in data centers and chips make pausing fatal. While energy demand is rising faster than production (the "alligator jaws"), solutions like microgrids and SMRs are being deployed. The real societal impact is not immediate mass firing but the "vanishing ladder." GDP growth is decoupling from job creation, with 2025 showing dramatic evidence of this. The automation cliff is characterized by ghost jobs—positions that are never created—leading to record-high job latency for new graduates. As AI becomes economically substitutable, the threshold for human labor shifts from augmentation to mandatory replacement, creating a regime of jobless growth.

## Counterpoints

- The chip bottleneck is solved; high-bandwidth memory is the next constraint but is being addressed by massive market investment.
- AGI is defined by impact, not consciousness; waiting for a 'brain wave' of recognition is a linguistic trap.
- The primary job impact is the vanishing of entry-level positions, not mass layoffs of existing workers.
- Anti-data center sentiment is bipartisan virtue signaling that ignores the economic necessity of AI for competitiveness.
