## Thesis

The vast majority of modern jobs are based on derived demand and will disappear because essential human labor constitutes only ~5.5% of employment, constrained by finite human attention.

## Argument

[00:01:41] Labor’s share of national income has collapsed from ~63% in the 1960s to below 53%, signaling a post-labor economy where capital displaces labor. [00:02:41] Most work is "derived demand"—human input is incidental to the outcome (e.g., building a house). When machines deliver better, faster, cheaper, and safer results, derived demand vanishes. [00:07:05] The only remaining moat is "essential demand," which I define as human involvement being intrinsically valued or required. This breaks into four categories: presence (embodied interaction), provenance (human-made origin), liability (accountability), and affinity (specific identity). [00:22:21] Analysis of 831 BLS job categories reveals that only ~5.5% of current employment relies on essential demand, with a high-water mark of just 15%. [00:26:20] Critics argue luxury spending will absorb displaced workers, but this ignores physical limits. [00:29:03] Even if wealth increases, human attention is the bottleneck; we only have ~5-8.5 hours of leisure daily. [00:31:16] Essential jobs follow a power law: local presence (massage) is capped by time, while global affinity (creators) concentrates revenue in the top 1%. [00:33:29] The median creator earns ~$300/month, and much leisure will remain unpaid. Thus, structural bottlenecks prevent new human-centric roles from absorbing the displaced workforce.

## Counterpoints

- Luxury spending on experiences and human provenance will grow as economies get wealthier.
- New jobs will emerge to replace automated tasks, preserving overall employment levels.
- Wealth redistribution could allow everyone to afford high-touch human services like massage therapy.
