## Thesis

AI and robotics could destroy capitalism, but only if four conditions are met, and the transition hinges on replacing wages with sovereign wealth fund dividends.

## Argument

The mechanism is structural. For capitalism to end, four conditions must align: marginal costs near zero, wage labor redundant, private ownership unstable, and market coordination pointless. If all four hold, capitalism as an institution could end. The current loop—production to wages to consumption to profit—breaks when automation eliminates wages, severing demand. The counterargument is the utility of friction: owners serve as risk sinks, decision compressors, and liability scapegoats. But if AI CEOs outperform humans and collective ownership of a hyperscaler becomes possible, these functions vanish. Two litmus tests: an AI CEO that ends managerial scarcity, and a collectively owned hyperscaler that ends billionaire capital concentration. If both pass, capitalism's days are numbered. However, physics walls—latency and capital concentration—currently prevent decentralized training. Liability walls are weaker; algorithms can be regulated. The real bottleneck shifts to compute, energy, and permission. States will optimize for automation capacity, not labor, leading to a posthuman geopolitical landscape. Without leverage, citizens lose rights and relevance. The solution is sovereign equity: tax rents on compute and energy to capitalize sovereign wealth funds, paying citizens dividends regardless of work. This preserves demand and GDP, creating a bifurcated market where basic goods deflate and positional goods inflate. The final choice: automation without distribution leads to neo-bourgeois secession; automation with distribution lets wages die so the market can live.

## Counterpoints

- Technology always creates new jobs; human labor won't become redundant.
- Owners provide risk absorption, decision compression, and liability scapegoats.
- Decentralized training is physically impossible due to latency and capital concentration.
- You can't jail an algorithm, so humans must remain liable.
- Collective decision-making is worse than concentrated authority.
