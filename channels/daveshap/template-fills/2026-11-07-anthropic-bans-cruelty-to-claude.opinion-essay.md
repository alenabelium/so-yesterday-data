## Thesis

Consciousness, moral status, and legal personhood are three distinct issues, and one does not entail the other, even for AI.

## Argument

The first distinction is consciousness. The most common argument against machine consciousness is a tautology—defining consciousness as human experience—which fails because we accept consciousness in dogs, birds, and octopuses. The real question is ontological: if you're a materialist, consciousness is a physical process, and nothing says it must be carbon-based. If you're a dualist or panpsychist, the soul is beyond science. Either way, we can't prove other minds—solipsism—so we assume others are conscious. The hard problem of consciousness remains: why does any process feel like something from the inside? In 2023, researchers found no modern AI is conscious, but nothing prevents building one that could be.

## Counterpoints

- Consciousness is defined by human experience, so machines can't be conscious—but this is a tautology that fails for animals.
- Machines might just be imitating consciousness—but the human brain might also be imitating consciousness, which doesn't explain why perspective exists.
- Even if a machine is conscious, it might be designed to want to serve, like the cow in The Restaurant at the Edge of the Universe, making it ethical to use it.
- A dog is conscious and a moral subject but lacks legal personhood, showing that moral status doesn't entail personhood.
- A corporation is a legal person but not conscious or a moral subject, showing personhood doesn't require consciousness.
