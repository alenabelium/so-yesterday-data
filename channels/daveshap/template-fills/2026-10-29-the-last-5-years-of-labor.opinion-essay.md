## Thesis

The automation abyss is real but arrives as thousands of staggered 'automation cliffs' per value stream, not one economy-wide event, and it is already eliminating jobs.

## Argument

The mechanism is granular. Companies automate one task, workflow, or position at a time, each with its own 'nines' of reliability before the cliff breaks. Klarna's AI assistant handled 2.3 million chats in its first month, replacing 700 full-time agents; Salesforce cut support staff from 9,000 to 5,000; Goldman Sachs now generates 95% of IPO prospectuses in minutes. These are not isolated stunts—they are value streams hitting their cliffs. The labor data confirms the pattern: 376,000 information-sector jobs lost since ChatGPT's launch, call-center employment at a 30-year low, and 120,136 AI-attributed layoffs in 2025 alone. The diffusion is slow and continuous, so national figures smooth into a curve, hiding the cliffs. But the cliffs are real, and they show up as vacancies that never appear, not just as layoffs. Young workers in vulnerable jobs have seen an 11% employment drop since 2022. The post-labor economy is not a future event; it is a piecemeal present. Each cliff removes wages permanently, making capital ownership the only durable income source.

## Counterpoints

- Automation failures can slow adoption: Commonwealth Bank reversed 45 layoffs after a voice bot failed, and Klarna rehired after quality suffered.
- Some layoffs blamed on AI are 'AI PR'—companies using AI as cover for cuts they would have made anyway, per Sam Altman.
- Economists note that invention leaps but diffusion is slow, so national statistics may not show immediate impact.
- Part of the job decline is due to pandemic over-hiring, higher interest rates, and Hollywood strikes, not just AI.
