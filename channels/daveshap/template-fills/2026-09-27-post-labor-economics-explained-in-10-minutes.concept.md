## TL;DR

The social contract of trading time for money is collapsing as machines outperform humans on speed, cost, and safety. Wealth concentration necessitates a shift from labor-based income to asset ownership and political leverage. Without redefining power independent of employment, society risks authoritarian bargains.

## The argument

### The Breaking Deal
- **Anchor Timestamps**: ['00:00:19']
- **Claim**: Post-labor economics defines a new test for every task: better, faster, cheaper, safer. When machines win on all four, human labor must justify a premium that becomes increasingly hard to sustain.
- **Role**: definition

### Derived vs Essential Demand
- **Anchor Timestamps**: ['00:03:03']
- **Claim**: Demand splits into derived (you want the result, not the person) and essential (the person is part of the purchase). While essential demand exists for empathy or accountability, it is narrow and won't employ everyone.
- **Role**: definition

### The Ownership Gap
- **Anchor Timestamps**: ['00:05:36']
- **Claim**: Household income comes from doing, receiving, and owning. With productivity decoupling from pay since 1979, transfers create dependence on the state, making ownership the only viable link to wealth.
- **Role**: evidence

### Leverage Without Labor
- **Anchor Timestamps**: ['00:08:36']
- **Claim**: Power requires the ability to impose cost. As states stop needing people's labor (like petrostates), citizens must build leverage independent of jobs to maintain control over their economic destiny.
- **Role**: synthesis

## Evidence and caveats

Historical data shows farm jobs dropped from 40% in 1900 to <2% by 2000, yet work continued. Productivity grew ~94% since 1979 while typical pay rose only ~33%. The speaker notes that essential demand for people is 'real' but 'narrow,' implying it cannot serve as the primary economic engine for the masses. A key caveat is that comfort provided by the state is not control; true power requires the ability to say no and make it stick.
