## Thesis

The $1.5 trillion AI valuation is an unsustainable bubble driven by a debt chain that outpaces reality, while deployment failures reveal systemic ethical and organizational risks.

## Argument

The industry’s financial structure is a fragile pyramid of debt. OpenAI demands a $1.5T valuation despite investors offering only $1.2T, planning to spend $856B on infrastructure against projected $350B revenue by 2030. This capital flows through layers: bond investors lend to SoftBank, which lends to OpenAI, which borrows from Oracle and CoreWeave for GPUs, with Nvidia guaranteeing asset value. If AI monetization fails, this chain collapses. Financial markets are already repricing risk; Oracle’s Project Jupiter loans trade at 89-91 cents on the dollar, and AI corporate bonds trade at wider spreads than Treasuries. This isn't just a tech story—it threatens ordinary savings via S&P 500 concentration.

Technologically, the gap between hype and reality is stark. While productivity gains exist (15-50% in specific tasks), 95% of enterprise AI pilots fail to deliver measurable business impact. Developers using AI tools were found to be 19% slower in trials. The industry relies on a "dynamo" analogy: early adopters haven't reorganized workflows for true gains yet. Small models handle routine tasks, but large models remain essential for complex reasoning, preventing a cost collapse.

Ethically, the technology works too well for bad actors. Flock Safety’s surveillance led to police harassment and contract cancellations. In Iran, AI-generated strike coordinates contributed to an elementary school bombing due to compressed human decision-making time. The failure isn't the AI; it's the lack of oversight and organizational maturity.

## Counterpoints

- AI has real productivity gains (15-50% in writing/support), but only 20% of firms see revenue growth.
- Small models (1-3B params) maintain 85-95% accuracy for domain tasks, reducing need for expensive large models.
- The bubble is 'localized,' not a total burst; capital still flows, but at higher costs and with stricter terms.
