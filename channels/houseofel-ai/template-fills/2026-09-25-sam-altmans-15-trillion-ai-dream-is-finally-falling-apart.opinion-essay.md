## Thesis

The $1.5 trillion AI valuation is an unsustainable bubble built on a fragile debt chain that will cascade into ordinary investors' savings when revenue fails to match the $856 billion infrastructure spend.

## Argument

The industry presents a stark contradiction: while AI delivers genuine productivity gains in specific domains like software development and customer support, it has failed to translate this efficiency into broad corporate revenue growth. A 2025 MIT study found that nearly 95% of enterprise generative AI pilots fail to achieve measurable business impact, and experienced developers using AI coding tools were actually 19% slower in controlled trials. The technology works, but organizational integration lags behind the hype.

The core danger lies in the financial structure underpinning this growth. OpenAI’s push for a $1.5 trillion valuation against a $300 billion gap between investor offers and company demands highlights a disconnect between price and reality. To fund an expected $856 billion in computing and infrastructure costs from 2026 to 2030, the industry relies on layered debt. Bond investors lend to SoftBank, which lends to OpenAI, which borrows from infrastructure firms like Oracle and CoreWeave, who borrow to buy GPUs. Nvidia acts as a guarantor for this collateral, creating a circular dependency where risk is merely shifted rather than eliminated.

This fragility is already visible in the market. Loans for Oracle’s Project Jupiter are trading at 89-91 cents on the dollar, indicating that investors are pricing in significant risk. ## Counterpoints

- AI provides real productivity gains of 15-50% in writing, support, and coding, with labor productivity growth doubling in 2025.
- Small models (1-3B parameters) maintain 85-95% accuracy for domain-specific tasks, reducing the need for massive models in routine work.
- The situation is a 'technological revolution with localized bubble dynamics' rather than a total collapse, similar to the electric dynamo adoption curve.
