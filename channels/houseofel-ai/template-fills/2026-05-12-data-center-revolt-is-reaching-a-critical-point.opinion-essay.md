## Thesis

The US lacks the regulatory frameworks seen in Europe to ensure transparency and accountability for data centers, leading to unauthorized resource extraction and hidden costs for local communities.

## Argument

The core issue is not AI itself, but the physical infrastructure supporting it, which is expanding at a scale that outpaces existing regulatory capacity. In Fayette County, Georgia, a Blackstone subsidiary extracted 29 million gallons of unmetered water during a drought, exploiting a system with only one inspector for commercial oversight. Similarly, infrasound from cooling systems and turbines causes physical harm to residents but remains invisible to standard decibel meters designed for audible frequencies. Financially, Maryland consumers are billed $1.66 billion for grid upgrades primarily serving data centers in other states, a cost structure that distributes industrial expenses to the public while profits accrue privately. This pattern mirrors historical crises like the Hinckley groundwater contamination and the opioid epidemic, where systemic harms were structurally invisible until aggregated at a catastrophic scale. While Ireland and the EU have implemented binding renewable energy obligations, mandatory sustainability reporting, and grid operator veto powers, the US relies on fragmented local governance that cannot handle industrial-scale infrastructure. The resulting backlash—moratoriums, ousted councils, and collapsing voter support—is a rational response to governance systems designed for residential oversight attempting to manage industrial mega-projects.

## Counterpoints

- Data centers are essential infrastructure supporting critical services like banking and cloud storage, so they cannot simply be stopped.
- Some operators claim construction water usage explains unmetered extraction and that operational cooling will be closed-loop.
- Ireland's approach is not perfect; environmental groups argue it still locks the country into emission increases via fossil fuel backups.
