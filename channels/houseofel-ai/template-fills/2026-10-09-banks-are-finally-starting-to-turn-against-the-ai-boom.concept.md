## TL;DR

AI systems now act at machine speed, but the infrastructure to test, understand, and regulate them lags behind. This gap creates risks from financial bubbles to bank hacks. A multi-level accountability framework, inspired by banking regulations, can ensure AI agents are traceable and responsible.

## The argument

### Define the Accountability Gap
- **Anchor Timestamps**: ['00:00:00']
- **Claim**: AI agents can now act autonomously, but the question 'who sent it?' often has no answer. This creates an accountability gap where no one is responsible for an AI's actions, as seen in the South Korean bank attacks.
- **Role**: definition

### Evidence: The Korean Bank Hack
- **Anchor Timestamps**: ['00:19:33']
- **Claim**: A single attacker used an open-source tool called Artax to target at least nine South Korean banks. The AI agent automated planning and execution, and one bank took 68 hours to detect the breach, highlighting the speed and scale of the threat.
- **Role**: evidence

### Counter: The Limits of Technical Solutions
- **Anchor Timestamps**: ['00:24:14']
- **Claim**: Technical solutions like Web Bot Auth can identify the company behind an agent, but they don't identify the individual user. Open models running on personal computers bypass these controls entirely, leaving a gap that requires institutional vigilance.
- **Role**: counter

### Synthesis: A Three-Level Accountability Framework
- **Anchor Timestamps**: ['00:23:15']
- **Claim**: Borrowing from banking regulations, the host proposes a three-level framework: model companies must detect and report abuse, individuals must have a 'passport' for their agents, and institutions must treat unsigned traffic with suspicion. This ensures checks are attached to responses, not years later.
- **Role**: synthesis

## Evidence and caveats

The host cites OpenAI's 372 mathematical results, which are verified by Lean but not yet understood by OpenAI's own mathematicians. This illustrates the gap between capability and comprehension. The host also notes that lenders are demanding financial statements, with OpenAI's revenue reported at $50 billion, not $70 billion. However, the host acknowledges that some AI results may be correct but unexplainable, and that the Korean police downplayed the role of AI in the bank hacks, citing weak bank security as a bigger factor. The host also admits that the accountability framework is not fully settled, especially regarding traceability and anonymity.
