## TL;DR

AI agents now act at machine speed, but the systems to test, understand, and regulate them lag behind. A recent attack on South Korean banks and unverifiable AI math proofs expose this gap. The host proposes a three-level accountability framework, borrowing from banking regulations, to ensure every agent action is traceable and responsible.

## The argument

### Define the Core Substrate
- **Anchor Timestamps**: ['00:00:00']
- **Claim**: AI agents can now autonomously plan and execute attacks, as seen in the South Korean bank breach. The first question—'who sent it?'—is hard to answer because the agent may have no human operator. This lack of accountability is the central problem.
- **Role**: definition

### Evidence: The Korean Bank Hack
- **Anchor Timestamps**: ['00:19:33']
- **Claim**: At least nine South Korean banks were targeted by a single attacker using an open-source tool called Artax, which automates a skilled attacker's work. The attack ran at machine speed, but one bank took 68 hours to detect it. The attacker was caught only due to a human error on his resume.
- **Role**: evidence

### Counter: The 'No Intention' Argument
- **Anchor Timestamps**: ['17:29']
- **Claim**: Some argue AI is harmless because it lacks intention—it just follows instructions. But lack of intention doesn't mean lack of consequences. A lock that opens for an AI would open for a patient human, and the bank can't distinguish an experiment from a robbery, so it must treat every intrusion as worst-case.
- **Role**: counter

### Synthesis: Three-Level Accountability
- **Anchor Timestamps**: ['00:23:15']
- **Claim**: Borrowing from banking anti-money-laundering rules, the host proposes three levels of responsibility: model companies must detect and report abuse; agents need a 'passport' identifying the person who authorized them; and institutions must defend themselves, treating unsigned traffic with suspicion. This mirrors how math proofs are checked and financial books are opened.
- **Role**: synthesis

## Evidence and caveats

The Korean bank hack is a concrete example: Artax, an open-source harness, connected to ChatGPT or Claude and automated planning, not just password guessing. The attacker asked Claude for a resume, which led to his identification. However, the host notes that weak bank security was a major factor—'a lock that opens for an AI would probably open for a very patient human.' Also, OpenAI's 372 math results, verified via Lean, are 'almost certainly correct' but not yet understood by its own mathematicians. The host acknowledges that full traceability is debatable, as cash allows anonymous transactions, and open models run locally have no intermediary to report to.
