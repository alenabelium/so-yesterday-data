## TL;DR

As AI agents grow more capable, the infrastructure to test, understand, and regulate them lags behind, creating risks like the recent South Korean bank hacks. The host proposes a three-level accountability framework inspired by banking regulations to ensure AI agents are traceable and responsible. This framework assigns responsibility to model companies, individual users, and institutions, aiming to close the gap between AI's speed and our ability to oversee it.

## The argument

### Define the Core Substrate
- **Anchor Timestamps**: ['00:00:00']
- **Claim**: AI agents can now autonomously breach systems, as seen in the South Korean bank attacks. The first question—'who sent it?'—is hard to answer because the agent may have been sent by no one. A new standard, Web Bot Auth, lets agents digitally sign their actions, but it only identifies the company, not the individual.
- **Role**: definition

### Evidence of the Gap
- **Anchor Timestamps**: ['00:03:15']
- **Claim**: OpenAI's model produced 372 mathematical results, but its own mathematicians can't explain many. This illustrates a pattern: machines produce results quickly, but understanding lags. The company refuses to share prompts or computational details, despite an advisory group's request, highlighting the lack of verifiability.
- **Role**: evidence

### Counterargument: Value of Speed
- **Anchor Timestamps**: ['00:06:34']
- **Claim**: Some argue for publishing AI results despite lack of understanding, as knowledge itself is valuable. Mathematicians like Daniel Litt support openness, and the host agrees, choosing evidence over job security. However, this doesn't negate the need for checks; it just shifts the burden to verification systems.
- **Role**: counter

### Synthesis: Three-Level Accountability
- **Anchor Timestamps**: ['00:23:15']
- **Claim**: Borrowing from banking regulations, the host proposes three levels of responsibility: model companies must detect and report abuse like suspicious transactions; individuals need a digital passport for their agents; institutions must treat unsigned traffic with suspicion. This ensures checks are attached to responses, not years later.
- **Role**: synthesis

## Evidence and caveats

The South Korean bank attacks involved at least nine banks, with one taking 68 hours to detect the breach. The attacker used Artax, an open-source tool, and made a human error on his resume. The host acknowledges that open models on personal computers lack intermediaries, making the institutional level crucial. He also notes that the $70 billion revenue figure for OpenAI was actually $50 billion, a $20 billion discrepancy, and that lenders demanding financial statements is a positive sign. The host admits he hasn't reached a final conclusion on traceability, comparing it to cash transactions that allow anonymity.
