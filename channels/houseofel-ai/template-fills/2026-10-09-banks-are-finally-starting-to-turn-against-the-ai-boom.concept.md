## TL;DR

AI systems are producing results faster than we can verify, understand, or regulate, creating a dangerous gap. The host proposes a three-level accountability framework inspired by banking regulations to ensure AI agents are traceable and responsible. This framework addresses the urgent need for checks in AI deployment, from model companies to individual users to institutions.

## The argument

### Define the Three Levels of Responsibility
- **Anchor Timestamps**: ['00:23:15']
- **Claim**: The host identifies three levels of responsibility for AI actions: the company that creates the model, the person who directs it, and the institution that fails to protect itself. Each level must share accountability, similar to how banks handle money laundering.
- **Role**: definition

### Level One: Companies Must Detect and Report Abuse
- **Anchor Timestamps**: ['00:23:15']
- **Claim**: Model companies must monitor and report suspicious AI activity, like banks reporting unusual transactions. This is technically feasible because agent traffic is visible to the company running the model, and a borderless regulator is needed to enforce it.
- **Role**: evidence

### Level Two: Personal Passports for Agents
- **Anchor Timestamps**: ['00:24:14']
- **Claim**: The WebBot standard gives agents a company stamp, but a personal stamp is missing. Agents should have a 'passport' identifying the user who authorized them, with traceability for serious actions like banking, while allowing anonymity for benign tasks.
- **Role**: synthesis

### Level Three: Institutions Must Defend Themselves
- **Anchor Timestamps**: ['00:25:14']
- **Claim**: Institutions like banks must treat unsigned traffic with suspicion, as they are the front line against attacks. The 68-hour detection delay in the Korean bank hack is unacceptable when attackers operate at machine speed, and open models without intermediaries require institutional vigilance.
- **Role**: evidence

### Checks Must Be Attached to Responses, Not Delayed
- **Anchor Timestamps**: ['00:25:14']
- **Claim**: The framework does not slow down work; it attaches verification to the response, as in mathematics and finance. AI currently skips checks, but the Korean hacker's mistake shows the need for a system that requires them, ensuring accountability without hindering progress.
- **Role**: synthesis

## Evidence and caveats

The host cites the Korean bank attacks as a concrete example: at least nine banks targeted, with one taking 68 hours to detect the breach. The attacker used Artax, an open-source harness, and was identified only by a human error on his resume. The host acknowledges counterarguments: Korean police say AI use is not the point, and weak bank security was a bigger factor. The host also notes that open models without intermediaries complicate enforcement, and that full traceability may not be desirable, leaving room for anonymous transactions. The framework is inspired by anti-money laundering rules, which are meticulous but can flag innocent activity.
