## TL;DR

AI agents are accelerating capabilities faster than our ability to test, understand, or regulate them, creating systemic risks. A new framework, inspired by banking regulations, proposes three levels of accountability to ensure traceability and responsibility. This approach aims to close the gap between AI's speed and our oversight.

## The argument

### Define the Core Problem: The Gap Between Speed and Understanding
- **Anchor Timestamps**: ['00:02:26']
- **Claim**: AI accelerates capabilities—like hacking or math—but our ability to test, understand, or pay for these systems lags behind. This gap creates risks that are both financial (unverifiable chip value) and security-related (AI-driven attacks).
- **Role**: definition

### Evidence: The Korean Bank Hack and the Limits of Current Oversight
- **Anchor Timestamps**: ['00:19:33']
- **Claim**: A single attacker using an open-source tool called Artax targeted at least nine South Korean banks, with one bank taking 68 hours to detect the breach. This shows that AI agents can operate at machine speed, outpacing human detection and response.
- **Role**: evidence

### Counter: The Argument for Openness and Knowledge
- **Anchor Timestamps**: ['00:06:34']
- **Claim**: Despite the risks, there are strong arguments for making AI-generated results public. Mathematicians like Daniel Litt argue that withholding results would harm science. The host personally values knowledge over job security, but acknowledges the cost to those displaced.
- **Role**: counter

### Synthesis: A Three-Level Accountability Framework
- **Anchor Timestamps**: ['00:23:15']
- **Claim**: Borrowing from anti-money laundering rules, the host proposes three levels of responsibility: companies must detect and report abuse, individuals must have a 'passport' for their agents, and institutions must defend themselves against unsigned traffic. This ensures checks are attached to responses, not years later.
- **Role**: synthesis

## Evidence and caveats

The Korean bank hack is a concrete example: Artax, an open-source tool, automated planning and execution, leading to breaches at nine banks. The attacker was caught only due to a human error—a resume with personal details. However, the host notes that weak bank security was a contributing factor, and the Korean police downplayed AI's role. The financial evidence includes Anthropic's $60 billion debt package and OpenAI's revenue discrepancy ($50B vs $70B), which highlights lender nervousness. The host also cites the aspirin analogy to show that knowing something works without understanding why can still be valuable, but it limits new discoveries. Caveats: the host admits uncertainty about traceability, stating 'Should everything be traceable? Probably not,' and acknowledges that open models running on personal computers cannot be regulated by intermediaries.
