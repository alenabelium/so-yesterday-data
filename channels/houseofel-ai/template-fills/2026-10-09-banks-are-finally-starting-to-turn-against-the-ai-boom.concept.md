## TL;DR

AI accelerates capabilities faster than our ability to test, understand, or regulate them, creating a dangerous gap. Recent events—a South Korean bank hack and unverifiable AI math proofs—highlight the urgency. The host proposes a three-level accountability framework, inspired by banking regulations, to ensure AI agents are traceable and responsible.

## The argument

### The Gap Between Speed and Verification
- **Anchor Timestamps**: ['00:02:26']
- **Claim**: AI lets us build faster than we can test, understand, or pay for it. Wall Street raised $100B in loans for chips whose value can't be verified, and lenders are already demanding higher interest rates or financial reports.
- **Role**: definition

### Unverifiable AI Math Results
- **Anchor Timestamps**: ['00:04:25']
- **Claim**: OpenAI published 372 math results, including progress on the Riemann hypothesis, but its own mathematicians can't explain many. Lean proofs verify correctness, but understanding lags. This pattern—fast output, slow comprehension—is typical of AI's current reality.
- **Role**: evidence

### The South Korean Bank Hack
- **Anchor Timestamps**: ['00:19:33']
- **Claim**: A single attacker used an open-source tool called Artax to breach at least nine South Korean banks, compromising 25,000 customers. The AI planned the attack, not just guessed passwords. Banks took up to 68 hours to detect it, and the attacker was caught only by a human error on his resume.
- **Role**: evidence

### Three-Level Accountability Framework
- **Anchor Timestamps**: ['00:23:15']
- **Claim**: Borrowing from anti-money-laundering rules, the host proposes three levels of responsibility: model companies must detect and report abuse; personal agents need a 'passport' identifying the user; and institutions must protect themselves, treating unsigned traffic with suspicion.
- **Role**: synthesis

## Evidence and caveats

The host acknowledges counterarguments: Korean police say AI use isn't the point, and weak bank security was a bigger factor. He agrees a patient human could have done the same, just slower. He also notes the difficulty of verifying AI math results, citing the aspirin analogy—knowing something works vs. why it works. He admits the framework is not fully settled, especially regarding traceability and anonymity.
