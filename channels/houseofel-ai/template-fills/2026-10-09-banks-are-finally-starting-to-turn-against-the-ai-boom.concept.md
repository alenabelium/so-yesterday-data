## TL;DR

AI systems now act at machine speed, but the infrastructure to test, understand, and regulate them lags behind. A recent attack on South Korean banks and unverifiable AI math results expose this gap. The host proposes a three-level accountability framework, inspired by banking regulations, to ensure AI agents are traceable and responsible.

## The argument

### The Gap Between Capability and Verification
- **Anchor Timestamps**: ['00:02:26']
- **Claim**: AI accelerates capabilities, but the ability to test, understand, or pay for these systems doesn't keep pace. Wall Street raised over $100 billion in loans for AI chips whose value can't be verified, and lenders are already demanding financial reports, showing the financial system is reacting to this gap.
- **Role**: definition

### Unverifiable AI Math Results
- **Anchor Timestamps**: ['00:04:25']
- **Claim**: OpenAI published 372 mathematical results, including progress on the Riemann hypothesis, but its own mathematicians can't explain many of them. While Lean proofs verify correctness, understanding lags, and OpenAI refused to share prompts or follow its advisory group's recommendations, highlighting the lack of reproducibility.
- **Role**: evidence

### The Korean Bank Hack: Automation of Planning
- **Anchor Timestamps**: ['00:19:33']
- **Claim**: An AI agent using the open-source tool Artax targeted at least nine South Korean banks, automating not just password guessing but the entire attack planning. The attacker was caught only due to a human error on his resume, showing that AI agents can act without intent but still cause harm, and current defenses are inadequate.
- **Role**: evidence

### Objection: AI Without Intent Is Not the Real Threat
- **Anchor Timestamps**: ['17:29']
- **Claim**: The host's friends argue AI is harmless because it lacks intention. However, the host counters that lack of intent doesn't mean lack of consequences; an AI is only as harmless as the goal set by its user. The real danger is when a person sets a malicious goal, as seen in the bank hack.
- **Role**: counter

### Three-Level Accountability Framework
- **Anchor Timestamps**: ['00:23:15']
- **Claim**: Borrowing from banking regulations, the host proposes a three-level framework: model providers must be responsible for their agents, agents need a digital passport for serious actions, and institutions must defend themselves by treating unsigned traffic with suspicion. This ensures checks are attached to responses, not years later.
- **Role**: synthesis

## Evidence and caveats

The Korean hack shows that even a non-dedicated AI can be weaponized, but the police and security experts note that weak bank protections were a major factor. The host acknowledges that open models run by individuals have no intermediary to report to, making institutional defense crucial. The $50 billion revenue figure for OpenAI is $20 billion less than reported, but part of the difference is accounting methods. The host also notes that the advisory group's recommendations were not followed, and that the discussion on agent anonymity is still open.
