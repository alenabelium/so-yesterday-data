## TL;DR

AI systems now act at machine speed, but the infrastructure to test, understand, and regulate them lags behind, creating dangerous gaps. Recent events—a South Korean bank hack by an AI agent, unverifiable mathematical proofs, and massive AI debt—show the urgency. The host proposes a three-level accountability framework inspired by banking regulations to ensure AI agents are traceable and responsible.

## The argument

### Define the Core Gap: Speed vs. Verification
- **Anchor Timestamps**: ['00:02:26']
- **Claim**: AI accelerates capability, but the ability to test, understand, or pay for it does not keep pace. This gap is the root of current risks, from unverifiable mathematical results to financial debt and security breaches.
- **Role**: definition

### Evidence: Unverifiable Math and Financial Strain
- **Anchor Timestamps**: ['00:04:25', '00:05:32']
- **Claim**: OpenAI's 372 mathematical results, including progress on the Riemann hypothesis, are verified by Lean but not understood by its own mathematicians. Simultaneously, Wall Street raised $100B in loans for AI chips, and OpenAI's real revenue turned out to be $20B less than reported, showing financial strain.
- **Role**: evidence

### Counter: The Case for Publishing AI Math
- **Anchor Timestamps**: ['00:06:34', '00:07:30']
- **Claim**: Despite the lack of understanding, publishing AI-generated math is beneficial. Mathematicians like Daniel Litt argue for openness, and the host personally values knowledge over job security. The aspirin analogy shows that knowing something works without knowing why can still lead to progress.
- **Role**: counter

### Synthesis: Three-Level Accountability Framework
- **Anchor Timestamps**: ['00:23:15', '00:24:14', '00:25:14']
- **Claim**: Borrowing from banking regulations, the host proposes three levels of responsibility: companies must detect and report abuse, persons must have a 'passport' for their agents, and institutions must defend themselves and treat unsigned traffic with suspicion. This ensures checks are attached to responses, not years later.
- **Role**: synthesis

## Evidence and caveats

The South Korean bank hack by an AI agent (Artax) shows that even a non-dedicated hacking tool can cause significant damage, but the Korean police noted that weak bank protection was a bigger factor. The host acknowledges that full traceability may not be desirable, as cash allows anonymous transactions. The framework is inspired by anti-money laundering rules, which are meticulous but can flag innocent transfers. The host also notes that open models run on personal computers have no intermediary to report to, making institutional defense crucial.
