## Thesis

AI didn't break education; it exposed the lie that unproctored exams and honor codes can function in an era of invisible, high-quality cheating, forcing a shift from testing memory recall to evaluating judgment and tool navigation.

## Argument

The recent reinstatement of proctored exams at Princeton is not a moral victory but a tacit admission that the 133-year-old honor code system was already broken. Data reveals the enforcement mechanism failed long before AI: at Princeton, only 0.4% of students reported peer violations, and at Stanford, just two out of 720 reports came from students. The system relied on visible cheating, but AI made misconduct invisible; a student checking a phone under a desk looks identical to one checking the time. Consequently, detection rates for AI cheating sit at a mere 6%, while AI-assisted misconduct now accounts for 60-64% of all cheating cases globally. The cheating rate didn't spike; the ceiling did. Traditional exams tested recall because knowledge was scarce. Today, information is universally accessible. The relevant skill is no longer storing data but knowing what exists, when to use it, and how to evaluate the output. As the host notes, he can't reproduce complex proofs from memory but knows where to find them and how to apply them. Education must shift to testing this cognitive map and judgment, mirroring professional environments where AI is a tool for amplification, not a threat to be banned. Companies that cut staff to save costs saw no ROI improvement, while those that amplified human skills did. The current proctoring model is a band-aid on a structural problem; it preserves the format of the 19th century while the world has moved on.

## Counterpoints

- Students are often pressured by deadlines and competition, making cheating understandable, though the purpose of education is skill acquisition, not just credentialing.
- The host acknowledges uncertainty about whether the 'suffering' of rote memorization builds the necessary cognitive map, inviting debate rather than prescribing a single fix.
- Proctoring is admitted by Princeton itself to be a 'band-aid' that won't eradicate cheating, but a necessary response to the collapse of peer surveillance.
