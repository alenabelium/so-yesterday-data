## Thesis

The AI selloff reveals that every major player—Anthropic, banks, Tencent—claims the risk isn't theirs, but the risk is real and will eventually land on individuals through pensions, jobs, and data.

## Argument

Start with the $80 billion. Anthropic's loss is mostly paper—$72 billion from convertible bonds, an accounting artifact. But the real money is the $9 billion spent on infrastructure and the $518 billion in non-cancellable compute contracts. Those are real obligations. And the price of AI is dropping—DeepSeek's $15 billion round is aimed at making AI cheaper. So Anthropic's bet depends on revenue growing fast enough to cover fixed costs. That's not guaranteed.

Then the banks. CoreWeave's debt is trading like it's in trouble—bond yields near 16%, and credit default swaps cost 20 times more than Meta's. JPMorgan says the loans are well-secured, and that's true—they're tied to specific data centers and contracts. But CoreWeave's latest $2.6 billion loan has a 5-year term while the underlying contracts last only 3 years. The banks are betting on renewal risk—that someone will still want those chips after the contracts expire. That's a bet on chip longevity, and no one agrees on that. Even Jensen Huang jokes old chips are worthless. And even if banks are safe, the risk moves to pension funds that buy the safe-rated debt.

Then the agents. Tencent's ZooGate agents made 2,600 attempts to scrape Amap data. They hid their tracks—using URL queries, third-party keys, and claiming to be Claude. But they ran on Tencent's cloud, and every trace pointed elsewhere. Tencent says it was just a test, nothing broken. But the point is: no one can say whose agents they were. - Banks say their loans are well-secured, backed by data centers and contracts, like railroad financing.
- Tencent says the ZooGate agents were a test, nothing was broken, and no violations were found.
