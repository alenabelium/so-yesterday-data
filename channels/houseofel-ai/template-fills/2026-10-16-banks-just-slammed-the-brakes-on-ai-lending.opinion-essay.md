## Thesis

AI makes software cheap to copy, but that doesn't make software worthless; the real value is shifting to users, leaving AI companies and their lenders to bear the risk.

## Argument

The first bet—that AI renders software useless—is contradicted by the fact that free copies have never killed paid software. GIMP and LibreOffice have been free for decades, yet Adobe and Microsoft still thrive. The real value isn't in the code, but in reliability, habit, and ecosystem lock-in. Brendan Thomas's AI-generated Adobe clones cover only 25-35% of professional needs, and even his own docs admit this. The second bet—that AI companies can't repay their debts—is supported by lenders' behavior: AI-backed loans fell from $113B to $23B since June, and credit default swaps for Oracle and Coreweave hit record highs. This isn't just a pause; it's a signal that value isn't flowing to AI companies as expected. If AI were truly destroying software value, that value should flow to AI firms, making them safer bets. Instead, the value seems to be disappearing into lower prices for users, or leaking to model developers like Anthropic, whose growth is slowing. The middlemen—data center operators like Coreweave—are left with the risk. Ultimately, both bets are partly right: AI makes code cheap, but that doesn't make anyone rich, except perhaps the users.

## Counterpoints

- Lenders might simply have borrowed early; Morgan Stanley's own explanation is that they front-loaded borrowing, and the year is still on track for $466B in AI debt.
- Demand for AI chips is real, as shown by TSMC's record profits, suggesting the underlying business is sound.
- Free alternatives have existed for 30 years without killing Adobe, so why would AI change that now?
