## Thesis

Grocery 'dynamic pricing' is actually surveillance pricing, where AI uses facial recognition and personal data to charge individuals their maximum willingness to pay, creating a discriminatory and unregulated revenue stream.

## Argument

The mechanism is **[[surveillance-capitalism]]** disguised as retail efficiency. Cameras on shelves analyze face, age, and gender, cross-referencing this with purchase history and broker-bought income data to calculate the **[[maximum-willingness-to-pay]]** in real time. This isn't theoretical; Kroger's Edge system and Microsoft's embedded cameras are already live in 500+ stores, with Walmart rolling out to 2,300 by 2026. The infrastructure is built to change prices in seconds via electronic shelf labels, a market growing to $7.54 billion by 2033.

The ethical failure is twofold. First, it decouples price from cost or supply, tying it instead to **[[algorithmic-bias]]** and personal pain thresholds. Second, it enables **[[price-discrimination]]** at scale. As Senator Warren noted, prices surge based on transitory events like weather, not actual scarcity. Crucially, facial recognition algorithms have higher error rates for darker skin tones, meaning the system can systematically overcharge Black and Brown customers due to misidentification, not malice. Retailers claim this lowers prices, but the capability to charge more exists and will be operationalized when shareholders demand growth. Maryland's recent ban is structurally weak, lacking enforcement or fines, rendering it a polite suggestion rather than a deterrent against billion-dollar infrastructure rollouts.

## Counterpoints

- Kroger claims cameras are only for personalized ads, not pricing, and insists the technology is designed to lower prices for customers.
- Maryland passed a ban on dynamic pricing, establishing a legal precedent against the practice in grocery stores.
- Retailers argue the system is a 'workable framework' for managing price instability and inflation pressures.
