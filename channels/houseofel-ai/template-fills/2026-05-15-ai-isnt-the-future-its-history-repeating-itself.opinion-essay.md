## Thesis

AI is structurally the next Industrial Revolution, meaning its benefits will be delayed for decades while elites capture value, making regulatory reform the only path to justice for displaced workers.

## Argument

The UCF commencement booing wasn't irrational; it was a rational response to a narrative that ignored the brutal reality of technological disruption. Gloria Caulfield’s comparison of AI to the Industrial Revolution is historically accurate but morally hollow. The British Industrial Revolution (1760–1840) wasn't a gentle transition. It involved 12-16 hour shifts, child labor, and life expectancies dropping to 25-35 years in industrial towns. Wealth concentrated in factory owners while workers suffered stagnation and disease for 60-100 years before reforms like the 1833 Factory Act arrived. Today, tech elites mirror this pattern: data centers extract resources from communities with no political voice, akin to the 'rotten boroughs' of 19th-century Britain. The key question isn't if AI is transformative, but whether reforms will come in five years or sixty. Without active political pressure and regulation, the pain of change will remain unevenly distributed, favoring those who build the infrastructure over those displaced by it.

## Counterpoints

- Caulfield’s comparison is factually accurate regarding structural disruption, even if her tone was tone-deaf.
- STEM graduates at CMU cheer because they are the builders capturing value, unlike humanities grads who are the displaced labor.
- The 'bubble' effect causes tech insiders to mistake their privileged position for universal experience.
- Reforms like the 1833 Factory Act eventually helped, but only after decades of suffering and political agitation.
