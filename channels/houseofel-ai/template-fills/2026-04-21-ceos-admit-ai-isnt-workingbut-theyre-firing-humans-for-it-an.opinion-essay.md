## Thesis

Corporate AI layoffs are largely opportunistic cost-cutting disguised as innovation, not genuine technological displacement, creating a fog where real automation and financial excuses are indistinguishable.

## Argument

The disconnect is stark: 90% of CEOs report no measurable AI productivity gains, yet nearly half of recent tech layoffs cite AI. This isn't just hype; it's a structural cover. Companies face margin pressure and need to fund massive infrastructure bets (like the $650B expected from Big Tech). Payroll is the largest controllable cost, so firing humans provides the capital for data centers while offering a forward-thinking narrative. 

This 'AI washing' is evident in Klarna’s failed customer service overhaul, where replacing 700 workers with chatbots cratered satisfaction, forcing rehiring. Research shows 55% of companies with AI-driven layoffs regret it. The time saved by AI is often eaten by verification (37-40% of saved time goes to fixing errors). Workers trust the tools less even as they use them more. The current phase is defined by this fog: genuine displacement and opportunistic cost-cutting happen simultaneously, but the economic data lags the narrative by years. Only those treating AI as augmentation, not wholesale replacement, will survive the inevitable correction.

## Counterpoints

- Some data shows modest gains: Fed Reserve notes 1.9% excess productivity since ChatGPT, and MIT projects 0.5% over a decade.
- Executives expect future gains (1.4 productivity increase), creating a gap between current reality and expectation.
- Some roles like coding assistance and basic data processing are genuinely being automated.
