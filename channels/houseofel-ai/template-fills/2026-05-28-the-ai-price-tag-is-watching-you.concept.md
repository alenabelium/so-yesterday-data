## TL;DR

Surveillance pricing sets a price for you personally from what an algorithm infers you will pay, not from supply and demand. The hardware to do it at scale is being installed in stores across the US right now. The stakes are whether that mechanism reaches the things you cannot refuse to buy.

## The argument

### Surveillance Pricing Is Not Dynamic Pricing
- **Anchor Timestamps**: [259]
- **Claim**: Dynamic pricing moves with supply and demand and is at least legible. Surveillance pricing changes the price for you specifically, because an algorithm read your browsing history, location, and behavior to estimate what you individually will pay. The input is not the market; the input is you, and you cannot see or opt out of it.
- **Role**: definition

### The Infrastructure Is Already Built
- **Anchor Timestamps**: [259, 436]
- **Claim**: Walmart is putting electronic shelf labels in all ~5,200 US stores and has patented software to automatically update prices, enabling instant changes across 120,000 products. Kroger, Whole Foods, and Aldi are following. The company says pricing stays human-led, but the architecture for individualized pricing now exists industrywide.
- **Role**: evidence

### The Data Reveals Who You Are
- **Anchor Timestamps**: [507]
- **Claim**: The algorithm needs your patterns, not your name: search history, app usage, location, even reported signals like phone battery level. From those it infers income, urgency, and inability to walk away. Because behavior correlates with race, income, and neighborhood, the result is discrimination with a better user interface.
- **Role**: evidence

### Applied To Essentials It Becomes Perfect Price Discrimination
- **Anchor Timestamps**: [689, 763]
- **Claim**: The mechanism that worked on eggs does not distinguish a luxury from rent, energy, or medicine. With enough information a seller could price each inelastic good at the maximum you can bear, capturing all consumer surplus. Economists call this perfect price discrimination: the best outcome for the seller, the worst for the buyer.
- **Role**: counter

### Only External Regulation Stops It
- **Anchor Timestamps**: [836, 1040]
- **Claim**: The DOJ's Real Page antitrust case showed algorithmic pricing already coordinating rent on 16 million units. Companies show no internal safeguard, so the only brake is external: Maryland's grocery ban, New York's disclosure act, FTC investigations, and proposed federal bans. The technology is not the problem; the deployment is a choice.
- **Role**: synthesis

## Evidence and caveats

Concrete evidence: a Consumer Reports / Groundwork / More Perfect Union study of 400+ volunteers found ~75% of Instacart items priced differently across customers, with one DC Safeway selling identical eggs at five prices ($3.99-$4.79) simultaneously, an estimated ~$1,200/year hit for some shoppers. JetBlue told a customer to clear cookies, then retracted it; a class action followed. Real Page settled in November 2025 without admitting wrongdoing. Caveats the speaker is careful to make: Walmart states pricing is human-led, reviewed by associates outside shopping hours, and equal for all customers in a store, and the speaker says he has no reason to doubt this is current practice. Digital shelf labels have genuine operational value (one team leader cut pricing duties by 75%). Maryland's ban has loopholes, applying only to groceries and still allowing loyalty programs. The endpoint scenario is explicitly framed as a thought experiment extrapolating from mechanisms that already exist, not a claim about what is happening today.
