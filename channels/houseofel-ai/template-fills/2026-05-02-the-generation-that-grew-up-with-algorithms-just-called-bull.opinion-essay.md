## Thesis

Gen Z's sabotage of corporate AI is not technological resistance but rational labor resistance against a power grab disguised as innovation.

## Argument

The core mechanism is a performative power grab. Executives, fearing job loss, deploy AI strategies they admit are mostly for show (75% admit this) despite 90% reporting zero productivity impact. This creates a two-tier workforce: an "AI elite" of executives who use the tools for hours daily and regular workers who are threatened with layoff or stalled promotion if they refuse. Gen Z, having witnessed the exploitative patterns of social media and gig economies, recognizes this as a repeat of platform capitalism. They use sabotage—feeding proprietary data to public bots, refusing tools, or generating low-quality output—as their only leverage to block a system that concentrates wealth and control while offering no real value. The result is a widening class divide, compounded security risks, and a collapse of trust in management.

## Counterpoints

- Executives claim AI adoption is necessary for future relevance, threatening non-compliance with layoffs.
- Some argue Gen Z's resistance stems from job displacement anxiety rather than strategic critique.
- Daily AI users show declining hopefulness, suggesting familiarity breeds understanding of its limitations.
