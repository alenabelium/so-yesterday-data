## Thesis

The panic over 'slop' is a reductive weapon that obscures the real issue: AI is an amplifier of human intent, and blanket bans are destroying the economic model of the web.

## Argument

**[00:01:01]** The internet has fundamentally shifted. In June 2026, bots became the majority on the network, generating 57.5% of requests. AI agent traffic grew 7,851% year-over-year. YouTube terminated channels with 35 million subscribers for scraping and spinning scripts. On LinkedIn, 41% of long-form posts are flagged as AI-generated.

**[00:04:46]** The term 'slop' welds 'low quality' to 'AI', implying the tool causes the value. This is false. Content farms and clickbait existed before LLMs. AI changed the economics, making garbage virtually free. Think of AI as an amplifier. It takes your signal and makes it louder. Feed it a melody, you get a concert. Feed it static, you get a headache. An expert using AI produces better work; a bot farm produces slop. The tool didn't change; the human input did.

**[00:11:52]** The economic damage of this panic is measurable. On CGTrader, AI models are 1 in 6 uploads but account for only $1 of every $90 in revenue. Buyers refuse to pay. In Australia, wholly AI music was banned from charts. Coca-Cola’s AI campaign faced backlash; Dove’s anti-AI pledge drove a 23% sales lift. Trust drops 50% when content is merely perceived as AI-generated.

**[00:18:56]** This panic hurts experts who use AI as an equalizer. Finance analysts, researchers, and engineers often have expertise that outstrips their communication skills. AI translates their insight into language. It is a bridge, not slop. The binary of human vs. machine is analytically useless. We need separate categories for music and content.
