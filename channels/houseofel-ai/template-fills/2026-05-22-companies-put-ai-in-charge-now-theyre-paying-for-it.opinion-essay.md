## Thesis

Corporate AI failures stem from strategic negligence, not technological flaws, as companies treat AI as a wholesale replacement for human judgment rather than an augmentation tool, creating catastrophic systemic risks.

## Argument

The Pizza Hut case reveals that Dragontail’s algorithm didn’t crash; it succeeded in optimizing for metrics while ignoring the **[[incentive-structures]]** of gig workers. By giving DoorDash drivers real-time kitchen visibility, it enabled **[[algorithmic-gaming]]** where drivers waited to batch orders, doubling rack time and halving on-time deliveries. This was a **[[systems-design-failure]]** where two independent optimization systems interacted unpredictably. Similarly, Klarna’s attempt to replace 700 agents with AI chatbots ignored the **[[complexity-of-emotion]]** in customer service. While AI handled volume, it failed at nuanced, emotionally charged interactions, forcing a public reversal and rehiring. The core error in both cases is treating AI as a **[[wholesale-replacement]]** rather than an **[[augmentation-tool]]**. Companies like Unity and Hiscox succeed by using AI to handle computation while humans retain judgment. Forrester data shows 55% of employers regret AI-driven layoffs, confirming that **[[parallel-systems]]** and gradual integration are essential. Reckless deployment harms public trust, creating a **[[backlash-against-ai]]** that stifles legitimate innovation. Executives who skip redundancy and testing are not innovating; they are practicing negligence with expensive consequences.

## Counterpoints

- AI is technically broken and unreliable in production environments.
- AI cannot handle complex or emotionally charged customer interactions.
- Gig workers are exploiting AI visibility to optimize their own earnings at the expense of service quality.
