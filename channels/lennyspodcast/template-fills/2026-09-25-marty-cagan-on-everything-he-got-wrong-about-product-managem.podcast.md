## Key arguments

### Underestimating Business Viability
- **Timestamp**: [05:45](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=fF3lkTCM5-c&t=345)
- **Summary**: Cagan admits he ignored business viability (legal, privacy, sales cost) in the first edition of Inspired. He argues that while engineering backgrounds helped in dev tools, modern AI products require holistic systems thinking to ensure solutions are viable for the business.
- **Anchor Quotes**: [1]

### Over-indexing on Problem Identification
- **Timestamp**: [08:50](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=fF3lkTCM5-c&t=530)
- **Summary**: He regrets emphasizing problem identification too much, noting that teams often get stuck there. He argues that innovation happens in finding solutions, and the real key to innovation is asking why people are NOT using your product.

### The Trap of Predictability
- **Timestamp**: [13:10](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=fF3lkTCM5-c&t=790)
- **Summary**: Cagan identifies the desire for predictability as a major regret, linking it to roadmaps and PRDs. He warns that using these artifacts arrogantly assumes knowledge we don't have, wasting engineering capacity and tokens on features that won't deliver results.
- **Anchor Quotes**: [1]

### Neglecting Product Leadership
- **Timestamp**: [16:45](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=fF3lkTCM5-c&t=1005)
- **Summary**: He acknowledges that focusing solely on team craft ignored the critical role of product leadership and corporate governance. Empowered teams need better management, not less, to navigate politics and strategy in a competitive market.

### Process as a Substitute for Thinking
- **Timestamp**: [19:40](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=fF3lkTCM5-c&t=1180)
- **Summary**: Cagan regrets structuring his work around 'people, process, product,' fearing it encouraged using frameworks and AI as substitutes for thinking. He asserts that good product work is fundamentally about mindset and creating to learn, not just delivering.
- **Anchor Quotes**: [1]
