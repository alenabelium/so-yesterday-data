## Thesis

Chain theory rebuilds fundamental physics from a single axiom about quantum information, deriving the fine structure constant to 10 significant digits and challenging the field's retreat from derivation.

## Argument

The mechanism is a shift in foundational assumptions. Physics has been starved of new structure since the 1970s, and the drought signals a need for a different starting point, not more data. The field retreated to anthropic reasoning and the string landscape, giving up on deriving constants. Chain theory instead holds derivation as the goal and finds new foundations in three existing clues: the finite information bound, the standard model gauge group, and the point-like nature of particles. These clues point to a network of finitely many maximally entangled pairs of finite-dimensional systems. The theory's single axiom—'Nothing exists but finitely many maximally entangled pairs of finite dimensional systems some shared known only by distilling them'—yields a parameter-free candidate for the fine structure constant, matching the measured value to 10 significant digits. This is a theorem of a stated axiom, not a fitted number, and it offers a sharp test: new measurements will either close in on the derived value or rule it out. The theory also interprets the standard model gauge group as a theorem, and derives other features like the Lorentz group and three generations, though many results remain conditional.

## Counterpoints

- The experimental measurements of alpha_0 still disagree with one another by more than their stated uncertainties, so the comparison is not yet decisive.
- Other programs have derived parts of the standard model from finite or algebraic inputs, but none supplies the value of a coupling constant to within experimental precision.
- Closed form expressions for the fine structure constant have a long and mostly numerological history; chain theory differs by being a theorem of a stated axiom.
