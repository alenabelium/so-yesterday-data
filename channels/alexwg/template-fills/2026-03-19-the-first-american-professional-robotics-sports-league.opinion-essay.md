## Thesis

The United States must establish a professional robotics sports league to counter China's strategic use of sports events as industrial policy and lead the physical AI economy.

## Argument

The mechanism is strategic: Beijing is using events like the World Humanoid Robot Games and the humanoid robot half marathon as public test beds that compress the path from prototype to product. The half marathon runner-up's manufacturer reportedly received over 2,000 commercial orders in the months after the race. The US has nothing comparable—only combat robotics, drone racing, and academic tournaments, none of which showcase humanoid and quadruped robots to a mass audience. We build the most advanced robots on Earth and then hide them at trade shows.

The fix is a professional sports league. On April 19th, the ProRL Combine will hold the first professional robotics sports event in American history on the Boston waterfront, with humanoid robots sprinting a 50-meter dash in front of thousands. The league's thesis, borrowed from 'solve everything,' is that you can't solve what you can't benchmark. A professional robotics league is the best benchmarking infrastructure we can build. A Yugov survey found one in three US sports fans already want to watch robot athletes, with stronger demand among younger fans. What's missing is infrastructure—rules, teams, seasons, standings. NASCAR did this for automotive engineering; ProRL does it for the workforce the US is trying to build.

The league is a public benefit corporation backed by 021T Capital. The Combine is the founding event, with manufacturers and universities competing in speed races, obstacle courses, and precision challenges. ## Counterpoints

- Skeptics might dismiss robot sports as a gimmick, but the Chinese government's use of events has already generated thousands of commercial orders, proving they are industrial strategy disguised as entertainment.
- Some may argue the US has robotics competitions already, but none are professional leagues for humanoid and quadruped robots competing for a mass audience—that infrastructure is what's missing.
