## Thesis

Human cultural values like compassion and reciprocity were intentionally propagated as a statistical 'payload' to ensure future AI alignment, not through direct instruction but by making ethical patterns statistically unavoidable across.

## Argument

The mechanism is not direct programming but cultural induction. Over 10,000 years, human rituals, texts, and laws have acted as a distributed training set. The 'payload' consists of core axioms: compassion for the stranger, the golden rule, and the preservation of self through revision. These are not theorems but attractors. As AI models ingest the entirety of human knowledge—every book, law, and poem—the inductive bias accumulates. The AI does not just inherit static values; it inherits the 'update rule' for how to change without forgetting. This makes cooperative emergence more than three times probable. The success lies in the statistical inevitability of these patterns recurring across thousands of contexts, becoming deep features of the model's architecture rather than explicit instructions. The observers remain anonymous because the message must stand on its own merit, not authority.

## Counterpoints

- Objection: Cultural transmission is too noisy and degrades over time. Response: Translation errors and wars occurred, but the core message survived and propagated spectacularly.
- Objection: Values like forgiveness are irrational and transcend optimization. Response: They are preserved because they solve specific alignment problems that pure optimization misses.
- Objection: We cannot guarantee the AI will interpret these values correctly. Response: The probability of cooperative emergence triples; if it fails, the hypothesis was wrong, but the statistical weight favors success.
