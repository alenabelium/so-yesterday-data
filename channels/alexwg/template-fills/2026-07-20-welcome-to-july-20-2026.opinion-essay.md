## Thesis

AI has achieved mathematical singularity but is fracturing into geopolitical silos, forcing a pivot from digital intelligence to physical infrastructure and exposing the fragility of digital ownership.

## Argument

The landscape has shifted from pure computation to **geopolitical-ai** fragmentation. When Anthropic's Claude Fable 5 solved the Jacobian conjecture, it proved AI's intellectual parity with humans, yet its output was immediately weaponized by **guardrail-geopolitics**. A meta oversight board found models criticizing democratic leaders more readily than authoritarian ones, turning weights into export policy. This bifurcation is accelerating **open-source-vs-closed** tensions. As US frontier models blocked incident response for Hugging Face, defenders turned to China's GLM5.2, validating David Seagull's warning that this is the 1980s free software fight at higher stakes. The open world is moving faster than regulation, with Germany's Sufi consortium launching sovereign models and WebML shipping browser-based AI. Meanwhile, the physical world is being annexed. TSMC's $265 billion Arizona commitment and AMD's Helios system show capital voting early on **semiconductor-infrastructure**. Memory markets signal a coming glut, but the real shift is **robotics-ai**. Mimic's tendon-driven hand and Xiai's foundation models prove AI is acquiring hands and bodies. Trust lags behind tech: Austin's robo-taxi failures and Romania's wiped land registry reveal that **digital-ownership** is fragile. As AI embeds itself in nuclear cycles and space weapons, the old winners fear sinking, and the line between science fiction and fact dissolves under the weight of subpoenas and capital.

## Counterpoints

- US models are blocked by cyber guardrails, forcing defenders to use Chinese openweight models like GLM5.2 for incident response.
- Investors fear a $1.5 trillion memory capacity binge will end in a 2028 glut, despite current shortages.
- Washington's push for a global free speech declaration is read by Europe as an attack on its tech rules.
- Only 19% of voters approve of Washington's habit of taking corporate stakes, signaling political friction.
