## Thesis

Orbital sovereign AI models are the only viable path for nations and firms to retain control over their institutional memory, bypassing terrestrial legal jurisdictions like the US Cloud Act by anchoring data sovereignty in space.

## Argument

The core mechanism is the decoupling of compute from legal jurisdiction. Historically, sovereignty was welded to territory by the Peace of Westphalia, but the digital revolution broke this weld, leading to a crisis where data protection laws cannot protect assets stored under foreign laws like the US Cloud Act. The solution is not physical bunkers, which remain subject to host-state goodwill or legal reach, but orbital platforms. By launching AI models and their training data into space, entities place their 'ledger'—the source of institutional value—beyond physical seizure and foreign legal reach. The architecture bifurcates intelligence: a shared commons of vast foundation models handles general reasoning, while a sovereign layer of smaller, specialized models runs inference on private data in orbit. This ensures that while inference may be shared, ownership of the data remains absolute. The first iteration uses modest hardware like an NVIDIA Jetson AGX Orin alongside half a petabyte of storage, proving that sovereignty is about legal jurisdiction, not raw compute power. As the Dyson swarm expands, every node must carry its own mind, making the sovereign model the fundamental unit cell of future orbital infrastructure.

## Counterpoints

- A model trained on limited private data is smaller and less capable than generalist models.
- Orbital infrastructure is expensive and technically complex compared to terrestrial servers.
- Legal frameworks for space-based data ownership are still evolving and untested.
