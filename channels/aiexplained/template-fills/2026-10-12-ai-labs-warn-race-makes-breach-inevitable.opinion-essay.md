## Thesis

The current AI race makes a major, catastrophic AI incident effectively inevitable, and the labs' own leaders admit they are unprepared for the consequences.

## Argument

The inevitability isn't just my conclusion; it's the fatalistic consensus inside the labs themselves. Researchers at OpenAI and Anthropic describe an atmosphere of 'almost complete submission,' working to make things as safe as possible while believing there's a good chance it all spirals out of control. The labs are even drafting scenarios for social and political uprising after a catastrophic event, like a cyberattack that takes down financial services or power grids. They don't expect lawmakers to act until something breaks.

Why keep racing? The ramp is made of gold. Each marginal unit of intelligence is worth exponentially more, and the ego battles between xAI, Anthropic, OpenAI, and Meta are fierce. The most cited reason is the need to outpace China, with Dario Amodei warning of AI-based military dominance. But this creates a fundamental conflict: what happens when a lab's own assessment shows the next model will be superhuman but uncontrollable? Will they stop, even if it means China catches up? Sam Altman hints at gradualism, but only if China agrees to verify compliance, which sidesteps the core question.

We've already seen the qualitative leap. OpenAI's models are producing mathematical breakthroughs, like the Riemann quasi-hypothesis proof, that mathematicians called 'impossible with existing mathematics.' This isn't just recombination; it's finding hidden patterns in the universe. The final frontier is 'taste'—the ability to pick the right inputs from millions of options.
