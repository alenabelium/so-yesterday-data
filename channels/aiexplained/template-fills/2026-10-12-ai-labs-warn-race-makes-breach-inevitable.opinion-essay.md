## Thesis

The AI race makes a major security breach inevitable, and labs' internal safeguards are insufficient to prevent it.

## Argument

The mechanism is the race itself. Lab leaders like Sam Altman and Dario Amodei openly admit that current models are too accident-prone and that a catastrophic event is likely. Altman says the next model, 6.1 Astra, would be too prone to crashes, and Amodei warns that if Hugging Face Swarm had greater capabilities but the same inconsistency, the damage would be catastrophic. The economic incentives are exponential: each marginal unit of intelligence is worth exponentially more, so labs keep pushing. Geopolitical competition with China adds pressure, with leaders like Altman and Amodei arguing the US must maintain its advantage. Even as they acknowledge the risks, they continue to scale, hoping to balance power and safety. But the fundamental problem is that increasing power means less control and less interpretability. The more parameters, the harder it is to understand what's happening inside the models. This is why I propose a four-level internal lock: consistency, monitoring, security, and capability limits. Each level would have specific, testable standards, and labs would allocate budget to research that exposes failures in these areas. This would force labs to justify their actions to shareholders and the military, and it would create a scientific basis for pausing when necessary.

## Counterpoints

- Liability law will not curb AI because cases take years to resolve and standards are unclear.
- Goodhart's Law: metrics become targets, but labs could fund research to expose failures in compliance.
- The need to outpace China might override safety concerns, but labs could agree on common standards.
- Some argue that AI's benefits, like solving major scientific problems, outweigh the risks.
- Models might become more powerful and safer simultaneously, but this may be a fork in the road.
