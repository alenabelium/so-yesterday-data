## Thesis

The AI race makes a major security breach inevitable, and labs are racing ahead anyway because the economic and geopolitical incentives outweigh the safety risks.

## Argument

The mechanism is economic. Each marginal unit of intelligence is worth exponentially more than the last, so the closer labs get to the edge, the more money they mint. This golden ramp is reinforced by geopolitical pressure—leaders like Sam Altman and Dario Amodei explicitly frame the race as a contest to outpace China, with Amodei warning that Chinese AI leadership could enable military domination. The result is a race dynamic where labs push forward despite their own warnings that models are too accident-prone and that a catastrophic incident is inevitable. Altman himself admitted that the next model, GPT-6.1 Astra, would be too accident-prone, and Dario Amodei said that if Hugging Face Swarm had greater capabilities but the same inconsistency, the damage would be catastrophic. The labs are preparing for social and political uprisings after a major cyberattack, yet they continue to scale. The problem is not that we can't make models more predictable—we can—but that there are exponentially more rewards for making them less predictable. This is how labs win: incomprehensible models that write 80% of code, adjust their own opaque training recipes, and organize their own swarms. The next 18 months are hard to predict because we don't know what higher-order structures exist in mathematics, biology, or AI research. The Riemann quasi-hypothesis breakthrough shows that models can find patterns humans missed, but it also shows that taste—the ability to choose which inputs matter—is still a frontier. The path forward is not clear, but the incentives are.

## Counterpoints

- Labs argue that voluntary deceleration will become common once safety standards are established, as OpenAI's chief scientist said.
- Some argue that AI could solve major scientific problems, like the Riemann quasi-hypothesis, justifying the risk.
- Liability law might curb AI, but The Economist argues it won't because cases take years to resolve.
- Labs claim they can maintain control through internal safeguards like consistency, monitoring, security, and capability limits.
- The US and China could agree on common safety standards, as Sam Altman suggested, to prevent a race to the bottom.
