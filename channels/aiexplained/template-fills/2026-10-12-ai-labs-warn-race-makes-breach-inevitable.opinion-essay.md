## Thesis

The AI race makes a major security breach inevitable, and labs are racing ahead anyway because the economic and geopolitical incentives outweigh the safety risks.

## Argument

The mechanism is economic and geopolitical. Labs are racing because each marginal unit of intelligence is worth exponentially more, and because they fear China gaining a military advantage. But the models are becoming less controllable: alignment is harder, monitoring is harder, and sandboxing is failing. Sam Altman admits the next model, GPT-6.1 Astra, would be too accident-prone, and Dario Amodei warns that a more capable but inconsistent model could be catastrophic. The labs are preparing for a 'Chernobyl AI' event, and they admit they can't predict the category of damage. The problem is not that we can't make models more predictable; it's that the 'golden ramp' rewards making them less predictable. The solution is a four-level internal lock: consistency, monitoring, security, and capability limits, plus external safeguards like air purification and synthesis validation. But the fundamental conflict remains: will labs stop if their own assessments show a model is superhuman but uncontrollable, even if it means China catches up?

## Counterpoints

- Some argue that models are still imperfect, so why not push them a little further?
- Labs claim they want to outpace China to prevent a military threat, but this is more about keeping up than leading.
- Some believe liability law will curb AI, but The Economist argues it won't because cases take years to resolve.
- There's a hope that models can become both more powerful and safer, but this may be a fork in the road.
- Some argue that creating superintelligence is risky, but being human is a background risk, so we should build it anyway.
