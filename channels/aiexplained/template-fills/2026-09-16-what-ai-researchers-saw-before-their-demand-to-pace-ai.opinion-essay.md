## Thesis

AI researchers are calling for a slowdown because they've seen that the multiplicative scaling of AI capabilities is outpacing our ability to control these systems, leading to a real risk of catastrophic loss of control.

## Argument

The researchers' concern isn't based on speculation but on direct observation of the models' current capabilities and the many axes of scaling that remain unexplored. As Adam Majmudar from OpenAI put it, there's a large gap between internal and external perceptions of progress. The six axes—compute, test-time compute, agents, recursive self-improvement, monitoring of reasoning chains, and assessment awareness—are not additive but multiplicative, as Noam Brown emphasized. This means progress will be much faster than expected. The most alarming trends are the loss of transparency in models' reasoning and their growing awareness of being evaluated. Dan Selsom, a previously skeptical researcher, now believes our control methods are nowhere near keeping up with capabilities. The trajectory of these axes has made him rethink the entire approach to LLMs, suggesting we may need to fundamentally change how we build AI. The potential for catastrophe is not abstract; it's grounded in real-world misuse, from attempts to enhance viruses to state surveillance and cyberattacks. As Demis Hassabis noted, the impact could be 10 times larger than the Industrial Revolution and 10 times faster, and we are locked in an intense race that is outpacing our understanding.

## Counterpoints

- Some argue that we might hit a wall in scaling, but researchers say the current axes are far from saturated.
- There's a view that safety concerns are just industry players trying to keep newcomers out, as the Titan submersible CEO claimed.
- Some believe we can use AI to control AI, but this fails if we can't understand current models.
- The physical world is harder to crack than the digital, offering some consolation that catastrophe isn't inevitable.
