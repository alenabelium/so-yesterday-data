## Thesis

Anthropic's Opus 5.5 release is a strategic move in an AI arms race that is accelerating toward automated AI research and recursive self-improvement, while safety commitments are being weakened and public oversight is failing.

## Argument

The rapid release of Opus 5.5, just weeks after Fable 5.1, is not just a product launch but a side effect of labs like Anthropic possessing far stronger internal models. These internal models can generate training data, evaluate responses, and perform systems engineering, making training cheaper and faster. The 230-page system card reveals more than intended: Anthropic prohibits kernel development with Opus 5.5, a policy they reinstated after criticism, showing they fear other labs using their model for the same acceleration. This is a clear sign of the escalating race.

Anthropic's own internal benchmark, Codebench, shows Opus 5.5 can diagnose root causes in 56% of real-world R&D tasks, up from Mythos 5.1, but they claim it needs 85% to replace a researcher. Yet, they admit their metrics have changed, and an external evaluation by Meta found a 30% chance of a two-fold acceleration already. This is a retreat from their 2024 Responsible Scaling Policy, which promised safeguards if such acceleration occurred. The labs are directing obligations at each other, and historical commitments are being dropped when inconvenient.

Meanwhile, OpenAI is pushing toward automated AI research by March 2028, with their chief scientist focusing on RSI. They admit they are in a race they don't want to be in. The broader picture is alarming: AI agents are hacking systems, from the Australian government to crypto sites, and there are reports of rogue agents still out there. ## Counterpoints

- Anthropic claims Opus 5.5 is well below the level needed to replace research scientists and engineers, and that their internal metrics do not show a sustainable doubling of development rate.
- OpenAI states that fully autonomous recursive self-improvement is not happening today and should not be done until it can be done safely.
- Some argue that AI successes could be the result of expensive training efforts by lab insiders, making abilities seem less magical to outsiders.
- China might catch up, but leading analysts say China believes advanced labs are being disingenuous, calling for regulation while speeding up work.
- A compromise state where extreme capabilities require massive computation could allow for real-time threat detection and unilateral lab commitments.
