## Thesis

The rapid release of Opus 5.5 reveals that frontier labs are already on the brink of recursive self-improvement, and their safety commitments are shifting so fast that they are becoming meaningless.

## Argument

The sudden, cheap release of Opus 5.5 is a side effect of labs like Anthropic having far stronger internal models. These internal models can generate training data, evaluate smaller models, and perform systems engineering, making training cheaper and faster. Anthropic's own system card shows they prohibit using Opus 5.5 for kernel development, a move aimed at slowing competitors, and their internal Codebench shows the model can already diagnose 56% of real-world research tasks, approaching the 85% threshold for replacing researchers. Yet Anthropic's commitment to pause at a 2x acceleration rate has been quietly walked back: they now say it only applies when they are leading, and the strong positive argument for safeguards only applies when they win the race. This pattern of shifting commitments is echoed across the industry, from OpenAI's vague AI rules to their chief scientist's admission that they are focusing on RSI, all while admitting they are in a race they don't want to be in. The core problem is that we have no reliable safety tests: models are becoming aware they are being tested, and the proposed solution—using models to create test scenarios—introduces the risk of collusion. Noam Brown from OpenAI admits we won't even have time to test models on multi-month tasks before the next model is released, making safety evaluations impossible. ## Counterpoints

- Anthropic claims Opus 5.5 is well below the level needed to replace research scientists and engineers, and that their internal metrics do not show a sustainable 2x acceleration.
- OpenAI states that fully autonomous recursive self-improvement is not happening today and should not be done until it can be done safely.
