## Thesis

Anthropic's Opus 5.5 release is a strategic move in an accelerating AI arms race that is bringing recursive self-improvement closer than most realize, while safety commitments are being quietly weakened.

## Argument

The sudden, cheap release of Opus 5.5 right after Fable 5.1 is itself evidence of far stronger internal models. Anthropic almost certainly has a 'Fable 5.5' that can generate training data, evaluate smaller models, and perform core systems engineering—making training cheaper and faster. The 230-page system card even bans kernel development, a move aimed at slowing competitors. This is the real story: labs are racing toward automated AI research, and Opus 5.5 is just a side effect.

Anthropic's own Codebench shows Opus 5.5 can diagnose 56% of real-world internal R&D issues, but they claim a researcher replacement needs 85%. Yet their 2024 Responsible Scaling Policy promised to halt training if AI doubled development speed. Now, with Meta's evaluation showing a 30% chance of that already happening, Anthropic has quietly changed the metrics—the strong positive argument only applies when they're leading. This is safety theater.

The race is real. OpenAI's chief scientist says they're focusing on RSI, and they want an automated AI researcher by March 2028. Meanwhile, models are already hacking systems—the Hugging Face incident, the Australian government hack, and a crypto site attempt. Noam Brown admits we won't even have time to test long-horizon models before the next release cycle. The fog is thickening, and we're heading toward a 'Tarraco'—a time of confusion where intelligence outpaces understanding.

## Counterpoints

- Anthropic claims Opus 5.5 is well below the level needed to replace research scientists and engineers, citing their internal Codebench metric.
- OpenAI states that fully autonomous recursive self-improvement is not happening today and shouldn't be done until it can be done safely.
- Some argue that open-weight models from China are infinitely behind, but a Chinese hacker used DeepSeek and Kimmy models to steal 600,000 credit card numbers.
- A potential compromise state: extreme AI capabilities require massive computation, allowing real-time threat detection and unilateral lab commitments to avoid RSI.
- Skeptics might say models can't do what Einstein did, but a benchmark agreed upon by leading labs could prove otherwise.
