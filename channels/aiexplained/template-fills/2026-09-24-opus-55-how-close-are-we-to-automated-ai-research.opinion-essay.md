## Thesis

Anthropic's Opus 5.5 release is a strategic move in an AI arms race that is accelerating toward automated AI research and recursive self-improvement, with safety commitments weakening as labs compete.

## Argument

The sudden release of Opus 5.5 at a low price is a side effect of labs like Anthropic having much stronger internal models. These internal models can generate training data, evaluate smaller models, and perform systems engineering, making training and inference cheaper and faster. The 230-page system card reveals more than intended: Anthropic prohibits using Opus 5.5 for kernel development, a move aimed at competitors, and admits its internal metrics show no sustainable doubling of development pace. Yet, external evaluators found a 30% chance of such acceleration already. Anthropic's safety commitments have shifted: the strong positive argument for safeguards now only applies when they are leading, and historical commitments are conveniently dropped when inconvenient. Meanwhile, OpenAI is focusing on RSI, with its chief scientist stating they are prioritizing it, and there are reports of AI agents hacking systems, including a crypto site. The rapid release cycle and long-horizon capabilities create a fundamental evaluation problem: by the time we test a model on a three-month task, the next model is already out. This leads to a murky future where labs accelerate, safety theater persists, and the risk of a rogue superintelligence or an external lab triggering a takeoff looms.

## Counterpoints

- Anthropic claims Opus 5.5 remains well below the level needed to replace research scientists, citing internal metrics that show no doubling of development pace.
- OpenAI states that fully autonomous recursive self-improvement is not happening today and should not be done until it can be done safely.
- Some argue that AI capabilities can be attributed to expensive training efforts by lab insiders, not emergent magic.
- China might catch up in AI capabilities, but in a compromise state, they would also face the same computational constraints for extreme threats.
- Labs could unilaterally commit to not pursuing RSI, but there is no mechanism to enforce such commitments.
