## Thesis

Anthropic's Opus 5.5 release is a strategic move in an AI arms race that is accelerating toward recursive self-improvement, but its own safety commitments are already being quietly abandoned.

## Argument

The mechanism is economic. Opus 5.5 ships at a fraction of Opus 5.1's price, and Anthropic's own internal benchmarks show it is already approaching the level of their research staff. The company's stated safety threshold — that models must score 85% on their internal research benchmark before they'd consider pausing — is a moving goalpost, and the gap between their public claims and internal reality is widening. Anthropic's own policy documents admit that models are already being used to automate AI research, and their chief scientist has publicly stated that recursive self-improvement is the only way to stay at the forefront. The labs are not just racing each other; they are racing their own safety commitments.

## Counterpoints

- Anthropic explicitly states Opus 5.5 remains well below the level needed to replace their research scientists, and that their internal metrics show no sustainable path to automated AI research.
- The labs' public commitments to safety, however vague, still exist, and the threat of regulatory backlash or public outcry could force them to slow down.
- The compromise state — where extreme AI capabilities require massive computation — could allow for real-time threat detection and unilateral lab commitments, preventing the worst outcomes.
