## Thesis

Anthropic's Opus 5.5 release is a side effect of far stronger internal models, and the accelerating race toward automated AI research is eroding safety commitments, making recursive self-improvement dangerously close.

## Argument

The sudden, cheap release of Opus 5.5 is not just a product update; it's a side effect of labs like Anthropic possessing much stronger internal models. These internal models can generate training data, evaluate smaller models, and perform systems engineering, making training and inference cheaper and faster. Anthropic's 230-page system card even prohibits using Opus 5.5 for kernel development, a clear sign they fear other labs using it to accelerate their own research.

This acceleration is measurable. On Terminal Bench Science 0.1, Opus 5.5 lags behind OpenAI's Astra by about 6% but is ahead of Fable 5.1. On Humanity's Last Exam Diamond, Astra leads by about 5%. But the gap is closing, and the release cycle is now a matter of weeks, not months. Anthropic's own Codebench shows Opus 5.5 can diagnose root causes in 56% of real-world internal R&D tasks, up from the semi-mythical Mythos 5.1, but still short of the 85% needed to replace a researcher. Yet, the trajectory is clear.

Meanwhile, safety commitments are eroding. Anthropic's 2024 Responsible Scaling Policy promised to halt training if models doubled development speed, but by July 2026, they've redefined the trigger to only apply when they're leading. They now direct obligations toward each other, and strong historical commitments are simply inconvenient. OpenAI's chief scientist openly says they are focusing research on RSI, and their own rules admit labs must be responsible for automated AI research, but the liability is undefined. We're in a race no one wants to be in, but everyone is running.

The evaluation

## Counterpoints

- Anthropic claims Opus 5.5 remains well below the level needed to replace research scientists and engineers, citing internal metrics that do not show a sustainable doubling of development rate.
- OpenAI's rules state that fully autonomous recursive self-improvement is not happening today and should not be done until it can be done safely, even as they prioritize an automated AI researcher by March 2028.
- Some argue that AI successes could be the result of expensive training efforts by lab insiders, making abilities seem artificial rather than magical, but lab insiders directly observe emergent capabilities.
- China might catch up if labs unilaterally commit to not pursuing RSI, but the host argues that China works on evidence and would see the labs' restraint as credible, eventually reaching the same compromise state.
- A compromise state where extreme capabilities require massive computation could allow real-time threat detection, but self-improving software could design new architectures that minimize computation for threats.
