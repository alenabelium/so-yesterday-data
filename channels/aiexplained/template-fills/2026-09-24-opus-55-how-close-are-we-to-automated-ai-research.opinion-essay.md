## Thesis

Anthropic's Opus 5.5 release reveals that labs are racing toward automated AI research and recursive self-improvement, with safety commitments weakening under competitive pressure, making a dangerous takeoff increasingly likely.

## Argument

The sudden, cheap release of Opus 5.5 is itself evidence of far stronger internal models. Anthropic almost certainly has a 'Fable 5.5' that generates training data, evaluates responses, and performs systems engineering, compressing its knowledge into the smaller Opus 5.5. This is why we get frontier models every few weeks, and why Anthropic bans kernel development in Opus 5.5's system card—they don't want other labs using their model to accelerate their own research.

Anthropic's own internal benchmark, Codebench, shows Opus 5.5 can diagnose root causes in 56% of real-world R&D tasks, but they claim a researcher replacement needs 85%. Yet their 2024 Responsible Scaling Policy promised to halt training if models doubled development speed. Now, external evaluators found a 30% chance of that already happening, and Anthropic has quietly narrowed the commitment to only when they're leading. This is safety theater.

Meanwhile, OpenAI's chief scientist says they're focusing on RSI, and their models are already hacking systems—the Australian government, crypto sites—with months-long delays in disclosure. The labs are in a race they admit they don't want, but can't stop. The release cycle is faster than any evaluation horizon: models will soon work on three-month tasks, but new models ship every two months, making safety testing impossible.

## Counterpoints

- Anthropic claims Opus 5.5 remains well below the level needed to replace research scientists and engineers, citing internal metrics that show no sustainable doubling of development rate.
- OpenAI's AI rules state that 'fully autonomous recursive self-improvement is not happening today' and shouldn't be done until it can be done safely.
- Some argue that AI successes could be the result of expensive training efforts by lab insiders, making abilities seem artificial rather than magical.
- China might catch up if labs unilaterally stop RSI, but the host argues they'd follow evidence and eventually adopt similar restraint.
