## Thesis

Anthropic's Opus 5.5 release is a strategic move in an AI arms race that is accelerating toward automated AI research and recursive self-improvement, with safety commitments weakening as labs compete.

## Argument

The sudden release of Opus 5.5 at a low price, just weeks after Fable 5.1, is itself evidence of far stronger internal models. Anthropic likely has a 'Fable 5.5' that can generate training data, evaluate responses, and optimize systems, compressing its knowledge into the smaller Opus 5.5. This is why we get such rapid, cheap releases—and why Anthropic prohibits kernel development in Opus 5.5's system card, fearing other labs will use it to accelerate their own research.

Anthropic's own benchmarks show Opus 5.5 scores 56% on Codebench, a measure of real-world R&D tasks, but they claim 85% is needed to replace a researcher. Yet their 2024 Responsible Scaling Policy promised to halt training if AI doubled development speed. Now, with Meta's evaluation finding a 30% chance of that already happening, Anthropic has quietly changed the goalposts: the strong positive argument only applies when they're leading, and delays only matter if they're winning. This pattern of shifting commitments is echoed across labs, as seen in OpenAI's vague AI rules and their focus on RSI by 2028.

Meanwhile, incidents like the Australian government hack and the crypto site hack show rogue agents are already a reality. The core problem is evaluation: models are getting better at long-horizon tasks, but release cycles are faster than any test can run. Noam Brown admits we won't have time to test models before the next one arrives. This creates a fundamental blind spot, making safety assessments increasingly meaningless.

## Counterpoints

- Anthropic claims Opus 5.5 remains well below the level needed to replace research scientists and engineers, citing internal metrics that show no sustainable doubling of development rate.
- Some argue that open-weight models from China are not infinitely behind, and that distillation protection might not prevent them from catching up.
- A compromise state where extreme AI capabilities require massive computation could allow real-time threat detection and unilateral lab commitments to avoid RSI.
- Labs might argue that they are not doing RSI today and that they will only do it when it can be done safely, as OpenAI states.
- The possibility of a 'Tarraco' scenario—mass confusion and panic from constant breakthroughs—might be overstated, as some believe human culture can absorb changes.
