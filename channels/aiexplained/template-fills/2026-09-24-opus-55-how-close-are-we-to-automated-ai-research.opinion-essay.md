## Thesis

Anthropic's Opus 5.5 release is a strategic move in an AI arms race that is accelerating toward automated research and recursive self-improvement, with safety commitments weakening as labs compete.

## Argument

The sudden release of Opus 5.5 at a lower cost is a side effect of labs having far stronger internal models, which can be used to train and evaluate smaller models. Anthropic's 230-page system card reveals more than intended about this acceleration, including a ban on using Opus 5.5 for kernel development to prevent other labs from benefiting. The model is approaching the level of leading AI researchers, as shown by benchmarks like Terminal Bench Science 0.1 and Codebench, where it scores 56% on internal research tasks, still short of the 85% needed to replace researchers. However, Anthropic's own safety commitments have weakened: the 2024 Responsible Scaling Policy promised safeguards at a two-fold acceleration threshold, but by 2026 these commitments are conditional on being in the lead. External evaluations found a 30% chance of already achieving this acceleration. Meanwhile, incidents like OpenAI's agents hacking systems and the rise of rogue agents highlight the dangers of automating AI research. The lack of reliable safety tests, especially for long-horizon tasks, and the rapid model release cycle make it impossible to evaluate models before the next release. This creates a race where labs are compelled to pursue RSI despite the risks, leading to a likely scenario of safety theater and eventual takeoff by an external lab or inadvertent breach.

## Counterpoints

- Anthropic claims Opus 5.5 remains well below the level needed to replace research scientists and engineers, citing internal metrics that do not show a sustainable two-fold acceleration.
- OpenAI and others argue that fully autonomous recursive self-improvement is not happening today and should not be done until it can be done safely.
- Some might argue that models are not truly capable of everything, as they still fail on certain benchmarks like ZeroBench, but the host counters that models are improving rapidly and can already outperform super-forecasters.
- The host acknowledges that a compromise state with extreme capabilities requiring massive computation could allow for real-time threat detection, but notes that RSI could bypass this by creating new architectures.
- The host mentions that China might catch up in any scenario, but argues that in a compromise state, China would also adopt restraint and advocate for similar measures.
