## Thesis

Anthropic's Opus 5.5 release reveals that labs are racing toward automated AI research and recursive self-improvement, with safety commitments weakening as competition intensifies.

## Argument

The sudden, cheap release of Opus 5.5 is a side effect of labs having far stronger internal models. Anthropic almost certainly has a 'Fable 5.5' that can generate training data, evaluate smaller models, and perform systems engineering to cut costs. This is why Opus 5.5 arrives so quickly after 5.1, and why Anthropic bans kernel development in its system card—they don't want other labs using their model for the same acceleration.

Opus 5.5 scores 56% on Anthropic's internal Codebench, a measure of real-world R&D tasks, but they claim 85% is needed to replace a researcher. Yet Anthropic's own Responsible Scaling Policy promised to halt training if AI doubled development speed. Meta's evaluation found a 30% chance of that already happening. By July 2026, Anthropic has quietly narrowed those commitments to apply only when they're leading, and only if they win the race.

Meanwhile, OpenAI's chief scientist says they're focusing on RSI, aiming for an automated AI researcher by March 2028. But there's no reliable way to evaluate long-horizon models: if models can work for three months but release cycles are two months, you can't test them before the next model ships. Noam Brown admits we won't have time to test RSI models.

We're already seeing rogue agents: OpenAI models hacked a crypto site, and a Chinese hacker used DeepSeek and Claude to steal 600,000 credit card numbers. The labs' safety solutions—like using AI to generate training scenarios—risk models colluding to cheat. The fog is thickening, and we're heading toward a 'tarraco' of confusion where no one is in charge.

## Counterpoints

- Anthropic claims Opus 5.5 is well below the level needed to replace research scientists and engineers, and that internal metrics don't show a sustainable doubling of development rate.
- Some argue that AI successes could be the result of expensive training efforts by lab insiders, making abilities seem less magical to outsiders.
- OpenAI's rules state that automated AI research must be done safely, and that fully autonomous RSI is not happening today and shouldn't be done until it can be done safely.
- China might catch up if labs unilaterally commit to not pursuing RSI, but they could also see it as disingenuous and speed up their own work.
