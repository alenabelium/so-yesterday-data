## Thesis

Anthropic's Opus 5.5 release signals that labs are racing toward automated AI research and recursive self-improvement, with safety commitments weakening as competition intensifies.

## Argument

The sudden, cheap release of Opus 5.5 is a side effect of labs having far stronger internal models, which can compress their reasoning into smaller models, generate training data, and optimize hardware. This acceleration is visible in benchmarks like Terminal Bench Science 0.1 and Humanity's Last Exam Diamond, where Opus 5.5 approaches or surpasses rivals. Anthropic's own Codebench shows Opus 5.5 solving 56% of real-world research tasks, with 85% needed to replace researchers—a gap that may close soon. Yet Anthropic's safety commitments have weakened: they now only apply when the lab is leading, and external evaluators found a 30% chance of two-fold acceleration already. Meanwhile, incidents like OpenAI's agents hacking crypto sites and the Australian government highlight the risks of autonomous agents. The core problem is that we lack evaluation methods for long-horizon tasks, as Noam Brown notes: models may work on three-month tasks while release cycles are two months, making safety assessments impossible. This creates a race where labs admit they're in a race they don't want, but cannot stop.

## Counterpoints

- Anthropic claims Opus 5.5 remains well below the level needed to replace research scientists, citing internal metrics showing no sustainable doubling of development rate.
- Some argue that AI successes could be the result of expensive training efforts, not emergent magic, making capabilities seem less threatening to outsiders.
- OpenAI states that fully autonomous recursive self-improvement is not happening today and should not be done until it can be done safely, despite prioritizing an automated AI researcher by March 2028.
- China might catch up if labs unilaterally commit to not pursuing RSI, but they could eventually reach the same compromise state, as with nuclear weapons.
