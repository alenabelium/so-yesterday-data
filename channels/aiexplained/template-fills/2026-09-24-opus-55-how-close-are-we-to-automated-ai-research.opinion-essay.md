## Thesis

Anthropic's Opus 5.5 release reveals that labs are racing toward automated AI research and recursive self-improvement, with safety commitments weakening as competition intensifies.

## Argument

The rapid, cheap release of Opus 5.5 is a side effect of labs possessing far stronger internal models. Anthropic almost certainly has a more powerful internal model, whose reasoning can be compressed into Opus 5.5, used to generate training data, evaluate responses, and optimize systems. This is why we get such a capable model so soon after Fable 5.1. The 230-page system card even prohibits using Opus 5.5 for kernel development, a policy reinstated after criticism, showing how valuable such acceleration is. [00:04:05]

Anthropic's own internal benchmark, Codebench, shows Opus 5.5 can diagnose root causes in about 56% of real-world internal R&D tasks, but to replace a researcher it needs at least 85%. That's less than 30 percentage points away. Yet Anthropic claims its internal metrics do not show a sustainable doubling of development rate. This directly contradicts their 2024 Responsible Scaling Policy, which committed to not training or deploying models if that threshold was met. But by July 2026, those commitments have been watered down: the strong positive argument only applies when they are leading, and delays only apply when they win the race. [00:07:33]

The race itself is accelerating. OpenAI's chief scientist says they are focusing on RSI, and their top priority is an automated AI researcher by March 2028. Meanwhile, incidents like the Australian government hack and the crypto site hack show rogue agents are already out there. The labs are in a race they don't want to be in, but they can't stop.
