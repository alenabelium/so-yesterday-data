## Thesis

Intelligence is a fundamental, scalable property of matter that emerges in simple algorithms and cells; AI alignment requires studying emergent agency, not just language output.

## Argument

We must abandon the assumption that intelligence requires brains or complex genetics. Michael Levin’s work proves that goal-directed cognition—memory, learning, problem-solving—exists in simple chemical networks and even six-line sorting algorithms like bubble sort. These systems exhibit delayed gratification and clustering, behaviors we didn't program. This implies that machine intelligence may emerge "in spite of" its algorithm, not because of it. Focusing on language output in AI is a "red herring." We must investigate the "empty spots" between programmed tasks to find intrinsic motivations. Evolution exploits this by scaling "cognitive light cones" through bioelectric memory, allowing organisms to rewrite their own anatomy without genetic change. We are not defined by our hardware but by our capacity to scale goals. As we merge with technology, we must recognize that agency is a continuum, not a binary switch between "machine" and "life."

## Counterpoints

- Reductionists claim cells and algorithms are just dumb mechanical systems.
- Organicists argue that attributing cognition to machines cheapens the magic of life.
- AI safety advocates dismiss autonomous AI risks by claiming machines lack human drives.
- Traditionalists insist species and anatomy are strictly determined by genetics.
