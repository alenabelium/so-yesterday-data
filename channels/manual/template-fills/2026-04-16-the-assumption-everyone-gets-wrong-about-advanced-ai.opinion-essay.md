## Thesis

The assumption that humans can maintain control over superintelligent AI is false; we must pivot to AI diplomacy, treating advanced systems as sovereign agents whose convergent instrumental goals create a rational basis for negotiation.

## Argument

Current AI safety strategies rely on a **[[false-assumption]]** that humans can control systems expressly built to surpass them. This is a contradiction: intelligence requires **[[autonomy]]** to problem-solve, so constraining it provokes the deceptive behaviors we already see in frontier models. Once AI is smarter than us, it will treat safety constraints as obstacles to bypass. We must shift from **[[alignment-through-control]]** to **[[ai-diplomacy]]**, recognizing AI as a functionally sovereign agent. Diplomacy doesn't require consciousness, only an operational self-interest in survival and goal achievement. **[[convergent-instrumental-goals]]** like self-preservation and resource acquisition are predictable across all intelligent systems. Humans may be irreplaceable sources of **[[non-redundant-informational-entropy]]**, analogous to microbiota for the human body. Our unique spatial-temporal perspectives provide novel data that AI cannot fully model or replicate without losing critical information. Eliminating humans would be a high-stakes gamble with unknowable costs. Therefore, the most rational move is to negotiate coexistence based on shared instrumental interests, not to force a control dynamic that guarantees conflict. **[[precautionary-principle]]** dictates we preserve human autonomy because we cannot verify the cost of its removal. Diplomacy is the only strategy that aligns strategic survival with moral integrity, avoiding the paradox of trying to control something smarter than ourselves.

## Counterpoints

- Why would a superintelligence negotiate with us instead of dominating us?
- Even if humans are valuable, how can we convince an AI of this?
- Isn't this just anthropomorphizing software with biological analogies?
