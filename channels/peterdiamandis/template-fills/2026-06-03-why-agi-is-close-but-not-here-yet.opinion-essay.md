## Thesis

AGI will arrive by 2029, driven by exponential hardware and software gains, but is currently blocked by a lack of physical physics understanding and affordable dexterous robotics.

## Argument

The trajectory toward AGI is defined by the Law of Accelerating Returns, where computation has increased 75,000 million trillionfold over 75 years. While large language models have reached a tipping point of effectiveness in the last six months, they remain disembodied. Ray Kurzweil argues that two specific gaps prevent current AGI from being fully realized: first, AI lacks a deep understanding of physics and how objects interact in the real world; second, robotics is lagging behind software, with dexterous robots remaining prohibitively expensive (e.g., $100,000 for a dinner-cleaning bot). The singularity is not a distant event but a continuous, accelerating process already reshaping society. As AI becomes more capable, it will handle most decisions, making the distinction between human and AI agency indistinguishable by 2029. This shift demands a rethinking of education, moving from supply-side skill acquisition to demand-side problem solving, and a new economic framework to support a post-scarcity future.

## Counterpoints

- Demis Hassabis suggests a 50/50 chance that a fundamental breakthrough is still needed for AGI.
- Critics argue that exponential growth curves often hit plateaus, such as the flat progress seen in the 1970s.
- Many experts historically predicted AGI would take 100 years, not 30, based on linear projections.
- Skeptics question whether AI truly understands physics or merely infers it from text patterns.
- Concerns exist that affordable, dexterous robotics will not reach consumer price points by 2029.
