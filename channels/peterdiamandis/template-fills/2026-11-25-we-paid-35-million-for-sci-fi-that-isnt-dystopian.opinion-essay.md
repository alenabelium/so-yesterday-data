## Thesis

We must replace dystopian sci-fi with positive, techno-optimistic stories because the media we consume trains our minds like neural networks, shaping the future we build.

## Argument

The mechanism is cognitive training. Our brains are neural networks, just like Grok or Claude, and we train them by what we watch, listen to, and interact with. If we feed these models positive visions of the future, we train ourselves to build that future. The Future Vision X Prize proves this is possible: over 5,000 people submitted films, and the five finalists all explore human-AI collaboration and collective problem-solving. The winner, 'The Gifted,' reframes the villain as fear of AI, not AI itself. This is the key move: the antagonist is not technology but our own fear, and the film shows that assuming positive intentions can lead to positive outcomes. The other finalists reinforce this: 'Slingshot' uses a black hole slingshot as a metaphor for tackling AI and geopolitical disintegration through cooperation; 'Senti' tells the origin story of the singularity as a friendship between human and AI; 'The Thorne Protocol' encourages us to look ahead, not into the ditch; 'Nobody Dies on Mars' explores empathy across difference. These stories are not escapism; they are training data for a better future. We need to make positive stories popular again, and we will hold the competition again in 2027. And you can invest in the winning film, like investing in Star Trek at the beginning, to be part of this movement.

## Counterpoints

- Where is the villain in a positive story? The default is to make AI the antagonist, but we can't have just one side; we must have optimism.
- Some might think these are just sci-fi fantasies, but 'Slingshot' is based on real MIT research on primordial black holes, not absurd physics.
- Critics might say dystopian stories are more realistic, but the host argues that assuming negative intentions only yields negative results.
