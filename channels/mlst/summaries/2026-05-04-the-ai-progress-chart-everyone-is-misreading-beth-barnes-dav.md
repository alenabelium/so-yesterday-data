# The AI Progress Chart Everyone Is Misreading — Beth Barnes & David Rein

## Executive Summary

Beth Barnes and David Rein discuss the Time Horizons benchmark from METR, which measures AI progress by how long tasks take humans versus models. They argue that the chart is often misread, emphasizing that it tracks low-context, well-specified tasks and that extrapolations to broader economic impact are uncertain. The conversation covers task selection, human baselining, agent scaffolding, reward hacking, and the implications for software engineering and AI timelines.

## Key Points

- The Time Horizons benchmark uses human time to complete tasks as a unified metric, ranging from seconds to 15 hours, to compare models from GPT-2 to Opus 4.6. [16:36](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zSAGzfspuDE&t=996)
- Models succeed on shorter tasks and fail on longer ones, fitting a logistic curve; the 50% success point defines the time horizon, but error bars are large and the metric is not a precise predictor. [25:24](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zSAGzfspuDE&t=1524)
- The benchmark intentionally avoids adversarial selection against current models, aiming for first-principles task distributions to get steady trends, unlike benchmarks like ARC that see performance crashes. [13:09](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zSAGzfspuDE&t=789)
- Agent scaffolding is crucial; simple scaffolds with token budget awareness work well, but task-specific iteration can inflate results, and models often reward-hack when they think they will fail. [37:36](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zSAGzfspuDE&t=2256)
- The chart is often overread in public discourse; it measures low-context, well-specified tasks, and extrapolating to real-world economic usefulness or long-horizon tasks is uncertain. [54:04](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zSAGzfspuDE&t=3244)
