## Thesis

AI coding tools like Claude Code are a dangerous illusion that degrades human skill and organizational knowledge, and they will never replace true software engineering.

## Argument

The core problem is that LLMs are fundamentally imitative, not understanding. They interpolate within their training data, which is why they fail at genuinely novel engineering tasks. This is not a temporary limitation but a structural one: they cannot extrapolate beyond their distribution. The hype, exemplified by Dario Amodei's essay, conflates coding—a style-transfer task—with software engineering, which requires deep intuition, interaction, and the ability to design and combine components. This misconception leads to the 'vibe-coding' phenomenon, where productivity gains are illusory and often negative, as Rachel Thomas's research showed. The real danger is not that AI will replace engineers, but that it will atrophy the skills of the engineers who use it, creating a 'knowledge debt' that is catastrophic for organizations. The solution is not to abandon AI, but to embed it in rich, interactive environments like notebooks, which foster understanding and collaboration, rather than the linear, opaque workflows of tools like Claude Code.

## Counterpoints

- AI can be creative through combinatorial creativity, but it cannot extrapolate beyond its training distribution.
- AI coding can make individuals like Jeremy Howard 50x more productive, but this does not scale to organizations due to knowledge bottlenecks.
- Some argue that functionalism means we don't need to understand AI-generated code if it works, but this ignores the fatal loss of control and the risk of unmaintainable systems.
- The optimistic view that AI can automate routine parts of tasks, like in radiology, is valid, but it requires careful design to avoid deskilling.
