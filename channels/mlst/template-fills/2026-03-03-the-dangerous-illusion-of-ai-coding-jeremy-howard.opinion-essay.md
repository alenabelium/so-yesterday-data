## Thesis

AI coding tools like Claude Code create a dangerous illusion of control and productivity that degrades human skills and organizational knowledge, and they will never replace true software engineering.

## Argument

The core problem is that AI coding tools are fundamentally different from software engineering. Coding is a style-transfer task—interpolating between training data—while engineering requires deep intuition, interaction, and understanding. This is why LLMs fail at novel problems: they can only combine what they've seen, not create genuinely new abstractions. The evidence is everywhere: the 'vibe coding' productivity gains are illusory, as studies show tiny real growth in shipped software. The real bottleneck is knowledge—it's embodied, not transferable. When you delegate cognitive tasks to AI, you blur organizational knowledge and lose the ability to adapt. The slot machine analogy is apt: you tinker with prompts and MCPs, pull the lever, and get a piece of code nobody understands. This is loss disguised as winning. The solution isn't to abandon AI but to build rich, interactive environments like notebooks where humans and AI can collaborate, maintain understanding, and develop skills. The future belongs to those who focus on their 'slope'—growth—not the 'intercept' of current AI capabilities.

## Counterpoints

- Some argue LLMs are creative, but they only do combinatorial creativity within training data, not extrapolation beyond it.
- Dario Amodei and others claim AI will automate all coding, but they confuse coding with software engineering; most engineering is not writing code.
- Optimists say functionalism means we don't need to understand AI-generated code if it works, but this leads to fatal loss of control and technical debt.
- People point to AI solving specific problems (like the IPython kernel fix), but these are interpolation tasks, not novel engineering.
- Some say AI tools can be used for learning, but they often lead to autopilot mode and skill degradation, not growth.
