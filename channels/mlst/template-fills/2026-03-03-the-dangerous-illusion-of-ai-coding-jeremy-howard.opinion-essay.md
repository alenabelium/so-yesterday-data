## Thesis

AI coding tools are a dangerous illusion: they generate code without understanding, degrade human skills, and centralize power, making them a threat to software engineering and society.

## Argument

The core problem is that LLMs only imitate understanding, not possess it. They excel at combinatorial creativity—interpolating within their training data—but fail at true software engineering, which requires deep intuition, interaction, and the ability to go beyond existing patterns. This is why they are poor at designing novel solutions and why the 'clean room' compiler claim is misleading: it's just style transfer from existing code.

This illusion is compounded by the 'slot machine' effect: users feel control while tinkering with prompts, but the process is stochastic and often yields code no one understands. The result is a degradation of human skills and organizational knowledge. As people delegate cognitive tasks, they stop developing their own understanding, leading to a 'debt in understanding' and a loss of the very expertise needed to guide AI effectively.

Moreover, the hype is not backed by evidence. Productivity gains are often overstated; studies show minimal real-world improvement. The real bottleneck is knowledge sharing and the inability to scale individual productivity to teams. The solution is not to abandon AI but to create rich, interactive environments—like notebooks—that foster human-AI collaboration and continuous learning, rather than the linear, file-based workflows of tools like Claude Code.

Finally, the centralization of AI power is a grave risk. Concentrating such technology in the hands of a few companies or governments enables power-hungry individuals to monopolize control, threatening civilization. The answer is to distribute AI

## Counterpoints

- Some argue LLMs are creative and can solve novel problems, but this is combinatorial creativity within training data, not true extrapolation.
- Anthropic's Dario Amodei claims AI makes engineers vastly more productive, but this is based on anecdotal evidence and contradicted by research showing minimal real-world gains.
- The 'functionalist' view holds that if AI-generated code works, it doesn't matter if we understand it, but this ignores the loss of control and the inability to maintain or extend such code.
- Some believe AI can automate routine tasks and free experts to focus on higher-level work, but this only works if the automation is reliable and doesn't erode the expert's own skills.
- The existential risk narrative is overblown; the real danger is the centralization of power and the degradation of human competence.
