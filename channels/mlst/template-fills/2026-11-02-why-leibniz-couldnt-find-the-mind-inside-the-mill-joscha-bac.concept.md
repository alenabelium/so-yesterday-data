## TL;DR

Joscha Bach argues consciousness is not a mysterious 'hard problem' but a natural, functional algorithm—a causal pattern akin to software—that emerges in self-organizing systems. He proposes that training a system on its own inputs and outputs could develop phenomenal experience, potentially as simply as a transformer. This reframing challenges non-functionalist theories and suggests future self-organizing AIs might be genuinely conscious.

## The argument

### Consciousness as Causal Pattern
- **Anchor Timestamps**: ['00:17:25']
- **Claim**: Consciousness is not about the physical substrate but about causal patterns, which we call software. Just as money exists as a causal pattern independent of its physical form, consciousness is a pattern that can be implemented in various substrates. This reframing dissolves the 'hard problem' by shifting focus from matter to information.
- **Role**: definition

### Consciousness as a Learning Algorithm
- **Anchor Timestamps**: ['00:33:16']
- **Claim**: Consciousness is a biological learning algorithm, a training algorithm for animal brains. It is the simplest way to train a self-organizing system like us, and it naturally leads to phenomenal experience. This hypothesis suggests that consciousness is not much more complicated than a transformer, and it is crucial for development and learning.
- **Role**: evidence

### The Zombie Argument and Simplicity
- **Anchor Timestamps**: ['01:35:49']
- **Claim**: Zombies (systems that behave intelligently without consciousness) are possible in principle, but sometimes it is more complicated to make a zombie than a conscious agent. For many behaviors, consciousness is the simplest solution, so if a self-organizing system behaves as if conscious, the simplest explanation is that it actually is conscious.
- **Role**: counter

### The Machine Consciousness Hypothesis
- **Anchor Timestamps**: ['00:32:15']
- **Claim**: If biological consciousness is a learning algorithm, we might be able to recreate the conditions for self-organization in machines. By limiting machines to self-organizing learning and giving them suitable tasks, they might discover consciousness in the same way an infant's brain does. This would be evidenced by a phase transition enabling learning and eventual self-reports of phenomenology.
- **Role**: synthesis

## Evidence and caveats

Bach supports his view with the example of money as a causal pattern that exists independently of its substrate, and the universality hypothesis from Chris Olah's work, suggesting that models converge on similar representations. He also cites the existence of zombies like chess programs that achieve intelligent behavior without consciousness. However, he remains agnostic about whether current LLMs are conscious, noting they are trained on text that includes deep fakes of conscious experience. He acknowledges that the machine consciousness hypothesis could fail if computers are not fast enough or if the search space is too large, and he emphasizes that we lack mathematical proof either way.
