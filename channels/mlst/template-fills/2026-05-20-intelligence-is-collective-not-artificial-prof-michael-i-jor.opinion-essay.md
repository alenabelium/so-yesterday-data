## Thesis

Intelligence is fundamentally collective and economic; AGI hype is science fiction that demoralizes researchers by ignoring the necessary integration of machine learning with statistics, game theory, and human-centered incentives.

## Argument

The current AI discourse is dominated by a "science fiction" narrative of superintelligence and extinction that serves no constructive purpose. This framing actively harms young researchers by suggesting their work is either dangerous or pointless, diverting attention from the real task: building trustworthy, human-centered ecosystems.

[[collective-intelligence]] The host argues that intelligence is not an internal property of a model but emerges from social and economic interactions. We are social animals who aggregate opinions; thus, AI must be viewed through [[economic-thinking]] and [[game-theory]]. Gradient descent on data is insufficient without understanding the incentives of the agents producing that data.

[[uncertainty-quantification]] A critical failure in current systems is the lack of rigorous uncertainty quantification. Using [[alphafold]] as an example, the host demonstrates that foundation models can be highly biased on novel queries because they lack ground truth for edge cases. We need methods like [[prediction-powered-inference]] to merge predictive power with statistical rigor, ensuring error bars cover the truth rather than just reflecting training distribution biases.

[[mechanism-design]] The solution lies in [[mechanism-design]] and contract theory. Instead of treating data as a free resource, we must build [[data-markets]] where users are compensated for their privacy and labor. ## Counterpoints

- AGI proponents argue that scaling foundation models will naturally lead to superintelligence and solve all problems through recursive self-improvement.
- Some believe that behavioral data collected at scale is sufficient to model human preferences without needing explicit economic or game-theoretic frameworks.
- Critics of the host's view might argue that mechanistic interpretability is necessary to understand how these systems actually reason, rather than treating them as black boxes.
