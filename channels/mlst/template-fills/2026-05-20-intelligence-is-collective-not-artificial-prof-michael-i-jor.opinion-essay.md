## Thesis

Intelligence is a collective, economic phenomenon rooted in human interaction and market incentives, not an artificial property of isolated algorithms.

## Argument

The current AI discourse is demoralizing young researchers by promoting science fiction narratives of superintelligence and extinction, which distract from the real work of building trustworthy systems. Jordan argues that intelligence emerges from social aggregation, game theory, and economic incentives rather than neural network scaling alone. He critiques the 'soup' of LLMs for lacking mechanistic interpretability and uncertainty quantification, noting that while they predict well, they fail to provide actionable explanations or handle distributional shifts without ground truth data. The solution lies in a collectivist framework: integrating machine learning with economics and statistics to create ecosystems where humans are valued producers and consumers. This requires mechanism design—such as data markets with differential privacy and incentive alignment—to ensure systems serve human welfare rather than extracting value through monopolistic practices. True progress comes from understanding the statistical contracts and equilibria that govern these interactions, not from anthropomorphizing models or chasing AGI.

## Counterpoints

- AGI hype is a PR distortion that harms young researchers by promoting alarmist or exuberant narratives over practical engineering.
- LLMs are merely predictive 'soup' lacking true understanding; they mimic confidence without quantifying epistemic uncertainty.
- Silicon Valley's detachment from economic reality ignores the need for data markets and incentive alignment to create value.
- Anthropomorphizing AI as 'intelligent' is a distraction; we should focus on building systems that reduce uncertainty in human contexts.
