## Thesis

Wall Street is financing the AI buildout with opaque, off-balance-sheet debt that mirrors 2008, and the weakest link in this chain is publicly listed Bitcoin miners.

## Argument

The mechanism is credit. Morgan Stanley puts global data center capex at $2.9 trillion for 2025-2028, but hyperscaler cash flow covers only $1.4 trillion, leaving a $1.5 trillion financing gap. That gap is being filled by private credit, securitized products, and off-balance-sheet vehicles. Meta's Hyperion Campus is a prime example: $27 billion of debt issued from a bankruptcy-remote vehicle, rated A+ by S&P, with a 2049 maturity, while Meta leases the site on rolling four-year terms to keep the debt off its books. Oracle carries $248 billion in lease commitments through third-party vehicles, and both S&P and Moody's have it on negative watch. The Nikkei investigation found $1.65 trillion in off-balance-sheet AI obligations at the five biggest tech firms, growing eightfold in four years.

The collateral is the problem. GPUs depreciate roughly 50% in three years, but the debt used to fund them matures in 5 to 24 years. That mismatch is the core risk. The optimists point to the cash generation of hyperscalers and investment-grade ratings on AI debt, but the ratings track the counterparty, not the asset. CoreWeave's first facility was A3 because it was anchored by Meta; seven weeks later, a facility secured by non-investment-grade customers got Ba2. Nvidia's platform to mobilize $500 billion, compared by Larry Fink to the creation of mortgage-backed securities, only works if chips hold their value—something never seen through a full tech cycle.

## Counterpoints

- Hyperscalers are enormously cash generative at the parent level, which could support the debt.
- AI debt is investment-grade, with ratings like A+ and A3, suggesting the risk is manageable.
- The AI buildout is a new era for markets, not a repeat of 2008.
