## Thesis

Wall Street is financing the AI buildout with opaque, off-balance-sheet debt that mirrors 2008, and the weakest link in this chain is publicly traded Bitcoin miners.

## Argument

The mechanism is credit. Morgan Stanley puts 2025-2028 data center capex at $2.9 trillion, but hyperscaler cash flow covers only $1.4 trillion, leaving a $1.5 trillion financing gap. That gap is being filled by private credit, securitized products, and off-balance-sheet vehicles. Meta's Hyperion Campus is a prime example: a bankruptcy-remote vehicle called Big Net Investors LLC issued $27 billion in debt, rated A+ by S&P, with a 6.58% coupon and maturity in 2049. Meta leases the site back on rolling four-year terms, keeping the debt off its books. Oracle carries $248 billion in lease commitments through third-party vehicles, and Anthropic's chip-backed financing is structured through an SPV with Broadcom guaranteeing part of the senior slice. The Nikkei investigation found $1.65 trillion in off-balance-sheet AI obligations at the five biggest tech firms, growing eightfold in four years.

The collateral problem is the mismatch: GPUs depreciate ~50% in three years, but the debt matures in 5 to 24 years. Ratings track the counterparty, not the asset—CoreWeave's A3 facility was anchored by Meta, but a Ba2 facility was tied to non-investment-grade customers. Nvidia's platform to mobilize $500 billion, compared by Larry Fink to mortgage-backed securities, assumes chips hold value, but nobody has seen GPU residuals through a full tech cycle.

## Counterpoints

- Hyperscalers are enormously cash generative at the parent level, so the debt is backed by strong cash flows.
- The debt is investment-grade (e.g., CoreWeave's A3 facility) and secured against high-performance computing, not subprime mortgages.
- This is a new era for markets, not a repeat of 2008—financial engineering has evolved.
- Nobody has seen GPU residual values through a full technology cycle, so the depreciation risk is unproven.
