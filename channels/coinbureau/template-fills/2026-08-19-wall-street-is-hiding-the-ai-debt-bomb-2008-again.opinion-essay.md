## Thesis

Wall Street is financing the AI buildout with opaque, off-balance-sheet debt that mirrors the 2008 crisis, and if GPU values collapse before the debt is repriced, the losses will hit the weakest link: publicly listed Bitcoin miners.

## Argument

The mechanism is credit. Morgan Stanley puts global data center capex at $2.9T for 2025-2028, but hyperscaler cash flow covers only $1.4T, leaving a $1.5T financing gap. That gap is being filled by private credit and securitization, not balance-sheet debt. Direct private credit to AI has exploded from nothing to $200B, and global AI debt issuance is tracking toward $570B in 2026. The game is keeping this debt off balance sheets. Meta's Hyperion campus is financed through a bankruptcy-remote vehicle, Big Net Investors LLC, which issued $27B of debt rated A+ by S&P, with Meta leasing back on rolling four-year terms. Oracle carries $248B in lease commitments through third-party vehicles. The Nikkei investigation found $1.65T of off-balance-sheet AI obligations at the five biggest tech firms, growing eightfold in four years. This is the same trick as 2006: pool weaker credit, slice into tranches, get a rating. But the collateral is the problem. GPUs depreciate 50% in three years, yet the debt matures in 5 to 24 years. CoreWeave's ratings track the counterparty, not the asset: a Meta-anchored facility got A3, but a facility backed by non-investment-grade customers got Ba2. Nvidia's platform to mobilize $500B, with Larry Fink comparing it to MBS creation, only works if chips hold value. Bitcoin miners, converting to AI landlords to survive mining at a loss, are the most leveraged link. ## Counterpoints

- Hyperscalers are enormously cash generative at the parent level, which could support the debt.
- The debt is investment-grade and secured against high-performance computing, as seen with CoreWeave's A3-rated facility.
- Optimists argue this is a new era for markets, not a repeat of 2008.
