## Thesis

Wall Street is lending against AI through opaque debt structures that echo 2008, and the collateral mismatch could trigger a broader financial shock.

## Argument

The mechanism is credit. Morgan Stanley puts the AI financing gap at $1.5 trillion, and Wall Street is filling it with off-balance-sheet vehicles that keep debt hidden. Meta's Hyperion Campus is a case in point: a bankruptcy-remote vehicle called Big Net Investors LLC issued $27 billion of debt, rated A+ by S&P, with a coupon of 6.58% and a maturity in 2049. Meta leases the site back on rolling four-year terms, keeping the debt off its books. Oracle is doing the same with $248 billion of lease commitments through third-party vehicles. The Nikkei investigation found the five biggest tech firms have $1.65 trillion of off-balance-sheet AI obligations, growing eightfold in four years.

The collateral is the problem. GPUs depreciate 50% in three years, but the debt matures in 5 to 24 years. Ratings track the counterparty, not the asset. CoreWeave's first facility was A3 because it was anchored by Meta; a later one was Ba2 because the tenants weren't investment grade. Nvidia's platform to mobilize $500 billion is the securitization moment, with Larry Fink comparing it to the creation of mortgage-backed securities.

Bitcoin miners are the weakest link. They converted to AI landlords to survive, taking on 12- to 20-year obligations against hardware with a three-year half-life. Core Scientific signed a $10.2 billion deal with CoreWeave, then sold 1,900 Bitcoin to fund the conversion. ## Counterpoints

- Hyperscalers are enormously cash generative at the parent level, which could support the debt.
- The AI buildout is a new era for markets, not a repeat of 2008.
- Nobody has seen GPU residual values through a full technology cycle, so the optimists rely on things playing out as planned.
