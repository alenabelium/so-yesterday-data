## Thesis

Wall Street is financing the AI buildout with opaque, off-balance-sheet debt that echoes 2008, and the weakest link in this chain is publicly listed Bitcoin miners.

## Argument

The mechanism is credit. Morgan Stanley puts global data center capex at $2.9T for 2025-2028, but hyperscaler cash flow covers only $1.4T, leaving a $1.5T financing gap. That gap is being filled by private credit, securitized products, and off-balance-sheet vehicles. Meta's Hyperion Campus is a prime example: a bankruptcy-remote vehicle called Big Net Investors LLC issued $27B of debt, rated A+ by S&P, with a 6.58% coupon and maturity in 2049. Meta leases the site back on rolling four-year terms, keeping the debt off its books. Oracle is doing the same with $248B in lease commitments through third-party vehicles. The Nikkei investigation found $1.65T in off-balance-sheet AI obligations at the five biggest tech firms, growing eightfold in four years.

The collateral underneath this debt is the problem. GPUs depreciate roughly 50% in three years, but the debt matures in 5, 10, or 24 years. That mismatch is the core risk. The optimists argue that hyperscalers are cash-generative and that investment-grade ratings on AI debt are justified. But CoreWeave's ratings track the counterparty, not the asset: a facility anchored by Meta got A3/A-, while one tied to non-investment-grade customers got Ba2/BB+. Nvidia's platform to mobilize $500B, compared by Larry Fink to mortgage-backed securities, only works if chips hold their value—something never tested through a full technology cycle.

## Counterpoints

- Hyperscalers are enormously cash generative at the parent level, so the debt is safe.
- Investment-grade ratings on AI debt (e.g., CoreWeave's A3/A-) show the risk is manageable.
- Nobody will default on the AI buildout; the exposure just relocates.
- This is a new era for markets, not a repeat of 2008.
