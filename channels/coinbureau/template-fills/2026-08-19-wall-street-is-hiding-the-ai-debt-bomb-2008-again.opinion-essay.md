## Thesis

Wall Street is financing the AI buildout with opaque, off-balance-sheet debt that echoes 2008, and the weakest link in this chain is publicly traded Bitcoin miners.

## Argument

The mechanism is credit. Morgan Stanley puts global data center capex at $2.9 trillion for 2025–2028, but hyperscaler cash flow covers only $1.4 trillion, leaving a $1.5 trillion financing gap. That gap is being filled by private credit, securitized products, and off-balance-sheet vehicles. Meta's Hyperion Campus is a prime example: a bankruptcy-remote vehicle called Big Net Investors LLC issued $27 billion of debt, rated A+ by S&P, with a 6.58% coupon and maturity in 2049. Meta leases the site back on rolling four-year terms, keeping the debt off its books. Oracle is doing the same with $248 billion in lease commitments through third-party vehicles. The Nikkei investigation found the five biggest tech firms have $1.65 trillion in off-balance-sheet AI obligations, growing eightfold in four years.

The collateral problem is the mismatch. GPUs depreciate roughly 50% in three years, but the debt used to fund them matures in 5, 10, or 24 years. Ratings track the counterparty, not the asset: CoreWeave's March 2026 facility was rated A3 because it was anchored by Meta, but a May facility tied to non-investment-grade customers got Ba2. Nvidia's platform to mobilize $500 billion of third-party capital, with BlackRock's Larry Fink comparing it to the creation of mortgage-backed securities, only deepens the exposure.

## Counterpoints

- Hyperscalers are enormously cash generative at the parent level, which could absorb shocks.

- Some say this is simply a new era for markets, not a repeat of 2008.
