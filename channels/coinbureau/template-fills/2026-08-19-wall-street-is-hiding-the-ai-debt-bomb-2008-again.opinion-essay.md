## Thesis

Wall Street is lending against AI through opaque, off-balance-sheet debt structures that echo 2006, and if GPU values collapse before the debt is repriced, the losses will hit the weakest link—publicly listed Bitcoin miners.

## Argument

The mechanism is credit. Morgan Stanley puts global data center capex at $2.9 trillion for 2025-2028, but hyperscaler cash flow covers only $1.4 trillion, leaving a $1.5 trillion financing gap. That gap is being filled by private credit, securitized products, and off-balance-sheet vehicles. Meta's Hyperion Campus is a prime example: a bankruptcy-remote vehicle called Big Net Investors LLC issued $27 billion of debt, rated A+ by S&P, with a fixed coupon of 6.58% and maturity in 2049. Meta leases the site back on rolling four-year terms, keeping the debt off its books. Oracle is doing something similar with $248 billion in lease commitments through third-party vehicles. The Nikkei investigation found the five biggest tech firms have $1.65 trillion in off-balance-sheet AI obligations, growing eightfold in four years.

The collateral problem is the mismatch: GPUs depreciate about 50% in three years, but the debt matures in 5 to 24 years. Operators depreciate GPUs over 4-6 years, but market data shows real economic decay is faster. The ratings track the counterparty, not the asset—CoreWeave's debt rating moved from A3 to Ba2 when the tenant changed from Meta to non-investment-grade customers. Nvidia's platform to mobilize $500 billion of third-party capital, with Larry Fink comparing it to mortgage-backed securities, shows the scale of the financial engineering.

## Counterpoints

- Hyperscalers are enormously cash generative at the parent level, which could support the debt.
- The debt is rated investment-grade, like CoreWeave's A3 facility anchored by a Meta contract.
- Optimists rely on GPU residual values holding through a full technology cycle, which hasn't been tested.
- Some argue this is a new era for markets, not a repeat of 2008.
