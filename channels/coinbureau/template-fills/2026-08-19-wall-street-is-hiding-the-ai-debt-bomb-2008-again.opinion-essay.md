## Thesis

Wall Street is lending against AI through opaque, off-balance-sheet debt that echoes 2008, and the weakest link in this chain is publicly listed Bitcoin miners.

## Argument

The mechanism is credit. Morgan Stanley puts global data center capex at $2.9 trillion for 2025-2028, but hyperscaler cash flow covers only $1.4 trillion, leaving a $1.5 trillion financing gap. That gap is being filled by private credit and securitized products, not traditional balance-sheet debt. The trick is to keep the debt off the books: Meta's Hyperion Campus is financed through a bankruptcy-remote vehicle, Big Net Investors LLC, with $27 billion of debt rated A+ and maturing in 2049, while Meta leases the site back on rolling four-year terms. Oracle carries $248 billion in lease commitments through third-party vehicles. The Nikkei investigation found $1.65 trillion of off-balance-sheet AI obligations, growing eightfold in four years. The collateral doesn't hold its value: an H100 rented for $8/hour in early 2024 now goes for $2-3, and resale value can halve in three years, while the debt runs 5-24 years. Ratings track the counterparty, not the asset: CoreWeave's A3 facility was anchored by Meta, but a later Ba2 facility was tied to non-investment-grade customers. Public miners are the most leveraged: Core Scientific signed a 12-year, $10.2 billion deal with CoreWeave, TeraWulf a 20-year, $19 billion lease with Anthropic, and Hut 8 a 15-year, $7 billion lease. They're taking on 12-20 year obligations against hardware with a 3-year value half-life, funded partly by selling Bitcoin.
