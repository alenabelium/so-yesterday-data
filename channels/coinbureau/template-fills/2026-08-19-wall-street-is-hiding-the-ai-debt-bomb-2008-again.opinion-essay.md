## Thesis

Wall Street is lending against AI through opaque, off-balance-sheet debt structures that echo the 2008 crisis, and the weakest link in this chain is publicly listed Bitcoin miners.

## Argument

The mechanism is credit. Morgan Stanley puts global data center capex at $2.9T for 2025-2028, but hyperscaler cash flow covers only $1.4T, leaving a $1.5T financing gap. That gap is being filled by private credit, securitized products, and off-balance-sheet vehicles. Meta's Hyperion Campus is financed through a bankruptcy-remote vehicle, Big Net Investors LLC, with $27B of debt rated A+ and maturing in 2049, while Meta leases back on four-year terms. Oracle carries $248B in lease commitments through third-party vehicles. The Nikkei investigation found $1.65T of off-balance-sheet AI obligations, growing eightfold in four years. This is the same trick as 2006: pool weaker credit, slice into tranches, get a rating agency stamp. The collateral is the problem. GPUs depreciate 50% in three years, but the debt matures in 5 to 24 years. CoreWeave's ratings track the counterparty, not the asset: a Meta-anchored facility got A3/A-, but a facility tied to non-investment-grade customers got Ba2/BB+. Nvidia's platform to mobilize $500B, compared by Larry Fink to mortgage-backed securities, relies on GPU residual values holding through a full cycle—something never seen. Bitcoin miners, already mining at a loss, have converted into AI landlords, signing 12- to 20-year leases against hardware with a three-year half-life, funded partly by selling Bitcoin. Core Scientific posted a $347M net loss in Q1 2026, and CoreWeave's CDS hit 855 bps, implying a 50% default probability. The stress is showing in prices, but securitization continues. ## Counterpoints

- Hyperscalers are enormously cash generative at the parent level, so the debt is manageable.
- The AI buildout is a new era for markets, not a repeat of 2008.
- Investment-grade ratings on AI debt (e.g., CoreWeave's A3) suggest the risk is contained.
