## Thesis

Wall Street is hiding an AI debt bomb by lending against rapidly depreciating GPUs through opaque, off-balance-sheet structures, echoing the lead-up to 2008.

## Argument

The mechanism is credit. Morgan Stanley puts global data center capex at $2.9T for 2025-2028, but hyperscaler cash flow covers only $1.4T, leaving a $1.5T financing gap. That gap is being filled by private credit, securitized products, and off-balance-sheet vehicles. Meta's Hyperion Campus is a prime example: a bankruptcy-remote vehicle, Big Net Investors LLC, issued $27B of debt, rated A+ by S&P, with a 6.58% coupon and maturity in 2049. Meta leases the site back on rolling four-year terms, keeping the debt off its books. Oracle carries $248B in lease commitments through third-party vehicles, and Anthropic's chip-backed financing is structured through an SPV with Broadcom guaranteeing part of the senior slice. The Nikkei investigation found $1.65T of off-balance-sheet AI obligations, growing eightfold in four years. The collateral problem is that GPUs depreciate ~50% in three years, while the debt matures in 5 to 24 years. Ratings track the counterparty, not the asset: CoreWeave's debt was rated A3 when anchored by Meta, but Ba2 when tied to non-investment-grade customers. Nvidia's platform to mobilize $500B, compared by Larry Fink to the creation of mortgage-backed securities, only deepens the exposure. The weakest link is publicly listed Bitcoin miners, which have converted to AI landlords, signing 12- to 20-year leases against hardware with a three-year half-life, funded partly by selling Bitcoin. ## Counterpoints

- Hyperscalers are enormously cash generative at the parent level, which could support the debt.
- The optimists rely on GPU residual values holding through a full technology cycle, which has never been observed.
- Some argue this is simply a new era for markets, not a repeat of 2008.
