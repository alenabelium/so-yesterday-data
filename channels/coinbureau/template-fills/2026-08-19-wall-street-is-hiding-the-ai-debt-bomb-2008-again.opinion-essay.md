## Thesis

Wall Street is financing the AI buildout with opaque, off-balance-sheet debt that mirrors 2008, and the weakest link in this chain is publicly listed Bitcoin miners.

## Argument

The mechanism is structural: Wall Street is lending against AI hardware, not investing in it. Morgan Stanley puts the financing gap at $1.5 trillion, and the money is coming from private credit and securitized products — not balance-sheet debt. The trick is the bankruptcy-remote vehicle: Meta's Hyperion Campus is financed through Big Net Investors LLC, with $27B of debt rated A+ and a 2049 maturity, while Meta leases back on rolling four-year terms. That keeps the debt off Meta's books. Oracle does the same with $248B of lease commitments. The collateral doesn't hold: GPUs lose 50% of value in three years, but the debt runs 5 to 24 years. Ratings track the counterparty, not the asset — CoreWeave's A3 facility is anchored by Meta, but its Ba2 facility is tied to non-investment-grade tenants. Nvidia's $500B platform is explicitly compared to mortgage-backed securities by BlackRock's Larry Fink. The weakest link is miners: they converted to AI landlords to survive, signing 12- to 20-year leases against 3-year hardware, funded by selling Bitcoin. Core Scientific lost $347M in Q1 2026 and was bought by CoreWeave. The stress is already visible: CoreWeave's CDS imply a 50% default chance, and Moody's warns on credit quality. The host's counterpoint — that hyperscalers are cash-generative — is addressed by showing the ratings move with the tenant, not the asset. The conclusion: the bubble doesn't need to burst; if hardware ages faster than debt, losses hit the weakest link, and this time it's publicly traded.
