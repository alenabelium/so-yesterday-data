## Thesis

Wall Street is lending against AI through opaque, off-balance-sheet debt structures that echo 2006, and the collateral mismatch will hit the weakest link—publicly listed Bitcoin miners—before the debt is repriced.

## Argument

The mechanism is credit. Morgan Stanley puts global data center capex at $2.9T for 2025-2028, but hyperscaler cash flow covers only $1.4T, leaving a $1.5T financing gap. That gap is being filled by private credit, securitized products, and off-balance-sheet vehicles. Meta's Hyperion Campus is financed through a bankruptcy-remote vehicle, Big Net Investors LLC, with $27B in debt rated A+ and maturing in 2049, while Meta leases back on rolling four-year terms. Oracle carries $248B in lease commitments through third-party vehicles. The Nikkei investigation found $1.65T in off-balance-sheet AI obligations, growing eightfold in four years. This is the 2006 playbook: pool weaker credit, slice into tranches, get a rating agency stamp. The collateral problem is the mismatch: GPUs depreciate ~50% in three years, but the debt matures in 5-24 years. CoreWeave's ratings track the counterparty, not the asset—A3 when anchored by Meta, Ba2 when tied to non-investment-grade customers. Nvidia's $500B capital mobilization platform, with Larry Fink comparing it to the creation of mortgage-backed securities, only works if chips hold their value. Bitcoin miners, converting to AI landlords to survive mining at a loss, are the most leveraged link. They've signed $70B in AI contracts, sold 15,000+ coins, and taken on 12-20 year obligations against 3-year hardware. Core Scientific posted a $347M net loss, and CoreWeave's CDS hit 855bps, implying a 50% default probability. ## Counterpoints

- Hyperscalers are enormously cash generative at the parent level, which could support the debt.
- Optimists rely on GPU residual values holding through a full technology cycle, which has never been tested.
- Some argue this is simply a new era for markets, not a repeat of 2008.
