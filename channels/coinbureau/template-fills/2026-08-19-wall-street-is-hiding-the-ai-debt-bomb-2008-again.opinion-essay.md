## Thesis

Wall Street is financing the AI buildout with opaque, off-balance-sheet debt that echoes 2008, and the weakest link in this chain is publicly listed Bitcoin miners.

## Argument

The mechanism is credit. Morgan Stanley puts global data center capex at $2.9T for 2025-2028, but hyperscaler cash flow covers only $1.4T, leaving a $1.5T financing gap. That gap is being filled by private credit, securitized products, and off-balance-sheet vehicles. Meta's Hyperion Campus is financed through a bankruptcy-remote vehicle, Big Net Investors LLC, with $27B of debt rated A+ and maturing in 2049, while Meta leases the site on rolling four-year terms to keep the debt off its books. Oracle carries $248B in lease commitments through third-party vehicles, and Anthropic's chip-backed financing is structured through an SPV. The Nikkei investigation found $1.65T of off-balance-sheet AI obligations at the five biggest tech firms, growing eightfold in four years.

The collateral is the problem. GPUs depreciate roughly 50% in three years, but the debt matures in 5 to 24 years. Ratings track the counterparty, not the asset: CoreWeave's March 2026 facility was rated A3/A- because it was anchored by Meta, but a May facility tied to non-investment-grade customers got Ba2/BB+. Nvidia's platform to mobilize $500B of third-party capital, with Larry Fink comparing it to the creation of mortgage-backed securities, assumes chips hold their value — but nobody has seen GPU residual values through a full technology cycle.

## Counterpoints

- Hyperscalers are enormously cash generative at the parent level, which could support the debt.
- The AI buildout is a new era for markets, not a repeat of 2008.
- Investment-grade ratings on AI debt (e.g., CoreWeave's A3/A-) suggest the risk is manageable.
- Nobody has seen GPU residual values through a full technology cycle, so the collapse is not certain.
