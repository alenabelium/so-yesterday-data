## Thesis

Wall Street is financing the AI buildout with opaque, off-balance-sheet debt that mirrors 2008, and the weakest link in this chain is publicly listed Bitcoin miners.

## Argument

The mechanism is the financing gap. Morgan Stanley puts global data center capex at $2.9T for 2025-2028, but hyperscaler cash flow covers only $1.4T, leaving a $1.5T hole that must be borrowed. Private credit to AI has exploded from nothing to $200B outstanding, and global AI debt issuance is tracking toward $570B in 2026. The structure is designed to hide this debt: Meta's Hyperion Campus uses a bankruptcy-remote vehicle, Big Net Investors LLC, to issue $27B in A+ rated bonds that don't mature until 2049, while Meta leases back on rolling four-year terms. Oracle carries $248B in off-balance-sheet lease commitments, and Anthropic's chip-backed financing is similarly structured. This is the 2008 playbook: pool weaker credit, slice into tranches, get a rating agency stamp. The collateral problem is that GPUs depreciate 50% in three years, but the debt matures in 5 to 24 years. Ratings track the counterparty, not the asset: CoreWeave's A3 facility was anchored by Meta, but a later facility secured against non-investment-grade customers got Ba2. The weakest link is listed miners: they've signed 12- to 20-year leases against hardware with a three-year half-life, funded partly by selling Bitcoin. Core Scientific posted a $347M net loss, and CoreWeave's CDS imply a 50% default probability while its debt still carries investment-grade ratings. The stress is already visible, but securitization continues. The exposure relocates down the chain, and if GPU values collapse before the debt is repriced, the losses hit the weakest link—publicly traded miners.

## Counterpoints

- Hyperscalers are enormously cash generative at the parent level, which could support the debt.
- The AI buildout is a new era for markets, not a repeat of 2008.
- Nobody will default on the AI buildout; the structure just relocates exposure.
