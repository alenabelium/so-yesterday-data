## Thesis

Wall Street is financing the AI buildout with opaque, off-balance-sheet debt that echoes 2008, and the weakest link in this chain is publicly listed Bitcoin miners.

## Argument

The mechanism is credit. Morgan Stanley puts global data center capex at $2.9 trillion for 2025-2028, but hyperscaler cash flow covers only $1.4 trillion, leaving a $1.5 trillion financing gap. That gap is being filled by private credit, securitized products, and off-balance-sheet vehicles. Meta's Hyperion Campus is financed through a bankruptcy-remote vehicle, Big Net Investors LLC, with $27 billion of debt rated A+ and a 2049 maturity, while Meta leases the site back on rolling four-year terms. Oracle carries $248 billion in lease commitments through third-party vehicles. The Nikkei investigation found $1.65 trillion in off-balance-sheet AI obligations, growing eightfold in four years.

The collateral is the problem. GPUs depreciate roughly 50% in three years, but the debt matures in 5 to 24 years. Ratings track the counterparty, not the asset: CoreWeave's March facility was A3/A- because it was anchored by Meta, but a May facility tied to non-investment-grade customers dropped to Ba2/BB+. Nvidia's platform to mobilize $500 billion, compared by Larry Fink to mortgage-backed securities, assumes chips hold value through a full cycle—something never observed.

Bitcoin miners are the most leveraged link. They converted to AI landlords to survive, with $70 billion in disclosed AI contracts. Core Scientific signed a 12-year deal with CoreWeave, then sold 1,900 BTC. TeraWulf signed a 20-year lease with Anthropic. ## Counterpoints

- Hyperscalers are enormously cash generative at the parent level, which could support the debt.
- Optimists argue this is a new era for markets, not a repeat of 2008.
- The AI buildout won't default; exposure relocates through the chain, so losses may be contained.
