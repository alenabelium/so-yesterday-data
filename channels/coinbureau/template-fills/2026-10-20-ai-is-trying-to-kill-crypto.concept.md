## TL;DR

AI's rapid progress in mathematics could soon crack the cryptographic signatures protecting crypto wallets. Ethereum researcher Justin Drake urges 'bunker mode,' while the industry debates urgency. The core question: can crypto update its locks before AI finds a shortcut?

## The argument

### The Mathematical Lock
- **Anchor Timestamps**: ['00:01:02']
- **Claim**: Crypto security relies on a mathematical problem: a public lock (address) and a private key. The lock is open for study, but finding the key is computationally infeasible. This has protected coins since Bitcoin's launch.
- **Role**: definition

### AI's Rise in Math
- **Anchor Timestamps**: ['00:03:48']
- **Claim**: AI has advanced from failing simple sums to solving Olympiad-level problems and finding new algorithms. It can now spot patterns humans miss, potentially discovering shortcuts in cryptographic math—a software threat, unlike quantum computing's hardware problem.
- **Role**: evidence

### The Bunker Mode Debate
- **Anchor Timestamps**: ['00:04:45']
- **Claim**: Justin Drake's warning splits the industry: one camp fears AI superintelligence and urges proactive migration; the other dismisses it as panic, noting past false alarms. Both agree cryptography isn't broken yet, but disagree on urgency.
- **Role**: counter

### Vulnerability and Response
- **Anchor Timestamps**: ['00:07:10']
- **Claim**: Wallets with exposed locks (used addresses) are most at risk; a third of Bitcoin sits in such addresses. Ethereum plans faster, public upgrades (hash-based locks), while Bitcoin debates slower, consensus-driven changes, including potential freezing of old coins.
- **Role**: synthesis

## Evidence and caveats

AI has achieved gold-medal level at the International Mathematical Olympiad and helped improve a cryptographic attack on a new signature (not Bitcoin/Ethereum). No known breaks exist yet. The threat is software-based, not quantum. Both camps agree on good habits: avoid address reuse, use modern wallets, and don't panic. Buterin lost money in migrations, highlighting risks of moving too fast. Institutions are watching but not yet acting.
