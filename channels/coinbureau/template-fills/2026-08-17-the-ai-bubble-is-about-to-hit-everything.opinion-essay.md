## Thesis

The AI boom is now a credit story, not a tech story, and if AI revenue arrives late, the damage will land on pensions and insurers, not the companies that ordered the chips.

## Argument

The mechanism is debt. Nvidia's $500 billion financing package, with six firms signing on, was treated as bad news because it signaled the buildout is no longer funded by profits. In the most recent quarter, Amazon, Alphabet, and Oracle all posted negative free cash flow, with Alphabet's first negative quarter in its history as a public company. PIMCO says capex is eating 94% of hyperscaler operating cash flow, up from under half two years ago. The gap is filled by bonds: tech issuance jumped from $16.7 billion in 2024 to $193 billion by mid-2026, and Morgan Stanley and JP Morgan see $1.5 trillion of new tech debt needed through 2028. The structure is circular: the Bank for International Settlements flagged circular AI financing as one of the top three risks to global financial stability, alongside sovereign debt fragility. The risk is pushed off Nvidia's balance sheet into SPVs and private credit funds, but GPUs age like laptops while the debt runs like a toll road. Michael Burry argues depreciation schedules understate industry depreciation by $176 billion across 2026-2028, overstating Oracle's 2028 profits by 27% and Meta's by 21%. The systemic channels are threefold: concentration (Mag 7 at 34% of the S&P 500, 10 points above the dot-com peak), rates (30-year Treasury at 5.25%, with Apollo's chief economist naming hyperscaler issuance as a driver), and private credit (exposure above $200 billion, with Coreweave's $12.4 billion in GPU-collateralized loans). This is the Lucent and Nortel playbook: vendor financing, booked revenue, then defaults when customers evaporate.

## Counterpoints

- These are the most creditworthy companies on the planet, with investment-grade ratings and diversified revenue from search, ads, and cloud that doesn't depend on AI monetization to survive.
- Nvidia brings in money from outside investors rather than funding deals itself, giving it more protection than dot-com era vendor financing.
- Jensen Huang says compute has become an investable asset class, with chips fungible and broadly adopted.
- Larry Fink compares the buildout to the birth of mortgage-backed securities in the 1970s, noting each gigawatt costs $50-60 billion and the US needs over 70 gigawatts.
- Nvidia circulated a seven-page memo rejecting Enron comparisons, arguing that four to six years is realistic because chips cascade from training into inference and enterprise workloads.
