## Thesis

The AI boom is now a credit story, not a tech story, and if AI revenue arrives late, the damage will hit pensions and insurers, not just tech stocks.

## Argument

The mechanism is debt. Nvidia's $500 billion financing package, with Apollo, BlackRock, Blackstone, Brookfield, Goldman Sachs, and KKR, isn't about growth—it's about credit. The market's 2.6% drop on announcement day signals that investors see the risk. Hyperscaler capex is exploding, but cash flows are collapsing: Amazon posted negative $8.8 billion free cash flow, Alphabet negative $5.9 billion, Oracle negative $1.9 billion. PIMCO says capex eats 94% of hyperscaler operating cash flow. So where's the money coming from? The bond market. Tech bond issuance hit $225 billion in H1, with hyperscaler issuance up nearly tenfold from $16.7 billion in 2024 to $193 billion by mid-2026. Morgan Stanley and JP Morgan see $1.5 trillion in new tech debt needed through 2028. Off-balance-sheet commitments are even larger: Moody's tracks $1.22 trillion in data center leases, with $820 billion on unbuilt facilities. The structure is circular: Nvidia and hyperscalers finance their own customers through SPVs and private credit funds, a practice the BIS flags as a top-three risk to global financial stability. This is vendor financing, like the dot-com era's Lucent and Nortel, which ended in massive defaults. The bullish case—that these are creditworthy companies, that compute is a new asset class, that chips cascade into inference—doesn't address the timing mismatch. If revenue is delayed, someone must service the debt. ## Counterpoints

- These are the most creditworthy companies on the planet, with investment-grade ratings and diversified revenue—this isn't 1999.
- Nvidia's vendor financing is more protected than dot-com era because risk is pushed into SPVs and private credit funds, not left on Nvidia's books.
- Compute is now an investable asset class, with chips as fungible and broadly adopted, and will be underwritten like mortgages.
- Nvidia's memo argues 4-6 year depreciation is realistic because chips cascade from training into inference and enterprise workloads.
