## Thesis

The AI buildout is now a credit story, not a tech story, and its debt-financed structure will hit pensions and insurers if AI revenue arrives late.

## Argument

The mechanism is credit. Nvidia's $500 billion financing deal, with six firms including Apollo and BlackRock, was treated as bad news because it revealed the AI trade is now about debt, not growth. The cash machines have stopped being cash machines: Amazon posted negative $8.8 billion free cash flow, Alphabet negative $5.9 billion, and Oracle negative $1.9 billion, while PIMCO says capex eats 94% of hyperscaler operating cash flow. So the money comes from bonds: tech issuance jumped from $16.7 billion in 2024 to $193 billion by mid-2026, and Morgan Stanley and JP Morgan see $1.5 trillion of new tech debt needed through 2028. The structure is circular: vendors like Nvidia guarantee loans, pushing risk into special-purpose vehicles and private credit funds, which the BIS flags as a top-three risk to global financial stability. The bullish rebuttals—that these are creditworthy firms, that compute is now an investable asset class, that chips cascade into inference—don't address the timing mismatch: revenue could take years to catch up, but debt payments are due now. The systemic channels are threefold: concentration (Mag 7 at 34% of the S&P 500), rates (10-year at 4.72%, up 53 basis points), and private credit (exposure above $200 billion, projected to $300-600 billion by 2030). This mirrors the dot-com bust, where Lucent and Nortel financed their own customers and over 90% of telecom high-yield debt defaulted. If revenue arrives three years late, the damage lands on pensions and insurers, not the chip buyers.

## Counterpoints

- These are the most creditworthy companies on the planet; this isn't 1999.
- Nvidia's vendor financing is safer than dot-com era because risk is pushed off balance sheet into SPVs and private credit.
- Compute is now an investable asset class; chips are fungible and broadly adopted.
- Depreciation schedules are realistic because chips cascade from training to inference.
- The demand thesis isn't wrong; it's just a timing issue, as with the internet.
