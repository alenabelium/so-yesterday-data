## Thesis

The AI boom has morphed from a profit-funded buildout into a debt-financed credit cycle that threatens the broader economy, not just tech stocks.

## Argument

The mechanism is credit. Nvidia's $500 billion financing package, with six firms including Apollo and KKR, was treated as bad news because it revealed the buildout is now debt-driven. The cash machines have stopped: Amazon posted negative $8.8 billion free cash flow, Alphabet its first negative quarter ever, and Oracle is one notch above junk. PIMCO says capex eats 94% of hyperscaler operating cash flow. So the bond market fills the gap: tech issuance jumped from $16.7 billion in 2024 to $193 billion by mid-2026, and Morgan Stanley and JP Morgan see $1.5 trillion more needed through 2028. The structure is circular financing, flagged by the BIS as a top-three global stability risk. Nvidia pushes risk into SPVs and private credit funds, but GPUs depreciate like laptops while debt runs like a toll road. Michael Burry estimates depreciation is understated by $176 billion, overstating Oracle's 2028 profits by 27%. The systemic channels: concentration (Mag 7 at 34% of S&P 500), rates (30-year at 5.25%, pushed by hyperscaler issuance), and private credit (exposure above $200 billion, Coreweave pledging GPUs as collateral). This is the Lucent playbook: vendor financing, booked revenue, then defaults. The demand thesis may be right, but the timing is a decade early and the paper matures sooner.

## Counterpoints

- These are the most creditworthy companies on the planet, with investment-grade ratings and diversified revenue that doesn't depend on AI monetization to survive.
- Nvidia brings in outside investors rather than funding deals itself, offering more protection than dot-com vendor financing.
- Jensen Huang says compute is now an investable asset class, with chips fungible and broadly adopted.
- Larry Fink compares it to the birth of mortgage-backed securities, and John Gray says compute will be underwritten like mortgages.
- Nvidia's memo rejects Enron comparisons, arguing 4-6 year depreciation is realistic as chips cascade from training to inference.
