## Thesis

The AI boom is now a credit story, not a tech story, and the debt-financed buildout will hit pensions and insurers if AI revenue arrives late.

## Argument

The mechanism is credit. Nvidia's $500 billion financing package, with Apollo, BlackRock, Blackstone, Brookfield, Goldman Sachs, and KKR, was treated as bad news because it signals the buildout is no longer funded by profits. The cash machines have stopped being cash machines: Amazon posted negative $8.8 billion free cash flow, Alphabet negative $5.9 billion, Oracle negative $1.9 billion, and only Microsoft remains positive. PIMCO says capex eats 94% of hyperscaler operating cash flow. So the bond market fills the gap: tech issuance jumped from $16.7 billion in 2024 to $193 billion by mid-2026, and Morgan Stanley and JP Morgan see $1.5 trillion more needed through 2028. The structure is circular: Nvidia guarantees up to 25% of the debt, pushing risk into SPVs and private credit funds. The BIS flags this as a top-three global stability risk. The mismatch is stark: GPUs age like laptops but are financed with debt designed for toll roads. Michael Burry estimates depreciation is understated by $176 billion, overstating Oracle's 2028 profits by 27% and Meta's by 21%. The impact spreads through three channels: concentration (Mag 7 at 34% of S&P 500), rates (30-year at 5.25%, up 39 bps), and private credit (exposure above $200 billion, projected to $300-600 billion by 2030). This mirrors the dot-com bust: Lucent and Nortel financed customers, and 90% of telecom high-yield debt defaulted. The demand thesis wasn't wrong then, just a decade early. If AI revenue arrives three years late, the damage lands on pensions and insurers, not the chip buyers.

## Counterpoints

- These are the most creditworthy companies on the planet; this isn't 1999. Microsoft, Alphabet, Amazon, and Meta have investment-grade ratings and diversified revenue that doesn't depend on AI monetization to survive.
- Nvidia's structure is safer than dot-com vendor financing because risk is pushed to outside investors, not left with the vendor.
- Jensen Huang says compute is now an investable asset class; chips are fungible and broadly adopted, justifying the debt.
- Larry Fink compares the buildout to the birth of mortgage-backed securities in the 1970s, implying the structure is sound.
- Nvidia circulated a memo rejecting Enron comparisons, arguing 4-6 year depreciation is realistic because chips cascade from training to inference.
