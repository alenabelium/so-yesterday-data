## Thesis

The AI boom is now a credit story, not a tech story, and the debt financing behind it could spread damage to pensions and insurers if AI revenue is delayed.

## Argument

The mechanism is credit. Nvidia's $500 billion financing package, with six firms including Apollo and BlackRock, was treated as bad news because it revealed the AI buildout is increasingly debt-funded, not profit-funded. The cash machines have stopped being cash machines: Amazon posted negative $8.8 billion free cash flow, Alphabet negative $5.9 billion, and Oracle negative $1.9 billion, while PIMCO notes capex eats 94% of hyperscaler operating cash flow. The gap is filled by bonds: tech issuance jumped from $16.7 billion in 2024 to $193 billion by mid-2026, and Morgan Stanley and JP Morgan see $1.5 trillion in new tech debt needed through 2028. Off-balance-sheet obligations are massive—Moody's tracks $1.22 trillion in data center lease commitments, with $820 billion on unbuilt facilities. The structure is circular: the Bank for International Settlements flagged circular AI financing as a top-three risk to global financial stability, alongside sovereign debt fragility. The risk is pushed off Nvidia's balance sheet into SPVs and private credit funds, but GPUs age like laptops while the debt runs like a toll road. Michael Burry argues depreciation is understated by $176 billion, overstating Oracle's 2028 profits by 27% and Meta's by 21%. The systemic channels are threefold: concentration (Mag 7 at 34% of S&P 500), rates (10-year at 4.72%, 30-year at 5.25%), and private credit (exposure above $200 billion, projected to $300-600 billion by 2030). This mirrors the dot-com bust: Lucent and Nortel financed customers, then ate defaults; over 90% of telecom high-yield debt

## Counterpoints

- These are the most creditworthy companies on the planet; this isn't 1999, and they have diversified revenue from search, ads, and cloud that doesn't depend on AI monetization to survive.
- Nvidia's vendor financing is arguably safer than dot-com era because risk is pushed off its balance sheet into SPVs and private credit funds, and Jensen Huang says compute is now an investable asset class.
- Nvidia circulated a memo rejecting Enron comparisons, arguing four-to-six-year depreciation is realistic because chips cascade from training into inference and enterprise workloads.
- BlackRock's Larry Fink compares the buildout to the birth of mortgage-backed securities, and John Gray says compute will be underwritten like mortgages.
