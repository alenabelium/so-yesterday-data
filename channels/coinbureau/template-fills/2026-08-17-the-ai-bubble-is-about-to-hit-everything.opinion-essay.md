## Thesis

The AI boom is now a credit story, not a tech story, and its debt-fueled financing will eventually hit pensions and insurers, not just tech stocks.

## Argument

The mechanism is credit. Nvidia's $500 billion financing deal, with six firms including Apollo and BlackRock, was treated as bad news because it revealed the buildout is no longer funded by profits. In the most recent quarter, Amazon, Alphabet, and Oracle posted negative free cash flow, with capex eating 94% of hyperscaler operating cash flow. Tech bond issuance has jumped nearly tenfold, and Morgan Stanley and JP Morgan see $1.5 trillion more needed through 2028. The structure is circular: vendors like Nvidia guarantee loans, pushing risk into SPVs and private credit funds. The BIS flags this as a top-three risk to global financial stability. The impact spreads through three channels: concentration (Mag 7 is 34% of the S&P 500), rates (10-year at 4.72%, 30-year at 5.25%), and private credit (exposure above $200 billion). This mirrors the dot-com bust, where Lucent and Nortel financed their own customers and over 90% of telecom high-yield debt defaulted. If AI revenue is delayed, the damage lands on pensions and insurers, not the companies that ordered the chips.

## Counterpoints

- These are the most creditworthy companies on the planet; this isn't 1999.
- Nvidia's risk is pushed off-balance sheet into SPVs and private credit funds, giving more protection than dot-com vendor financing.
- Jensen Huang says compute is now an investable asset class, and BlackRock's Larry Fink compares it to the birth of mortgage-backed securities.
- Nvidia circulated a memo rejecting Enron comparisons, arguing chips cascade from training to inference, making 4-6 year depreciation realistic.
