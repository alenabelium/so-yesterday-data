## Thesis

The AI boom is now a credit story, not a tech story, and if AI revenue arrives late, the damage will hit pensions and insurers, not just tech stocks.

## Argument

The mechanism is debt. Nvidia's $500 billion financing package, with Apollo, BlackRock, Blackstone, Brookfield, Goldman Sachs, and KKR, is not about growth but about credit. The market's 2.6% sell-off on announcement day shows investors understand this. The cash machines have stopped being cash machines: Amazon posted negative $8.8 billion free cash flow, Alphabet negative $5.9 billion, Oracle negative $1.9 billion, and only Microsoft remains positive. PIMCO says capex eats 94% of hyperscaler operating cash flow, up from under half two years ago. So where's the money coming from? The bond market. Tech bond issuance is up nearly tenfold, from $16.7 billion in 2024 to $193 billion by mid-2026, and Morgan Stanley and JP Morgan see $1.5 trillion more needed through 2028. Goldman says over a third of AI infrastructure spend is debt-funded. The structure is circular: Nvidia and others use vendor financing, pushing risk into SPVs and private credit funds, which the BIS flags as a top-three risk to global financial stability. The bear case is stark: Michael Burry argues depreciation schedules understate industry depreciation by $176 billion, overstating Oracle's 2028 profits by 27% and Meta's by 21%. Nvidia's CDS doubled in two months. ## Counterpoints

- These are the most creditworthy companies on the planet, with investment-grade ratings and diversified revenue from search, ads, and cloud that doesn't depend on AI monetization to survive.
- Nvidia's vendor financing is arguably more protected than dot-com era vendor financing because the risk is pushed off its balance sheet into SPVs and private credit funds.
- Jensen Huang says this is the first time compute has become an investable asset class, with chips as fungible and broadly adopted.
- Larry Fink compares the whole thing to the birth of mortgage-backed securities in the 1970s, noting each gigawatt of capacity costs $50-60 billion to build and the US needs over 70 gigawatts.
- Nvidia circulated a seven-page memo rejecting Enron comparisons, arguing that four to six years is a realistic depreciation schedule because chips cascade from training into inference and enterprise workloads.
