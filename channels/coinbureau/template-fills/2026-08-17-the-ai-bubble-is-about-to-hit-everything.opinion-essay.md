## Thesis

The AI boom is now a credit story, not a tech story, and if AI revenue arrives late, the damage lands on pensions and insurers.

## Argument

The mechanism is credit. Nvidia's $500 billion financing deal, with six firms including Apollo and BlackRock, was treated as bad news because it signaled the buildout is no longer funded by profits. In the most recent quarter, Amazon posted negative $8.8 billion free cash flow, Alphabet negative $5.9 billion, and Oracle negative $1.9 billion, while capex eats 94% of hyperscaler operating cash flow. The gap is filled by debt: tech bond issuance hit $225 billion in H1, with hyperscaler issuance up nearly tenfold from 2024 to 2026. Off-balance-sheet commitments are even larger—Moody's tracks $1.22 trillion in data center leases, with $820 billion on unbuilt facilities. The BIS flagged circular AI financing as a top-three risk to global financial stability. This spreads through three channels: concentration (Magnificent 7 at 34% of S&P 500), rates (30-year Treasury at 5.25%, up 39 basis points), and private credit (lending to AI firms up from $3 billion in 2010 to $40 billion in 2025). If revenue is delayed, the damage hits pensions and insurers, not the companies that ordered the chips.

## Counterpoints

- These are the most creditworthy companies on the planet; this isn't 1999.
- Nvidia has more protection than dot-com vendor financing because investors bring outside money.
- Jensen Huang says compute is now an investable asset class; chips are fungible.
- Larry Fink compares it to the birth of mortgage-backed securities in the 1970s.
- Nvidia circulated a memo rejecting Enron comparisons, arguing 4-6 year depreciation is realistic.
