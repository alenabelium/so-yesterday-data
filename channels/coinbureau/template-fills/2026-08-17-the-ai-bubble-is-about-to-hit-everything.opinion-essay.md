## Thesis

The AI boom is no longer a tech story but a credit story, and the debt-financed buildout will hit pensions and insurers if AI revenue arrives late.

## Argument

The mechanism is credit. Nvidia's $500 billion financing package, with Apollo, BlackRock, Blackstone, Brookfield, Goldman Sachs, and KKR, was treated as bad news because it signals the buildout is now debt-funded, not profit-funded. The cash machines have stopped being cash machines: Amazon posted negative $8.8 billion free cash flow, Alphabet negative $5.9 billion, Oracle negative $1.9 billion, and PIMCO says capex eats 94% of hyperscaler operating cash flow. Tech bond issuance has jumped nearly tenfold to $193 billion by mid-2026, with Morgan Stanley and JP Morgan seeing $1.5 trillion more needed through 2028. The structure is circular: vendor financing, flagged by the BIS as a top-three global stability risk, pushes risk off Nvidia's balance sheet into SPVs and private credit funds. The mismatch is stark—GPUs age like laptops but are financed with debt designed for toll roads. Michael Burry argues depreciation is understated by $176 billion, overstating Oracle's 2028 profits by 27% and Meta's by 21%. The systemic channels are threefold: concentration (Mag 7 at 34% of the S&P 500), rates (30-year Treasury at 5.25%, pushed by hyperscaler issuance), and private credit (exposure above $200 billion, with Coreweave pledging GPUs as collateral). This mirrors the dot-com bust—Lucent and Nortel financed customers, and over 90% of telecom high-yield debt defaulted. The demand thesis wasn't wrong then, just a decade early and financed on shorter paper. Today, if revenue arrives three years late, the damage lands on pensions and insurers, not the companies that ordered the chips.

## Counterpoints

- These are the most creditworthy companies on the planet; this isn't 1999.
- Nvidia's vendor financing is safer than dot-com era because risk is pushed to outside investors, not left with the vendor.
- Jensen Huang says compute is now an investable asset class; chips are fungible and broadly adopted.
- Larry Fink compares it to the birth of mortgage-backed securities; John Gray says compute will be underwritten like mortgages.
- Nvidia's memo rejects Enron comparisons, arguing 4-6 year depreciation is realistic because chips cascade into inference and enterprise workloads.
