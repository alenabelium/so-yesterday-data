## Thesis

The AI boom is now a credit story, not a tech story, and its debt-financed structure will drag the broader economy down if AI revenue arrives late.

## Argument

The mechanism is credit. Nvidia's $500 billion financing package, with Apollo, BlackRock, Blackstone, Brookfield, Goldman Sachs, and KKR, wasn't about growth—it was about debt. The market's 2.6% drop on announcement day shows investors get it. Hyperscaler capex is exploding, but cash flows are collapsing: Amazon posted negative $8.8 billion free cash flow, Alphabet its first negative quarter ever, and Oracle is one notch above junk. PIMCO says capex eats 94% of hyperscaler operating cash flow. So where's the money coming from? The bond market. Tech issuance jumped from $16.7 billion in 2024 to $193 billion by mid-2026, and Morgan Stanley and JP Morgan see $1.5 trillion more needed through 2028. Off-balance-sheet commitments are even bigger: Moody's tracks $1.22 trillion in data center leases, $820 billion on unbuilt facilities. The structure is circular financing—Nvidia guarantees a slice of repayments, books sales upfront, and pushes risk into SPVs and private credit funds. The BIS named this a top-three global stability risk. The bullish rebuttals—compute as an asset class, mortgage-style underwriting, chips cascading into inference—don't address the timing mismatch. Revenue could take years, but debt payments are due now. The contagion spreads three ways: concentration (Mag 7 at 34% of the S&P 500), rates (30-year at 5.25%, up 39 bps), and private credit (exposure above $200 billion, Coreweave's $12.4 billion in GPU-backed loans). ## Counterpoints

- These are the most creditworthy companies on the planet, with investment-grade ratings and diversified revenue—this isn't 1999.
- Nvidia brings in outside investors, not vendor financing, giving it more protection than dot-com era vendors.
- Jensen Huang says compute is now an investable asset class, with chips fungible and broadly adopted.
- Larry Fink compares it to the birth of mortgage-backed securities; John Gray says compute will be underwritten like mortgages.
- Nvidia's memo argues 4-6 year depreciation is realistic because chips cascade from training to inference.
