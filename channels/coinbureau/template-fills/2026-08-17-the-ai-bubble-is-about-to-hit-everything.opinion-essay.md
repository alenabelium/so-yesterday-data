## Thesis

The AI boom is now a credit story, not a tech story, and the debt financing behind it threatens pensions and insurers if AI revenue arrives late.

## Argument

The mechanism is credit. Nvidia's $500 billion financing package, with Apollo, BlackRock, Blackstone, Brookfield, Goldman Sachs, and KKR, was treated as bad news because it signaled the buildout is now debt-funded, not profit-funded. The cash machines have stopped being cash machines: Amazon posted negative $8.8 billion free cash flow, Alphabet negative $5.9 billion, Oracle negative $1.9 billion, and PIMCO says capex eats 94% of hyperscaler operating cash flow. The bond market is filling the gap: tech issuance jumped from $16.7 billion in 2024 to $193 billion by mid-2026, and Morgan Stanley and JP Morgan see $1.5 trillion of new tech debt needed through 2028. Off-balance-sheet obligations are massive: Moody's tracks $1.22 trillion in data center leases, with $820 billion on unbuilt facilities. The structure is circular financing, which the BIS named a top-three risk to global financial stability. Nvidia pushes risk into SPVs and private credit funds, but Jensen Huang says Nvidia may backstop up to 25%, or $125 billion. Michael Burry argues depreciation schedules understate industry depreciation by $176 billion, overstating Oracle's 2028 profits by 27% and Meta's by 21%. The systemic channels are threefold: concentration (Mag 7 at 34% of the S&P 500), rates (30-year Treasury at 5.25%, up 39 basis points), and private credit (exposure above $200 billion, projected to $300-600 billion by 2030). ## Counterpoints

- These are the most creditworthy companies on the planet; this isn't 1999. Microsoft, Alphabet, Amazon, and Meta have investment-grade ratings and diversified revenue that doesn't depend on AI monetization to survive.
- Nvidia brings in money from outside investors rather than funding deals itself, giving it more protection than dot-com-era vendor financing.
- Jensen Huang says compute has become an investable asset class, with chips fungible and broadly adopted; BlackRock's Larry Fink compares it to the birth of mortgage-backed securities.
- Nvidia circulated a seven-page memo rejecting Enron comparisons, arguing that four-to-six-year depreciation is realistic because chips cascade from training into inference and enterprise workloads.
