## Thesis

The AI boom is now a credit story, not a tech story, and its debt-financed structure could hit pensions and insurers if revenue arrives late.

## Argument

The shift from profits to debt is the mechanism. Nvidia's $500 billion financing deal, with six firms including Apollo and BlackRock, was treated as bad news because it signals credit risk, not growth. The cash flows confirm it: Amazon posted negative $8.8 billion free cash flow, Alphabet negative $5.9 billion, Oracle negative $1.9 billion, while capex eats 94% of hyperscaler operating cash flow. To fund this, tech bond issuance jumped from $16.7 billion in 2024 to $193 billion by mid-2026, with Morgan Stanley and JP Morgan seeing $1.5 trillion in new debt needed through 2028. The structure is circular: vendor financing where Nvidia guarantees up to 25% of the deals, flagged by the BIS as a top three risk to global financial stability. The exposure spreads through three channels: concentration (Mag 7 at 34% of S&P 500), rates (30-year Treasury at 5.25%, up 39 basis points), and private credit (exposure above $200 billion, projected to $300-600 billion by 2030). The dot-com parallel is clear: Lucent and Nortel financed their customers, booked revenue, then ate defaults when the fiber buildout failed. The demand thesis wasn't wrong, just a decade early and financed with short-term paper. Today, if AI revenue arrives on schedule, the leverage is invisible; if it's 3 years late, the damage lands on pensions and insurers, not the chip companies.

## Counterpoints

- These are the most creditworthy companies on the planet, with investment grade ratings and diversified revenue that doesn't depend on AI monetization to survive.
- The risk is pushed off Nvidia's balance sheet into SPVs and private credit funds, giving more protection than dot-com era vendor financing.
- Nvidia circulated a memo arguing 4-6 year depreciation is realistic because chips cascade from training into inference and enterprise workloads.
- Jensen Huang says compute has become an investable asset class, with chips described as fungible and broadly adopted.
- Larry Fink compares the buildout to the birth of mortgage-backed securities, noting each gigawatt costs $50-60 billion and the US needs over 70 gigawatts.
