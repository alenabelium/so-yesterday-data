## Thesis

The AI boom is now a credit story, not a tech story, and the debt-fueled buildout threatens pensions and insurers if AI revenue arrives late or not at all.

## Argument

The mechanism is credit. Nvidia's $500 billion financing package, with Apollo, BlackRock, Blackstone, Brookfield, Goldman Sachs, and KKR, wasn't about growth—it was about debt. The market's 2.6% drop on announcement day signaled that. Hyperscaler capex is exploding—$690–800 billion for 2026—but cash flows are collapsing: Amazon posted negative $8.8 billion free cash flow, Alphabet negative $5.9 billion, Oracle negative $1.9 billion. PIMCO says capex eats 94% of hyperscaler operating cash flow. So where's the money coming from? Bonds. Tech issuance jumped from $16.7 billion in 2024 to $193 billion by mid-2026, and Morgan Stanley and JPMorgan see $1.5 trillion more needed by 2028. Goldman says over a third of AI infrastructure spend is debt-funded. The structure is circular: Nvidia guarantees up to 25% of the financing, pushing risk into SPVs and private credit funds. The BIS flagged this as a top-three risk to global financial stability. The analogy is a car dealership that arranges your loan—manufacturing its own customer. The debt is mismatched: GPUs depreciate like laptops, but the debt runs for decades. Michael Burry estimates depreciation is understated by $176 billion, overstating Oracle's 2028 profits by 27% and Meta's by 21%. This spreads systemically: the Magnificent 7 are 34% of the S&P 500, so a repricing hits pensions directly. The 10-year Treasury is at 4.72%, up 53 basis points, and Apollo's chief economist ties hyperscaler issuance to 30-year yields at 20-year highs. ## Counterpoints

- These are the most creditworthy companies on the planet—Microsoft, Alphabet, Amazon, Meta are profitable with diversified revenue; this isn't 1999.
- Nvidia's risk is arguably more protected than dot-com vendor financing because outside investors fund the deals, not Nvidia itself.
- Jensen Huang argues compute is now an investable asset class—chips are fungible and broadly adopted.
- Larry Fink compares the buildout to the birth of mortgage-backed securities, noting each gigawatt costs $50–60 billion and the US needs over 70 gigawatts.
- Nvidia circulated a memo rejecting Enron comparisons, arguing 4–6 year depreciation is realistic because chips cascade from training to inference.
