## Thesis

The AI boom is now a credit story, not a tech story, and its debt-funded structure threatens pensions and insurers if revenue arrives late.

## Argument

The mechanism is credit. Nvidia's $500 billion financing package, with Apollo, BlackRock, Blackstone, Brookfield, Goldman Sachs, and KKR, was treated as bad news because it revealed the buildout is debt-funded, not profit-funded. The cash machines have stopped: Amazon posted negative $8.8 billion free cash flow, Alphabet negative $5.9 billion, Oracle negative $1.9 billion, while capex eats 94% of hyperscaler operating cash flow. Tech bond issuance jumped from $16.7 billion in 2024 to $193 billion by mid-2026, with Morgan Stanley and JP Morgan seeing $1.5 trillion more needed through 2028. Off-balance-sheet obligations exceed on-balance-sheet ones: Moody's tracks $1.22 trillion in data center leases, $820 billion on unbuilt facilities. The structure is circular financing, flagged by the BIS as a top-three global stability risk. The risk is pushed into SPVs and private credit funds, with Nvidia backing up to 25% ($125 billion). The mismatch: GPUs age like laptops but are financed with decades-long debt. Michael Burry argues depreciation is understated by $176 billion, overstating Oracle's 2028 profits by 27% and Meta's by 21%. The impact spreads: the Magnificent 7 are 34% of the S&P 500, so a repricing hits pensions directly. The 10-year Treasury at 4.72% and 30-year at 5.25% are pushed higher by hyperscaler issuance, crowding out other borrowers. Private credit exposure to AI is over $200 billion, with Coreweave pledging GPUs as collateral. This mirrors the dot-com bust: Lucent and Nortel financed customers, then ate defaults; over 90% of telecom high-yield debt defaulted.

## Counterpoints

- These are the most creditworthy companies on the planet, with investment-grade ratings and diversified revenue; this isn't 1999.
- Nvidia's vendor financing is safer than dot-com era because risk is pushed to outside investors, not left with the vendor.
- Jensen Huang argues compute is now an investable asset class, with chips fungible and broadly adopted.
- Larry Fink compares the buildout to the birth of mortgage-backed securities, with each gigawatt costing $50-60 billion.
- Nvidia's memo rejects Enron comparisons, arguing 4-6 year depreciation is realistic as chips cascade into inference and enterprise workloads.
