## Thesis

The AI boom is now a credit story, not a tech story: over a third of the buildout is debt-financed, and a delay in AI revenue will hit pensions and insurers, not just tech stocks.

## Argument

The mechanism is credit. Nvidia's $500 billion financing package, with Apollo, BlackRock, Blackstone, Brookfield, Goldman Sachs, and KKR, was treated as bad news because it revealed the buildout is no longer funded by profits. In the most recent quarter, Amazon, Alphabet, and Oracle posted negative free cash flow while spending tens of billions on capex; only Microsoft remains a cash machine. PIMCO notes capex now eats 94% of hyperscaler operating cash flow, up from under half two years ago. So the money comes from bonds: tech issuance jumped from $16.7 billion in 2024 to $193 billion by mid-2026, and Morgan Stanley and JPMorgan see $1.5 trillion more needed through 2028. Off-balance-sheet commitments are even larger—Moody's tracks $1.2 trillion in data center leases, with $820 billion on unbuilt facilities. The structure is circular: vendors like Nvidia guarantee loans and book sales upfront, a practice the BIS named a top-three risk to global financial stability. The risk spreads through three channels: concentration (the Magnificent 7 are 34% of the S&P 500, so a repricing hits pensions directly), rates (hyperscaler issuance pushes 30-year yields to 20-year highs, crowding out other borrowers), and private credit (exposure above $200 billion, with Coreweave pledging GPUs as collateral). The dot-com parallel is explicit: Lucent and Nortel financed their own customers, and over 90% of telecom high-yield debt defaulted. ## Counterpoints

- These are the most creditworthy companies on the planet—this isn't 1999. Microsoft, Alphabet, Amazon, and Meta have investment-grade ratings and diversified revenue that doesn't depend on AI monetization to survive.
- The risk is pushed off Nvidia's balance sheet into SPVs and private credit funds, giving Nvidia more protection than dot-com vendor financing, where the vendor often held the risk.
- Jensen Huang argues compute is now an investable asset class—chips are fungible and broadly adopted, unlike the bespoke telecom gear of the dot-com era.
- Larry Fink compares the buildout to the birth of mortgage-backed securities, and John Gray says compute will be underwritten like mortgages, with AI usage across Blackstone's portfolio up sevenfold.
- Nvidia circulated a memo rejecting Enron comparisons, arguing that 4-6 year depreciation is realistic because chips cascade from training into inference and enterprise workloads.
