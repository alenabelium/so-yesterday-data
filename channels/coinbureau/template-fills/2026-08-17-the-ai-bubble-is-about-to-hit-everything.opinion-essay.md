## Thesis

The AI boom is now a credit story, not a tech story, and its debt-fueled structure will hit pensions, insurers, and the broader economy if AI revenue is delayed.

## Argument

The mechanism is credit. Nvidia's $500 billion financing deal, with six firms including Apollo and BlackRock, was treated as bad news because it revealed the buildout is debt-funded, not profit-funded. Hyperscaler capex is exploding—$690-800 billion for 2026—but cash flows are collapsing: Amazon posted negative $8.8 billion free cash flow, Alphabet its first negative quarter ever, and Oracle is one notch above junk. PIMCO says capex eats 94% of operating cash flow. So the bond market fills the gap: tech issuance jumped from $16.7 billion in 2024 to $193 billion by mid-2026, and Morgan Stanley sees $1.5 trillion more needed. Off-balance-sheet commitments—$1.2 trillion in data center leases, $820 billion unbuilt—are the real exposure. The structure is circular: Nvidia guarantees loans, books revenue, and pushes risk into SPVs and private credit funds. The BIS flags this as a top-three systemic risk. The bullish rebuttals—creditworthiness, diversification, compute as an asset class—don't address the timing mismatch: revenue may lag years while debt payments come due. This spreads via three channels: concentration (Mag 7 at 34% of S&P 500), rates (10-year at 4.72%, 30-year at 5.25%), and private credit ($200 billion exposure, Coreweave's GPU-collateralized loans). The dot-com parallel is clear: Lucent and Nortel financed customers, booked revenue, then ate defaults. If AI revenue stalls, the damage lands on pensions and insurers, not the chip buyers.

## Counterpoints

- These are the most creditworthy companies on the planet, with investment-grade ratings and diversified revenue from search, ads, and cloud that doesn't depend on AI monetization to survive.
- Nvidia's vendor financing is more protected than dot-com era because outside investors, not Nvidia, hold the risk; Jensen Huang calls compute an investable asset class.
- BlackRock's Larry Fink compares the buildout to the birth of mortgage-backed securities, and John Gray says compute will be underwritten like mortgages.
- Nvidia circulated a memo rejecting Enron comparisons, arguing 4-6 year depreciation is realistic because chips cascade from training to inference.
