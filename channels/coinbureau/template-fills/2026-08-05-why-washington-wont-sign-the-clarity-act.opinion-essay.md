## Thesis

The Clarity Act is stalled not by Wall Street or Senate gridlock but by a single ethics provision that would ban federal officials and their spouses from issuing digital assets, a direct conflict of interest given the Trump family's.

## Argument

The mechanism is a one-page compromise. On July 30, Senators Tillis and Gallego sent the White House a revised ethics section of the Clarity Act, prohibiting federal officials and their spouses from issuing or sponsoring digital assets while in office, with enforcement by the Attorney General and a state AG lawsuit safeguard. The White House's silence is telling: the Office of Government Ethics released the president's 2025 financial statements on June 30, and a Senate Banking Committee minority report on July 22 estimated the Trump family's crypto income at nearly $800 million from World Liberty Financial, $636 million from the memecoin, totaling over $1.4 billion—two-thirds of the president's declared income. This is more than any public American crypto company earned in the same period. The White House's claim of no conflict of interest is contradicted by the fact that the one provision the administration won't agree to is the one that touches the family that earns the most in the industry it regulates. Meanwhile, Wall Street is not the obstacle: BlackRock, Fidelity, Franklin Templeton, Goldman Sachs, and SoFi publicly supported the combined text on July 28. Community banks are negotiating small amendments, not blocking. The real cost is a regulatory risk premium: Bitcoin has been flat while the S&P rose 2.63%, Coinbase fell over 10%, Circle lost almost 8%, and analysts like Mizuho and Morgan Stanley cut price targets due to the bill's likely failure. ## Counterpoints

- White House spokesperson Anna Kelly claims the president's assets are in discretionary accounts managed independently, and there is no conflict of interest.
- Some blame Wall Street for the delays, but major firms like BlackRock, Fidelity, and Goldman Sachs publicly supported the bill on July 28.
- Community banks are often blamed, but the ABA seeks only small, surgical amendments, not blocking.
