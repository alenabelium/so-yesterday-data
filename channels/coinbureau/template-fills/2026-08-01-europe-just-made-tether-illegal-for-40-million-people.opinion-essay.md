## Thesis

Europe has effectively made Tether illegal for 40 million Revolut users by filtering it out of regulated platforms, a move that reveals a deliberate strategy to protect the banking system and promote compliant stablecoins as permanent.

## Argument

The mechanism is regulatory filtering, not outright prohibition. MiCA requires licensed platforms to only offer stablecoins from authorized issuers, and Tether's refusal to comply with the 60% EU bank reserve requirement meant it was delisted from every major regulated exchange. This is a more effective elimination than a ban because it requires no court defense. Europe prepared alternatives in advance: Circle's USDC and EURC, plus a wave of bank-backed euro stablecoins, ensuring no market chaos. The ECB's push for a digital euro and warnings about deposit outflows reveal the true motive: protecting the banking system from dollar-denominated stablecoins. The result is a two-way market: regulated Western platforms use compliant assets, while offshore markets continue with USDT. This is not just about Tether; it's a global template. Europe licenses and excludes, Washington licenses and promotes. The choice of jurisdiction by Tether, aligning with US rules, and the rush of 37 European banks into stablecoin issuance confirm that stablecoins are now permanent infrastructure.

## Counterpoints

- Removing the world's largest stablecoin from a 450-million-person economy would cause chaos, but it didn't because compliant alternatives were prepared in advance.
- Tether's refusal to comply with MiCA's 60% bank reserve requirement is justified by the risk of bank failures, as seen with Circle's USDC during the Silicon Valley Bank collapse.
- The MiCA liquidity rules harm the competitiveness of euro stablecoins, as noted in an analytical paper from May this year.
