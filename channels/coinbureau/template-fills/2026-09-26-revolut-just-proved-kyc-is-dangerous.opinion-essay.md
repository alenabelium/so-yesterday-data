## Thesis

KYC files are now the most dangerous documents attached to a person's name, and the regulatory frameworks that mandate their creation and retention make data minimization effectively illegal.

## Argument

The attack on Revolut wasn't a hack; it was a compliance desk doing exactly what European regulators designed it to do. The lawful request desk is engineered to say yes to the state quickly, and speed is the product. When an attacker compromised an Italian government email account, every technical check came back clean because the mail was genuine. The attacker even got coaching on fixing paperwork. The result: passport scans, selfies, home addresses, and full Bitcoin transaction histories for 680 crypto-rich individuals were handed over.

This incident exposes the core problem: KYC files are assembled without asking, because it's required. Every deposit, conversion, and withdrawal builds a dossier that says, "Here is a named human being, here is a recent photograph of their face, here is the door that they walk through at night, and here's how much Bitcoin they control." This goes beyond digital privacy; it puts people in physical danger. The targets were identified on-chain first, then the compliance desk gave up the person.

The regulatory framework makes this worse. Under EU AML rules, records must be retained for at least five years, extendable to ten. The travel rule pushes identity data along with transactions. DAC8 forces automatic reporting to tax authorities, creating yet another copy of the file. Meanwhile, GDPR Article 5 requires data minimization. These laws cannot both be obeyed.
