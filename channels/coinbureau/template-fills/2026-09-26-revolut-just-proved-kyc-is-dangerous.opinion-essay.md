## Thesis

KYC files are now the most dangerous documents attached to a person's name, and the Revolut breach proves that regulatory frameworks like AML and GDPR make data minimization effectively illegal, leaving self-custody as the only real.

## Argument

The attack on Revolut wasn't a hack—it was a lawful request desk doing exactly what European regulators designed it to do. A criminal gained control of an Italian government email account and sent fraudulent police requests for six months, and Revolut's compliance desk answered, handing over passport scans, selfies, home addresses, and full Bitcoin transaction histories for 680 crypto-rich individuals. The targets were chosen onchain first, then attached to names and faces via the KYC files. This is the mechanism: KYC files are assembled without asking, because platforms are required to collect and retain them. Every deposit, withdrawal, and transaction history is added automatically, creating a document that says, 'Here is a named human, their face, their door, and their Bitcoin.' The danger is physical, not just digital—wrench attacks are up 33%, and home invasions went from 1 to 20 in a year. The standard reading is that Revolut messed up, but the deeper truth is that Revolut was complying. The EU's AML regulation mandates five years of record retention, extendable to ten, and the travel rule pushes identity data along with transactions. GDPR Article 5 demands data minimization, but both are EU law and cannot both be obeyed—minimization isn't discouraged, it's effectively prohibited. The technology to do better exists, like ZK Passport, but politicians don't want to hear it. ## Counterpoints

- Revolut messed up: The compliance desk was hoodwinked by a fraudulent request, which is a company failure.
- Ransom theater: Paying the ransom offers no receipt or proof of deletion, so it's pointless.
- Regulatory compliance: Revolut was just following EU law, which mandates data retention and sharing.
