## Thesis

KYC files are now the most dangerous documents attached to a person's name, and the Revolut breach proves that regulatory compliance, not company failure, is the root cause.

## Argument

The attack on Revolut wasn't a hack; it was a lawful request desk doing exactly what European regulators designed it to do: say yes to the state quickly without tipping off the customer. The attacker compromised an Italian government email account and sent fraudulent European investigation orders. Every technical check came back clean because the mail was genuine. Revolut's compliance desk answered for six months, handing over passport scans, selfies, home addresses, and full Bitcoin transaction histories for 680 crypto-rich individuals. The targets were identified onchain first, then the compliance desk attached a name, face, and street address to them. This is the second half of the KYC file built without asking, because it's required to. The assembled document says: here is a named human, a recent photo of their face, the door they walk through at night, and how much Bitcoin they control. This goes beyond digital privacy; it puts people in physical danger. The regulatory framework makes this inevitable. Under EU AML rules, records must be retained for at least five years, extendable to ten. The travel rule pushes identity data along with transactions, and DAC8 forces reporting to tax authorities, creating yet another copy of the file. GDPR Article 5 requires data minimization, but both are EU law and cannot both be obeyed. Minimization isn't discouraged; it's effectively prohibited.
