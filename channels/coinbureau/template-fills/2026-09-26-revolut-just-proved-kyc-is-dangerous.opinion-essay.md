## Thesis

KYC files are now the most dangerous documents attached to a person's name, and the regulatory frameworks that mandate their creation and retention are the root cause of this danger.

## Argument

The attack on Revolut wasn't a hack; it was a lawful request desk doing exactly what European regulators designed it to do: say yes to the state quickly without tipping off the customer. The desk's speed mandate turned a compromised Italian government email into a six-month data pipeline. The real danger isn't the breach itself but the nature of the KYC file. It's not just a passport scan; it's an assembled dossier that combines identity, face, home address, and financial history, including Bitcoin transaction histories. This dossier is so valuable that criminals are now targeting crypto holders specifically, as seen in the rise of physical wrench attacks and home invasions. The regulatory framework makes this worse: AML rules require retaining data for up to a decade, while GDPR demands data minimization. These laws are contradictory, making minimization effectively illegal. The technology to avoid storing sensitive documents exists—zero-knowledge proofs like ZK Passport can verify identity without transmitting the document—but regulators aren't interested. The result is that customers are forced to hand over data that can't be changed, and they bear the risk of extortion and physical harm.

## Counterpoints

- Revolut messed up and should have verified the requests more thoroughly.
- KYC is necessary for customer protection and anti-money laundering efforts.
- The breach is a one-off, not a systemic problem.
- Paying the ransom would have been a viable option to protect victims.
