## Thesis

KYC files are now the most dangerous documents attached to a person's name, and the Revolut breach proves that regulatory frameworks like AML and GDPR make data minimization illegal, leaving self-custody as the only real protection.

## Argument

The attack on Revolut wasn't a hack; it was a lawful request desk doing exactly what European regulators designed it to do. The desk's entire purpose is to say yes to the state quickly, and speed is the product. When an attacker compromised an Italian government email account and sent fraudulent European investigation orders, every technical check came back clean because the mail was genuine. Revolut's compliance desk even coached the attacker on fixing paperwork. The result: 680 crypto-rich individuals had their passport scans, selfies, home addresses, and full Bitcoin transaction histories exposed. This wasn't a one-off. Ledger's 2020 breach exposed 272,000 customers, and Coinbase's 2025 breach exposed 69,000. The consequences are escalating: Certik logged 52 physical wrench attacks in the first half of 2026, up 33% year-over-year, with home invasions jumping from 1 to 20. The standard reading is that Revolut messed up, but the deeper truth is that Revolut was complying. EU AML rules require crypto firms to retain records for five to ten years, the travel rule pushes identity data with transactions, and DAC8 forces automatic reporting to tax authorities. Meanwhile, GDPR Article 5 demands data minimization. These laws are contradictory, making minimization effectively prohibited. The technology to do better exists—zero-knowledge tools like ZK Passport can prove a document's authenticity without storing it—but politicians don't want to hear it. So where does that leave us?
