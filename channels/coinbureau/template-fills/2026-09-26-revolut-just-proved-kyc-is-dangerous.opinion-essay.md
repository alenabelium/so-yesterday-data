## Thesis

KYC files are now the most dangerous documents attached to a person's name, and EU regulations make data minimization effectively illegal, forcing platforms to build vaults of identity data that criminals can exploit.

## Argument

The attack on Revolut wasn't a hack; it was a compliance desk doing exactly what European regulators designed it to do. The lawful request desk is engineered to say yes to the state quickly without tipping off the customer, and speed is the product. That's the vulnerability. An attacker got into an Italian government mailbox via an infostealer infection, sent genuine-looking European investigation orders, and Revolut's desk answered them for six months, even coaching the attacker on formatting. What went out the door wasn't just emails—it was passport scans, onboarding selfies, home addresses, bank statements, and full Bitcoin transaction histories for 680 people, all pre-selected because they were visibly rich in crypto. The KYC file is the problem: it combines identity, financial, and location data into a single document that says, 'Here is a named human being, here is a recent photograph of their face, here is the door they walk through at night, and here's how much Bitcoin they control.' This goes beyond digital privacy—it puts people in physical danger. The chain gave up the money; the compliance desk gave up the person. And this isn't a one-off: Ledger's 2020 breach exposed 272,000 names and addresses, leading to years of extortion and swatting, and Coinbase's 2025 contractor bribery exposed 69,000 customers. Physical wrench attacks are up 33% year-over-year, with home invasions jumping from 1 to 20 in a year.
