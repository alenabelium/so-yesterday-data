## Thesis

KYC files are now the most dangerous documents attached to a person's name, and the Revolut breach proves that regulatory frameworks like AML and GDPR are contradictory, making data minimization effectively illegal.

## Argument

The attack wasn't a hack; it was a lawful request desk doing exactly what European regulators designed it to do. An attacker compromised an Italian government email account and sent fraudulent European investigation orders to Revolut's compliance desk, which answered them for six months, handing over passport scans, selfies, home addresses, and Bitcoin transaction histories for 680 crypto-rich individuals. The desk's mandate is to say yes to the state quickly without tipping off the customer, so hesitation is treated as obstruction—speed is the product, and that's the vulnerability. The KYC file itself is the real problem: it combines identity, financial, and location data into a single dossier that says, "Here is a named human being, here is a recent photograph of their face, here is the door they walk through at night, and here's how much Bitcoin they control." This goes beyond digital privacy—it puts people in physical danger, as evidenced by the rise in wrench attacks and home invasions. The regulatory framework forces this: EU AML rules require records to be retained for at least five years, extendable to ten, and the travel rule pushes identity data along with transactions. GDPR Article 5 demands data minimization, but both are EU law and cannot both be obeyed—minimization isn't discouraged, it's effectively prohibited. The technology to do better exists, like ZK Passport, but politicians don't want to hear it. The conclusion is that self-custody and minimizing data held by platforms are essential for protection.

## Counterpoints

- Revolut messed up—true to some degree, but you could also view this as Revolut complying with EU regulations.
- Some argue that KYC is about customer protection, but the customer supplies the data, carries the risk, and gets extorted.
- The ransom theater might be the least interesting part—the real problem is what was stolen: identities that cannot be replaced.
