## Thesis

KYC files are now the most dangerous documents attached to a person's name, and the Revolut breach proves that regulatory frameworks like AML and GDPR make data minimization effectively illegal, forcing platforms to build vaults that.

## Argument

The attack wasn't a hack; it was a lawful request desk doing what European regulators designed it to do. An attacker compromised an Italian government email account and sent fraudulent police requests for six months, and Revolut's compliance desk complied, handing over passport scans, selfies, home addresses, and full Bitcoin transaction histories for 680 crypto-rich individuals. The system is engineered to say yes quickly without tipping off the customer, making speed the product and hesitation obstruction. The KYC file itself is the danger: it combines identity, financial, and location data into a document that says, 'Here is a named human being, here is a recent photograph of their face, here is the door they walk through at night, and here's how much Bitcoin they control.' The targets were identified on-chain first, then the compliance desk attached a name, face, and street address. Victims like Mark Karpelès and Felix Romer faced physical threats and extortion before Revolut even notified them. This isn't a one-off: Ledger, Coinbase, and others have suffered similar breaches, and physical wrench attacks on crypto holders are rising sharply. The root cause is regulatory: EU AML rules require retaining records for up to a decade, the travel rule pushes identity data along with transactions, and DAC8 forces reporting to tax authorities, while GDPR demands data minimization. ## Counterpoints

- Revolut messed up and should have verified the requests more thoroughly.
- KYC is supposed to be about customer protection, not endangerment.
- The technology to check identity without storing documents already exists, but politicians don't want to hear it.
