## Thesis

Wall Street has chosen Ethereum over Bitcoin as the infrastructure asset, and the only thing that could break this thesis is a governance battle over staking yields.

## Argument

The shift is visible in the flows: in July, Ethereum ETFs pulled in $365 million while Bitcoin ETFs saw record outflows, and corporate treasuries replaced ETFs as the main engine of ETH accumulation. But the real story is the change in what the asset is for. Bitcoin treasuries like Strategy's can't grow without financing; Ethereum balance sheets grow themselves through staking. Bitmain, the leading ETH treasury, has over 87% of its stack staked, generating ~$291 million in annualized rewards. Wall Street built the infrastructure for this: BNY wired native staking rewards into its custody platform, BlackRock launched a staked ETH product and adjusted its ETF for institutional desks, and tokenized treasuries on Ethereum now total over $6 billion. Institutions now split the view: Bitcoin is the reserve asset, Ethereum is programmable financial infrastructure. But this thesis is fragile. EIP-8361, proposed on August 4th, would taper issuance and cut staking yields from ~2.6% to ~1.1-1.2%, sparking immediate pushback from DeFi leaders and staking providers. The core developers want decentralization and bounded supply; institutional capital wants payout. For years these aligned, but this proposal puts them on a collision course. If yields drop, the $10 billion in treasury capital and the institutional infrastructure built around staking could look elsewhere.

## Counterpoints

- Bitcoin isn't losing: it still has its bid, with ETFs taking in $170M and $211M on consecutive days in August, and it retains its identity as a reserve asset.
- The yield doesn't save you from price: Sharplink's cost basis is ~$3.5k, so staking rewards don't protect against a falling ETH price.
- EIP-8361 has a real argument: staking participation could pass 55% by 2028, concentrating the network in a few custodians and taxing non-stakers through dilution.
- The proposal may not even make it into the next upgrade—no formal pull request has been opened, so it could slip to a later fork.
