## Thesis

Institutional capital has chosen Ethereum over Bitcoin as the asset to build on and earn yield from, marking a fundamental shift in what these assets are for.

## Argument

The shift is visible in the flows. In July, Ethereum ETFs took in $365 million while Bitcoin ETFs saw record outflows, and corporate treasuries replaced ETFs as the main engine of ETH accumulation. But the real story is what those treasuries are doing with their ETH. Bitmain, the largest, has staked over 87% of its stack, generating roughly $291 million in annualized staking rewards. That cash flow arrives whether or not the company ever raises another dollar. Bitcoin balance sheets need financing to grow; Ethereum balance sheets grow themselves. This changes what the asset is for, and Wall Street has spent the summer building for it. BNY wired native staking rewards into its custody platform, BlackRock launched a staked Ethereum product that passes through 82% of yield, and tokenized treasuries on Ethereum now total $15.2 billion. Institutions now want one asset to store value and a different asset to move it, and for the first time, they're funding both separately. But there's a governance fight brewing. EIP-8361 proposes to taper issuance and cut staking yields from 2.6% to 1.1%, sparking immediate pushback from DeFi leaders. This is a fight about who Ethereum is being run for: core developers optimizing for decentralization, or institutional capital optimizing for payout. If you own ETH, you're betting on a governance process that can reprice your yield depending on a vote.

## Counterpoints

- Bitcoin isn't losing; it retains its identity as a reserve asset and sovereign hedge, and its ETFs still see inflows on many days.
- EIP-8361 authors argue that without issuance taper, staking participation could pass 55% by 2028, concentrating the network in a few custodians and taxing non-stakers through dilution.
- Some critics say the proposal is too theoretical, breaks DeFi credit markets, and was rushed with a 48-hour comment window.
