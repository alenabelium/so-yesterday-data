## Thesis

Wall Street has chosen Ethereum over Bitcoin as the infrastructure asset for institutional finance, and the only thing that could break this thesis is a governance battle over staking yields.

## Argument

The shift is visible in the flows. In July, Ethereum ETFs pulled in $365 million while Bitcoin ETFs saw record outflows, and corporate treasuries replaced ETFs as the main engine of ETH accumulation. But the real story is what these buyers are doing with the asset. Bitmain, the largest ETH treasury, has staked over 87% of its stack, generating ~$291 million in annualized staking rewards. This is a fundamental difference from Bitcoin's static treasury model: Bitcoin balance sheets need financing to grow, but Ethereum balance sheets grow themselves through yield. Wall Street has built the infrastructure to capture this yield. BNY wired native staking rewards into its custody platform, BlackRock launched a staked ETH product (ETHB) that passes through 82% of rewards, and tokenized treasuries on Ethereum now total $15.2 billion. Institutions now view Bitcoin as a reserve asset and Ethereum as programmable financial infrastructure — the thing you use. This is why ETH outperformed Bitcoin by 11 points in July and why BlackRock re-engineered its ETHA product for institutional desks. The thesis is now under threat from EIP-8361, a proposal to taper issuance and cut staking yields from ~2.6% to ~1.1%. This has sparked a governance battle between core developers optimizing for decentralization and institutional capital optimizing for payout. If yields drop, the $10 billion of treasury capital that came for the yield could leave.

## Counterpoints

- Bitcoin isn't losing: it still has its identity as a digital reserve asset and sovereign hedge, and spot Bitcoin ETFs took in $170M on Aug 3 and $211M on Aug 4.
- EIP-8361 authors argue that without a taper, staking participation could pass 55% by January 2028, concentrating the network in a few custodians and taxing non-stakers through dilution.
- Some critics say the proposal is theoretical and doesn't achieve its goals, while others object to the 48-hour comment window, suggesting governance process concerns.
