## Thesis

Wall Street has structurally chosen Ethereum over Bitcoin as the operating layer for institutional finance, a shift that a single governance vote could now reverse.

## Argument

The shift is visible in the flows: in July, Ethereum ETFs took in $365 million while Bitcoin ETFs suffered record outflows, and corporate treasuries replaced ETFs as the main engine of ETH accumulation. The mechanism is yield. Bitmain, the largest ETH treasury, has staked over 87% of its stack, generating roughly $291 million in annualized staking rewards without needing to raise new capital. Bitcoin balance sheets, by contrast, can only grow through financing because the asset produces nothing. Wall Street built the infrastructure around this: BNY wired native staking rewards into its custody platform, BlackRock launched a staked Ethereum product and re-engineered its ETF for institutional desks, and tokenized treasuries now settle 43% on Ethereum. Institutions now split the assets: Bitcoin is the reserve, Ethereum is the programmable rails. But the entire thesis rests on a governance process. EIP-8361, proposed on August 4, would taper issuance and cut staking yields from ~2.6% to ~1.1-1.2%, directly threatening the yield that drew these institutions in. The pushback was immediate, and the fight is about who Ethereum is run for: the network's decentralization or institutional payouts. If the yield drops, the $10 billion treasury capital could leave.

## Counterpoints

- Bitcoin isn't losing: it still has its bid, with spot ETFs taking in $170M and $211M on consecutive days in early August, and it retains its identity as a digital reserve asset.
- The proposal's authors argue that without a cap, staking participation could pass 55% by 2028, concentrating the network in a few custodians and taxing non-stakers through dilution.
- Some critics say EIP-8361 is theoretically flawed and would break DeFi credit markets that rely on looped borrowing strategies.
