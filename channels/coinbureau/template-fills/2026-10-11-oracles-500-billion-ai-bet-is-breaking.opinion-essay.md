## Thesis

Oracle's Stargate project is breaking down because the AI boom's debt-financed infrastructure is vulnerable to mundane delays like a gas pipeline, not just chip shortages.

## Argument

The mechanism is physical and financial at once. A 17-mile gas pipeline stuck in New Mexico permitting has pushed Project Jupiter's in-service date to February 2027, forcing Oracle to issue a force majeure notice that defers rent if the site misses its 2028 target. That single pipe exposes the entire AI trade's weak point: power and permits don't scale like software. The site's fuel-cell microgrid is only 10% built, and the air permit is still pending. But the real story is the debt stack. Blue Owl put in $3B equity and $18B in construction loans; Oracle's capex tripled to $28.5B in a quarter, leaving free cash flow negative and total debt at $156B. OpenAI funds itself with investor money, eyeing a $30B round. Every link depends on the next one's cash arriving on schedule. A stalled site means no revenue, but interest keeps ticking. That's why the force majeure notice matters: it pushes the cost down the chain to lenders and equity holders. The lenders already feel it—construction loans trade at 89 cents on the dollar, and S&P cut Oracle to triple-B, one notch above junk. Larry Ellison's personal borrowing adds another layer: he's pledged 430 million shares as collateral, and his 10b5-1 selling plan was cancelled two days after it surfaced. Oracle stock is down 56% from its high. The site, the debt, the stock, and the founder's loans are one tangled mess. A single failure can drag the rest down. Stargate was meant to prove the boom works; instead, it shows how fragile the whole edifice is.

## Counterpoints

- Stargate is just hitting a rough patch, not a systemic failure.
- The AI boom is unstoppable and will overcome infrastructure delays.
- Force majeure is a routine legal protection, not a sign of collapse.
- Debt financing is normal for large infrastructure projects and will be refinanced.
