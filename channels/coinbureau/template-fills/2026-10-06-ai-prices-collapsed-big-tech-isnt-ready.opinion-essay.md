## Thesis

China's open-weight AI models have collapsed AI prices, undermining the US tech industry's scarcity-based business model and making its trillion-dollar infrastructure bet a potential financial blunder.

## Argument

The mechanism is price. DeepSeek's R1 matched OpenAI's o1 on key benchmarks and was given away free, triggering a $589 billion single-day loss for Nvidia. Since then, Alibaba's Qwen, Moonshot's Kimi, and DeepSeek's V4.1 Flash (MIT-licensed) have kept the pressure on, with Chinese open-weight models now just 2-5 months behind the US frontier, while US open models lag 6-9 months. The premium only survives on the hardest tasks, which are a small fraction of the work. US labs have responded by cutting prices and, in OpenAI's case, releasing open-weight models—a move forced by Chinese competition.

The US response has been to go physical. Amazon, Microsoft, Alphabet, and Meta poured $511 billion into infrastructure, with data center construction spending now exceeding $75 billion a year. But this bet faces hard constraints. Nearly half of planned 2026 data center capacity has been cancelled or delayed due to grid connection waits of 4-7 years, and power transformer lead times have stretched to 3-5 years. Meanwhile, China added 543 GW of new power capacity in 2025 and builds its data centers next to the power source. The chip supply is equally fragile, with TSMC fabricating nearly all advanced AI chips on a single island. Export controls meant to slow China down backfired, pushing Chinese labs to innovate with Mixture of Experts and Huawei's Ascend chips, which now hold half of China's AI accelerator market.

## Counterpoints

- Some argue US labs still lead in frontier model quality, but Chinese open-weight models are only 2-5 months behind, and the premium only survives on the hardest tasks.
- Proponents claim export controls will protect US chip dominance, but they backfired, pushing China to develop efficient architectures and domestic chips like Huawei's Ascend.
- Skeptics say the infrastructure buildout will pay off eventually, but history shows telecom companies that overbuilt didn't profit from the subsequent boom.
