## Thesis

China's open-weight AI models have collapsed AI prices, undermining the scarcity-based business model of US tech giants, whose trillion-dollar infrastructure bet may become a financial blunder.

## Argument

The mechanism is price. DeepSeek's R1, released in January 2025, matched OpenAI's o1 on key benchmarks and was given away free, triggering a $589 billion single-day loss for Nvidia. Since then, Alibaba's Qwen, Moonshot's Kimi, and DeepSeek's V4.1 Flash (MIT-licensed) have kept the pressure on. American labs charge per token, but Chinese models undercut them by orders of magnitude: Anthropic's Claude Opus 5.5 charges $4 per million input tokens, while DeepSeek's flash charges 15 cents off-peak. Quality gaps are small—Chinese open-weight models trail the US frontier by only 2-5 months, while US open models lag by 6-9 months. So enterprises like DoorDash, Airbnb, and Perplexity route routine workloads to cheap Chinese models, reserving premium models for the hardest tasks. The US response has been to go physical: Microsoft, Alphabet, Amazon, and Meta poured $511 billion into infrastructure, with spending projected to top $650 billion this year. But this bet faces hard constraints. Power is the first bottleneck: nearly half of planned US data center capacity for 2026 has been delayed or cancelled, and grid connection waits run 4-7 years. Transformers take 3-5 years to arrive, and GE Vernova is booking gas turbines out to 2031. Meanwhile, China added 543 GW of power in 2025 and builds next to the grid. Chips are the second constraint: Nvidia controls ~75% of AI accelerator revenue, and nearly all chips are fabricated by TSMC in Taiwan. ## Counterpoints

- US labs are cutting prices and releasing open-weight models, suggesting they can adapt to competition.
- China's models are still 2-5 months behind the US frontier, so the premium may survive on the hardest tasks.
- The telecom bubble analogy may be flawed because AI demand could grow faster than bandwidth did.
- Decentralized networks may not be able to match the scale and reliability of centralized data centers.
