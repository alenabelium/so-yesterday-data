## Thesis

The US CBDC ban, passed without Trump's signature via a housing bill, was a victory for commercial banks protecting their business model, not a win for freedom, and it has unilaterally disarmed America in the global digital currency race.

## Argument

The ban's passage was a constitutional accident: Article 1, Section 7 turned Trump's 10-day silence into law, letting a housing bill carry the CBDC prohibition. The housing bill was politically untouchable—85-5 in the Senate, 358-32 in the House—so the CBDC ban, which barely passed the House 219-210, rode it to enactment. The official narrative was anti-surveillance, but the real beneficiaries were commercial banks. A Fed-issued digital dollar would let citizens hold accounts directly with the central bank, bypassing banks and draining the deposits that fund their loans. During a panic, it would trigger faster, more powerful bank runs. The banking lobby—American Bankers Association, Banking Policy Institute, and others—pushed hard against it, and the same housing bill also gave banks regulatory bonuses: higher public welfare investment limits, simplified inspections, and exemptions on custodial deposits. So the 'surveillance state' argument was a convenient cover for protecting bank profits. The US has now banned CBDCs until at least 2031, the only major bloc voluntarily withdrawing. ## Counterpoints

- The official justification was preventing a surveillance state, which resonated across the political spectrum.
- Some argue the ban is principled restraint, a government refusing to create a surveillance state.
- The dollar's dominance is based on rule of law and capital markets, not digital infrastructure, so the ban may not hurt.
- The digital yuan is a payment instrument, not a threat to the reserve currency, so the ban's strategic cost is limited.
