## Thesis

Google has already won the AI race by leveraging its distribution monopoly, and that victory is the problem because it entrenches a single company's control over the entire internet stack.

## Argument

The win wasn't a comeback; it was a distribution ambush. OpenAI earned its billion users one at a time, but Google just plugged Gemini into Android's 3 billion devices, Chrome's 69% browser share, and a search box handling 8.5 billion queries a day. No ad campaign needed. The Gemini app crossed a billion monthly users, and AI overviews reach 2.5 billion monthly. That's the first asymmetry.

The second is compute. Anthropic and OpenAI are paying rent to the company they're trying to dethrone. Anthropic's cloud deal with Google gives it access to up to a million TPUs, with commitments ballooning to $200 billion over five years. Google designs the chips, sells them, leases them, guarantees the lease, owns a slice of the building, and owns a piece of the tenant. It's a landlord who also owns the brick factory and co-signs your mortgage. Google's TPUs are 40-50% cheaper than Nvidia's, and while Nvidia runs a 75% gross margin, Google pays itself and invoices the field. UBS estimates 27% of Google Cloud revenue comes from OpenAI and Anthropic combined.

The third is the quality gap closing. Gemini is no longer significantly behind on reasoning tests, and its API traffic hit 22 billion tokens per minute in Q2 2026. Distribution decides the outcome, and Gemini just needs to be good enough.

## Counterpoints

- OpenAI's billion is weekly active users, a harder bar than Google's monthly figure, so by engagement intensity OpenAI is still ahead.
- Google depends on other companies for hardware; TPU production is bottlenecked, and Google claims it still doesn't have enough compute.
- Anthropic is spreading its bets across Amazon, Nvidia, AMD, and others rather than relying too heavily on Google.
- The 'better model wins' argument held for a while, but Gemini has closed the gap on reasoning and coding.
- The antitrust ruling was supposed to break Google's monopoly, but the remedy was limited and Google changed nothing.
