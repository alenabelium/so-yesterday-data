## Thesis

Wall Street isn't absorbing crypto; it's moving the dollar onto crypto's infrastructure, and Washington still owns the currency.

## Argument

The mechanism is the GENIUS Act's compliance clock. Once the OCC finalizes its rulebook (targeted for November) and the law's hard effective date hits in January 2027, issuing an unauthorized dollar stablecoin becomes a crime. That deadline forced every major player to pick a side. BlackRock, ICE, DTCC, Visa, and MasterCard are now named validators on Circle's Arc, a permissioned network—they're not renting access, they're owning the rails. Then 21 banks including Goldman, Citi, and BofA formed a consortium to launch their own regulated stablecoin, and Stripe, Visa, MasterCard, Coinbase, and 140+ others back OpenUSD. You don't spend two years building a competitor to something you think is a fad. The format has won.

The deeper point: this isn't finance adopting crypto—it's the dollar acquiring a new distribution network. Every compliant stablecoin is backed by short-dated Treasuries, so a shopkeeper in Lagos or an Argentine escaping the peso is financing the US government without a bank account. Tether alone holds $141B in Treasuries, ranking above South Korea, Saudi Arabia, or Australia as a creditor. Washington is counting on this: Treasury Secretary Bessent projects tenfold growth, and the crypto czar sees trillions in new Treasury demand. This is the cheapest dollar expansion available, no trade deals needed.

## Counterpoints

- Running a node is no big deal—but 21 banks forming a consortium to launch their own stablecoin shows they see the format as a winner, not a fad.
- This looks like finance adopting crypto, but it's actually the dollar acquiring a new distribution network—stablecoins are just Treasury-backed IOUs.
- Europe's defensive Euro stablecoin consortium and Lagarde's 'digital dollarization' warning show the geopolitical stakes, not just a tech trend.
