## Thesis

Wall Street isn't absorbing crypto; it's moving the dollar onto crypto's infrastructure, and Washington still owns the currency.

## Argument

The mechanism is regulatory. The GENIUS Act, signed in July 2025, created a federal stablecoin framework with a hard compliance deadline of January 2027. Once the OCC finalizes its rulebook (targeted for November), issuing an unauthorized dollar stablecoin becomes a crime. That deadline forced the race: BlackRock, ICE, DTCC, Visa, and MasterCard are now validators on Circle's Arc, a permissioned network. These institutions don't just participate—they own. In September 2026, 21 banks (Goldman, Citi, BofA) announced a joint venture for their own regulated stablecoin, and Stripe, Visa, MasterCard, Coinbase, and 140+ firms back OpenUSD. You don't build a competitor to a fad.

The real prize is dollar distribution. Every compliant stablecoin is backed by short-dated US Treasuries, so a shopkeeper in Lagos or an Argentine paying a supplier is financing the US government without a bank account. Tether's Treasury exposure ($141B as of Q1 2026) makes it the 17th largest holder of US debt—above South Korea, Saudi Arabia, or Australia. Washington is counting on this: Treasury Secretary Bessent sees a tenfold stablecoin market by 2030; crypto czar David Sacks sees trillions in new Treasury demand pushing rates down. The Treasury Borrowing Advisory Committee and Brookings have modeled it. This is the cheapest dollar expansion available—no trade deals needed.
