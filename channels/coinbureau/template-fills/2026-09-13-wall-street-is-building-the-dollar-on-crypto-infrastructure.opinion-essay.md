## Thesis

Wall Street isn't absorbing crypto; it's moving the dollar onto crypto's infrastructure, and Washington still owns the currency.

## Argument

The mechanism is regulatory. The GENIUS Act, signed in July 2025, created a federal stablecoin framework with a hard compliance deadline in January 2027. That deadline forced every major financial institution to pick a side: build or be left out. So they built. BlackRock, ICE, DTCC, Visa, and MasterCard now run validator nodes on Circle's Arc, a permissioned network. Then 21 banks, including Goldman Sachs and Citi, formed a joint venture to launch their own regulated dollar stablecoin. You don't spend two years in a consortium building a competitor to something you think is a fad. They've decided the format has won, and they'd rather own the system than rent it.

But this isn't finance adopting crypto. It's the dollar acquiring a new distribution network. Every compliant stablecoin is backed by short-dated US Treasuries, so a shopkeeper in Lagos holding digital dollars is financing the American government without a bank account. Tether's Treasury exposure is about $141 billion, making it the 17th largest holder of US debt, above South Korea or Saudi Arabia. Washington is counting on this: Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent says the stablecoin market could grow tenfold, and the crypto czar sees trillions in new demand for Treasuries, pushing rates down. This is the cheapest dollar expansion available.

Europe's panic proves the point. 37 institutions across 15 countries formed a consortium to issue a Euro stablecoin, and ECB President Lagarde warns of 'digital dollarization.' Crypto built the system, but Washington still owns the currency.

## Counterpoints

- Running a node is no big deal; press releases about companies running nodes are common.
- Crypto natives argued for 15 years about whether TradFi would ever accept crypto; the answer might be neither yes nor no.
