## Thesis

Wall Street isn't absorbing crypto; it's moving the dollar onto crypto's infrastructure, and Washington still owns the currency.

## Argument

The mechanism is the dollar's delivery network. Sending a dollar internationally moves a message through correspondent banks, each with cutoffs and compliance checks, so a Friday wire can sit until Tuesday. The dollar runs on the worst delivery network in modern commerce, and everyone inside finance has known it for 30 years. What changed is a law with a clock: the GENIUS Act, signed July 2025, creates a federal stablecoin framework, and once the OCC finalizes its rulebook (targeted November), the hard effective date is January 2027. After that, issuing an unauthorized dollar stablecoin becomes flat-out crime. That deadline kicked off a race. Circle named founding validators for Arc, a permissioned chain where BlackRock, ICE, DTCC, Visa, and MasterCard run nodes. Then, in September 2026, 21 banks—Goldman, Citi, Bank of America—announced a joint venture for their own regulated dollar stablecoin, targeting H1 2027, just after the compliance window shuts. Stripe, Visa, MasterCard, Coinbase, and 140+ companies back OpenUSD, which shares reserve earnings with distribution partners. You don't spend two years in a consortium building a competitor to something you think is a fad. The format has won, and they'd rather own the system than rent it. But this isn't finance adopting crypto; it's the dollar acquiring a new distribution network. Every compliant stablecoin is backed by short-dated US debt, so a shopkeeper in Lagos holding digital dollars is financing the American government without a bank account.
