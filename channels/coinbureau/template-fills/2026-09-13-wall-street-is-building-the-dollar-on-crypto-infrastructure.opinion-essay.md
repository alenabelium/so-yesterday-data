## Thesis

Wall Street isn't absorbing crypto; it's moving the dollar onto crypto's infrastructure, and Washington still owns the currency.

## Argument

The mechanism is regulatory. The GENIUS Act, signed in July 2025, created the first federal stablecoin framework, forcing compliance and setting a January 2027 deadline. That deadline triggered a race: BlackRock, ICE, DTCC, Visa, and MasterCard now run validator nodes on Circle's Arc, a permissioned network. Then, in September 2026, 21 banks including Goldman Sachs and Citi announced a joint venture for their own regulated dollar stablecoin, targeting launch right after the compliance window. You don't build a competitor to a fad. The format has won, and they'd rather own the system than rent it.

But this isn't finance adopting crypto—it's the dollar acquiring a new distribution network. Every compliant stablecoin is backed by US Treasuries, so a shopkeeper in Lagos or an Argentine paying a supplier is financing the American government without a bank account. Tether holds $141 billion in Treasuries, ranking above South Korea, Saudi Arabia, or Australia as a creditor. Washington is counting on this: Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent sees the market growing tenfold, and the crypto czar sees trillions in new Treasury demand. This is the cheapest dollar expansion available.

Europe's panic proves the move. 37 institutions across 15 countries formed a consortium to issue a Euro stablecoin, and ECB President Lagarde warns of 'digital dollarization' and loss of monetary sovereignty. Crypto built the system, but Washington owns the currency.

## Counterpoints

- Running a node is no big deal—but 21 banks forming a joint venture to launch their own stablecoin shows they believe the format has won.
- This looks like finance adopting crypto, but it's actually the dollar acquiring a new distribution network.
- Some might see this as crypto winning—but the host argues it's the dollar taking over the network that was supposed to replace it.
