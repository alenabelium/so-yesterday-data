## Thesis

Wall Street isn't absorbing crypto; it's moving the dollar onto crypto's infrastructure, and Washington still owns the currency.

## Argument

The mechanism is regulatory. The GENIUS Act, signed in July 2025, created a federal stablecoin framework with a hard compliance deadline in January 2027. Once the OCC finalizes its rulebook, issuing an unauthorized dollar stablecoin becomes a crime. That clock forced every major financial institution to pick a side, and they chose to build rather than rent.

Circle's Arc blockchain is the proof. BlackRock, ICE (owner of the NYSE), DTCC, Visa, and MasterCard are all running validator nodes on a permissioned network. These aren't passive experiments; they're infrastructure commitments. Then, in September 2026, 21 banks including Goldman Sachs, Citi, and Bank of America announced a joint venture to launch their own regulated stablecoin, targeting the first half of 2027. Stripe, Visa, MasterCard, Coinbase, and 140+ companies back OpenUSD, which shares reserve earnings with distribution partners. You don't spend two years building a competitor to something you think is a fad.

The real story is the dollar acquiring a new distribution network. Every compliant stablecoin is backed by short-dated US Treasuries. A shopkeeper in Lagos holding digital dollars is financing the American government without a bank account. Tether's Treasury exposure was $141 billion as of Q1 2026, making it the 17th largest holder of US debt, above South Korea, Saudi Arabia, or Australia. ## Counterpoints

- Running a node might seem like a no big deal, but the scale of institutional commitment—21 banks forming a joint venture—proves they see it as the winning format.
- Some might think this is just finance adopting crypto, but the evidence shows it's the dollar acquiring a new distribution network, not crypto becoming money.
- Europe's defensive consortium and Lagarde's warnings show the move is seen as a threat to monetary sovereignty, not a neutral tech upgrade.
