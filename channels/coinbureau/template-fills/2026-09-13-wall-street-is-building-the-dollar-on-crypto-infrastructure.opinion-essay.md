## Thesis

Wall Street isn't absorbing crypto; it's moving the dollar onto crypto's infrastructure, and Washington still owns the currency.

## Argument

The mechanism is regulatory and infrastructural. The GENIUS Act, signed in July 2025, created a federal stablecoin framework with a hard compliance deadline in January 2027. That deadline forced every major financial player to pick a side: build or be left out. So they built. Circle's Arc blockchain launched with BlackRock, ICE (owner of the NYSE), DTCC, Visa, and MasterCard as named validators — a permissioned network, not an open one. Then, in September 2026, 21 banks including Goldman Sachs, Citi, and Bank of America announced a joint venture to launch their own regulated dollar stablecoin, targeting the first half of 2027. Stripe, Visa, MasterCard, Coinbase, and 140+ other companies are backing OpenUSD, a shared infrastructure that distributes reserve earnings. This isn't adoption; it's the dollar acquiring a new distribution network. Every compliant stablecoin is backed by short-dated US Treasuries, so a shopkeeper in Lagos holding digital dollars is financing the US government without a bank account. Tether's Treasury exposure was $141 billion as of Q1 2026, making it the 17th largest holder of US debt — above South Korea, Saudi Arabia, or Australia. Washington is counting on this: Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent said the stablecoin market could grow tenfold by the end of the decade, and the Treasury Borrowing Advisory Committee and Brookings have modeled the impact. Europe's defensive response — a 37-institution Euro stablecoin consortium called Quiverous and ECB President Lagarde's warning of 'digital dollarization' — confirms the stakes.
