## Thesis

Wall Street isn't absorbing crypto; it's moving the dollar onto crypto's infrastructure, and Washington still owns the currency.

## Argument

The mechanism is the GENIUS Act. Signed in July 2025, it creates a federal stablecoin framework with a hard compliance deadline in January 2027. Once the OCC finalizes its rulebook (targeted for November), issuing an unauthorized dollar stablecoin becomes a crime. That clock kicked off a race: BlackRock, ICE, DTCC, Visa, and MasterCard now run validator nodes on Circle's Arc, a permissioned network. These aren't experiments—21 banks including Goldman, Citi, and Bank of America formed a joint venture to launch their own regulated stablecoin by H1 2027, and Stripe, Visa, MasterCard, Coinbase, and 140+ companies back OpenUSD. You don't spend two years building a competitor to something you think is a fad.

The real story is dollar expansion. Every compliant stablecoin is backed by short-dated US Treasuries, so a shopkeeper in Lagos or an Argentine paying a supplier is financing the US government without a bank account. Tether's Treasury exposure hit $141 billion in Q1 2026—more than South Korea, Saudi Arabia, or Australia. Washington is counting on this: Treasury Secretary Bessent says the market could grow tenfold, and the crypto czar sees trillions in new Treasury demand. This is the cheapest dollar expansion available, no trade deals needed.

Europe's panic proves the point. 37 institutions across 15 countries formed a consortium to issue a Euro stablecoin, and ECB President Lagarde warns of 'digital dollarization.' Crypto built the system, but Washington owns the currency.

## Counterpoints

- Running a node is no big deal—but 21 banks forming a joint venture to launch a stablecoin shows they're not just participating, they're owning.
- Some might see this as crypto winning by becoming the infrastructure—but the host argues it's the dollar winning, taking over the network that was supposed to replace it.
