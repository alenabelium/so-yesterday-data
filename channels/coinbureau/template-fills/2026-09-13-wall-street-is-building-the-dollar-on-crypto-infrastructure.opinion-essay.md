## Thesis

Wall Street isn't absorbing crypto; it's moving the dollar onto crypto's infrastructure, and Washington still owns the currency.

## Argument

The mechanism is regulatory. The GENIUS Act, signed in July 2025, created a federal stablecoin framework with a hard compliance deadline in January 2027. Once the OCC finalizes its rulebook, issuing an unauthorized dollar stablecoin becomes a crime. That clock forced every major financial institution to pick a side, and they chose to build rather than rent.

Circle's Arc blockchain is the proof. BlackRock, ICE, DTCC, Visa, and MasterCard are all running validator nodes on a permissioned network—not a public chain. These aren't passive investments; they're operational commitments. Then, in September 2026, 21 banks including Goldman Sachs, Citi, and Bank of America announced a joint venture to launch their own regulated dollar stablecoin, targeting the first half of 2027. Stripe, Visa, MasterCard, Coinbase, and 140 others back OpenUSD, which shares reserve earnings with distribution partners. You don't spend two years in a consortium building a competitor to something you think is a fad.

The dollar is acquiring a new distribution network. Every compliant stablecoin is backed by short-dated US Treasuries, so a shopkeeper in Lagos or an Argentine paying a supplier is financing the American government without a bank account. Tether's Treasury exposure was $141 billion as of Q1 2026, making it the 17th largest holder of US debt—above South Korea, Saudi Arabia, or Australia. ## Counterpoints

- Running a node is no big deal—but 21 banks forming a joint venture to launch their own stablecoin shows they're not just dabbling.
- This looks like finance adopting crypto, but it's actually the dollar acquiring a new distribution network.
- Some might see this as crypto winning, but the host argues it's the dollar winning, taking over the network that was supposed to replace it.
