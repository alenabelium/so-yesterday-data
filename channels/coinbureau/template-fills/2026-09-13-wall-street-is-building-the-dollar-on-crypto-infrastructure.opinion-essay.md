## Thesis

Wall Street isn't absorbing crypto; it's moving the dollar onto crypto's infrastructure, and Washington still owns the currency.

## Argument

The mechanism is regulatory. The GENIUS Act, signed in July 2025, created a federal stablecoin framework with a hard compliance deadline in January 2027. Once the OCC finalizes its rulebook, issuing an unauthorized dollar stablecoin becomes a crime. That clock forced every major financial institution to pick a side, and they chose to own the rails rather than rent them.

Circle's Arc blockchain is a permissioned network, not an open one. Its founding validators include BlackRock, ICE (owner of the NYSE), DTCC, Visa, and MasterCard. These aren't experiments; they're named companies running infrastructure. Then, in September 2026, 21 banks—Goldman Sachs, Citi, Bank of America—announced a joint venture to launch their own regulated dollar stablecoin, targeting the first half of 2027, just after the compliance window shuts. Separately, Stripe, Visa, MasterCard, Coinbase, and 140+ companies back OpenUSD, which shares reserve earnings with distribution partners. You don't spend two years building a competitor to something you think is a fad.

The real prize is dollar dominance. Every compliant stablecoin is backed by short-dated US Treasuries. A shopkeeper in Lagos holding digital dollars is financing the US government without a bank account. Tether's Treasury exposure was $141 billion as of Q1 2026, making it the 17th largest holder of US debt—above South Korea, Saudi Arabia, or Australia. ## Counterpoints

- Running a node is no big deal—but 21 banks forming a joint venture to launch their own stablecoin shows they think the format has already won.
- Some might see this as finance adopting crypto, but it's actually the dollar acquiring a new distribution network.
- Europe's defensive Euro stablecoin consortium and Lagarde's 'digital dollarization' warning show the geopolitical stakes.
