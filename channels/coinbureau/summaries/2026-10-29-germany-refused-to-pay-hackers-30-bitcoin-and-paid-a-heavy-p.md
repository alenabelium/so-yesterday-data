# Germany REFUSED To Pay Hackers 30 Bitcoin... And Paid A Heavy Price

## Executive Summary

This video from Coin Bureau examines the 2026 ransomware attack on Berlin's government network by the Rhysida group, which stole 5.8 terabytes of confidential data and demanded 30 bitcoins. Berlin refused to pay, leading to the public release of sensitive files, including personal data of citizens and employees. The video highlights the vulnerabilities in Germany's digital infrastructure, such as plain-text passwords and fragmented network security, and draws parallels with similar attacks on the British Library and Ireland's health service. It concludes by questioning whether governments should ever pay ransoms, given the long-term consequences of data breaches.

## Key Points

- Berlin refused to pay the 30 bitcoin ransom demanded by the Rhysida ransomware group, leading to the public release of 5.8 terabytes of stolen government data. [00:00:00](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=1MpPSlFT2SY&t=0)
- The attack began with a fake captcha that tricked an employee into running a command, and the attackers exploited plain-text passwords stored in office files to navigate the network. [00:02:43](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=1MpPSlFT2SY&t=163)
- After the deadline passed, Rhysida published the stolen data, including personal information of citizens and employees, leading to phishing scams using real case numbers. [00:07:16](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=1MpPSlFT2SY&t=436)
- Germany's digital infrastructure is fragmented and outdated, as seen in previous attacks like the 2019 Emotet infection at Berlin's appeal court and the failure to meet the OZG digitization deadline. [00:09:08](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=1MpPSlFT2SY&t=548)
- Similar ransomware attacks on the British Library and Ireland's health service show that refusing to pay often leads to data leaks and prolonged disruption, raising the question of whether governments should ever pay. [00:10:10](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=1MpPSlFT2SY&t=610)
