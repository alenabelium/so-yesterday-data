## 5. Anthropic's 'public benefit' fund criticized as PR move, sparking governance debate

Critics, including David Shapiro, argue Anthropic's 5% share donation to a public benefit fund is an LLC owned by Anthropic itself, keeping control in-house rather than transferring real ownership to the public. This fuels a broader debate on AI governance and whether companies should cede control to sovereign wealth funds or other independent entities.

<!--detail
Anthropic's announcement that it would donate 5% of its shares to a public benefit fund was met with immediate skepticism, and the criticism cuts to the heart of what "public benefit" actually means when a company controls the vehicle meant to deliver it. David Shapiro's central objection is structural: the fund is an LLC owned by Anthropic itself, meaning the company retains ultimate authority over how those resources are deployed. This is not a transfer of ownership to the public, but a self-administered gesture that keeps decision-making power in-house while projecting an image of altruism. The distinction matters because the fund's governance structure determines whether it can ever act against Anthropic's interests, which is precisely when public benefit provisions would be tested.

The deeper tension in the critique is between two competing models of accountability. Shapiro advocates for a sovereign wealth fund model, where shares would be transferred to a government-owned entity with transparent governance, adherence to the Santiago principles, and permanent public ownership. This approach would create genuine external oversight and tax incentives, but it also raises questions about government competence and political capture. Anthropic's in-house fund, by contrast, offers speed and alignment with company strategy, but at the cost of meaningful independence. Neither model is obviously superior, and the debate exposes a fundamental unresolved question: who should hold power over AI companies that are increasingly positioned as stewards of transformative technology?

For practitioners driving AI transformation, this governance debate is not academic. The structure of public benefit commitments directly affects how organizations can be held accountable, and the choice between internal funds and external oversight will shape whether AI companies can credibly claim to serve the public interest. The practical implication is that governance design must be treated as a first-class concern, not an afterthought. When evaluating AI partnerships or investments, practitioners should scrutinize not just the stated mission, but the actual control mechanisms and whether they create real accountability or merely perform it. The gap between rhetoric and structure is where trust is either built or broken.
