Moonshot AI released Kimi K3.1 Pro, a 3-trillion-parameter model that benchmarks show matches or beats leading models like Claude and GPT-6 while being significantly cheaper per task. This signals a major shift in the economics of frontier AI, where efficiency and cost-effectiveness are becoming as important as raw capability.

This democratizes advanced biological AI, enabling smaller labs and researchers to leverage state-of-the-art genomics without massive compute budgets.

While the speed is impressive, the results are preliminary and lack experimental validation, and critics note the emphasis on agent count may be hype ahead of OpenAI's IPO.

## 4. Anthropic's 'public benefit' fund criticized as PR move, sparking governance debate

Critics like David Shapiro argue Anthropic's donation of 5% of shares to a public benefit fund is misleading because the fund is an LLC owned by Anthropic, not a truly independent public entity. This raises questions about AI companies' governance and accountability, with calls for sovereign wealth fund models to ensure real public benefit.

<!--detail
Anthropic’s announcement that it would dedicate 5% of its shares to a "public benefit fund" has ignited a sharp governance debate, with critic David Shapiro dismissing the move as "ostentatious moralizing" rather than substantive accountability. The core of the dispute is structural: the fund is an LLC wholly owned by Anthropic, meaning the company retains ultimate control over how those resources are deployed. Shapiro argues this creates an inherent conflict of interest—the entity meant to serve the public is legally and financially indistinguishable from the corporation whose interests it is supposed to check. In his view, this is less a transfer of power than a branding exercise, particularly given the astronomical valuations projected for frontier AI firms.

Shapiro’s proposed alternative is a sovereign wealth fund model, where shares would be transferred to a government-owned entity with transparent governance, adherence to the Santiago principles, and permanent public ownership. This framing exposes a fundamental tension: whether public benefit can be achieved through corporate self-governance or requires genuine external oversight. The critique also extends to the broader "AI psychosis" he attributes to leaders like Dario Amodei and Sam Altman, whose aggressive growth projections he sees as detached from realistic assessments of risk and value.

For practitioners driving AI transformation, this debate is a cautionary tale about the optics versus the substance of governance. When organizations tout ethical commitments, the legal structure matters as much as the rhetoric. A fund that is controlled by the same entity it is meant to oversee will not satisfy stakeholders demanding real accountability. The actionable lesson is to design governance mechanisms with independent teeth—whether through external trustees, government partnerships, or binding public reporting—rather than self-referential structures that invite skepticism. In an era of heightened scrutiny, the credibility of AI ethics initiatives will hinge on whether they transfer actual power, not just perceived goodwill.
