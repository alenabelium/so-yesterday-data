# Daily Digest — November 27, 2026

The 5 most important AI developments right now, ranked by importance.

## 1. Google DeepMind's SIM enables continuous recursive self-improvement of AI harnesses

Google DeepMind's new paper introduces Self-Improvement Memory (SIM), which records the full history of an AI's problem-solving attempts—root causes, rationales, and outcomes—allowing the system to optimize its surrounding harness over time and transfer that knowledge across different models. This shifts value from the model itself to the accumulated knowledge, making models interchangeable while the harness becomes the key to performance. It marks a significant step toward autonomous, compounding AI improvement.

<!--detail
Google DeepMind's latest paper on Self-Improvement Memory (SIM) reframes the locus of AI value creation. Rather than attempting to recursively improve the model's weights—a notoriously unstable endeavor—SIM targets the "harness," the software wrapper governing how a model interacts with tools and environments. The system logs not just successful outputs but the entire problem-solving trajectory: root causes of failures, the rationale behind chosen fixes, and the outcomes. This structured memory becomes a transferable asset, allowing a harness optimized on one model to be ported to another without the typical performance cliff. In Terminal Bench tests, the system demonstrated genuine compounding learning, such as installing a "blocking shutter" and later recalling its purpose to avoid destructive edits.

The source video surfaces a critical tension: while SIM's cross-model transfer is its headline achievement, the paper itself acknowledges that naive harness transfer usually degrades performance. The breakthrough is not that harnesses are universally portable, but that SIM's rich contextual records make portability possible. This distinction matters—it suggests the method's value lies less in the memory itself and more in the structured causal reasoning embedded within it. The video also notes that multiple heterogeneous models (Grok, Claude, Gemini) can collaboratively improve a shared toolkit, performing on par with model-specific tools, which hints at a future where model identity becomes commoditized.

For practitioners, the implication is strategic: invest in the scaffolding, not just the model. Organizations should treat their AI harnesses—prompt chains, tool integrations, evaluation loops—as first-class intellectual property. SIM suggests that a well-documented improvement history is more durable than any single model upgrade. The actionable move is to build instrumentation that captures the "why" behind every AI action, creating an organizational memory that survives model churn and accelerates onboarding of new systems. The competitive moat shifts from proprietary weights to proprietary process knowledge.

## 2. Anthropic proves VP≠VNP, a baby version of P vs. NP, with AI and Lean

Anthropic announced a proof that VP is not equal to VNP over every field, a result in algebraic complexity theory discovered by an internal Claude model and formalized in Lean. This establishes a super-polynomial lower bound for the permanent and Hamiltonian cycle polynomials, providing weak abductive evidence that P is not equal to NP. It represents another major AI-driven breakthrough in mathematics, further collapsing the complexity class zoology.

<!--detail
Anthropic has announced a formal proof that VP ≠ VNP over every field, a landmark separation in algebraic complexity theory that was discovered by an internal Claude model and verified in Lean. The result establishes a super-polynomial lower bound for the permanent and Hamiltonian cycle polynomials—specifically, requiring more than m^(c·∛log m) gates—which separates these algebraic complexity classes. This is widely described as a "baby version" of the P vs. NP question, and the proof provides weak abductive evidence that P ≠ NP in the Boolean setting.

The source video is careful to hedge the significance of this result. The key caveat is that arithmetic circuits must work over all field extensions, whereas Boolean circuits do not, so the separation does not directly transfer to the classical P vs. NP problem. The video frames this as a genuine step forward in algebraic complexity theory, but not a resolution of the central question. There is also an implicit tension in how to interpret the AI's role: the proof was discovered by Claude, but the formalization in Lean suggests that human verification remains essential. The community reaction on Hacker News, as noted in the video, reflects a mix of excitement about AI-driven mathematics and skepticism about how much this specific result advances the broader agenda.

For practitioners driving AI transformation, this development is a concrete demonstration that AI can now produce novel, verifiable mathematical results—not just assist with computation or pattern recognition. The implication is that organizations should treat AI as a genuine research partner, capable of generating hypotheses and proofs that humans can then verify. However, the Lean formalization requirement is the critical safeguard: it ensures that AI-generated results are trustworthy enough to build upon. The practical lesson is to invest in verification infrastructure alongside AI capabilities, because the value of an AI-discovered result is only as strong as the formal assurance that it is correct. This is a template for how AI can accelerate discovery in any domain where rigorous verification is possible.

## 3. OpenAI proposes an AI 'speed limit' via perplexity chart, signaling a safety cartel

OpenAI published its pre-training perplexity chart, proposing a common 'speed limit' for AI development. Analysts argue this is less about safety and more about signaling OpenAI's regained pre-training capability and potentially forming a safety cartel to control the pace of frontier AI. This move could reshape competitive dynamics and regulatory discussions around AI development.

<!--detail
OpenAI’s release of its pre-training perplexity chart is a masterclass in strategic ambiguity. On its face, the publication is a technical transparency play: sharing loss curves to benchmark model efficiency. But the accompanying proposal for a common “speed limit” on AI development transforms a data dump into a policy intervention. The chart signals that OpenAI has regained its pre-training edge, and by framing the next phase of competition as a matter of collective restraint, the company positions itself as both the pace-setter and the gatekeeper of frontier progress.

The source video’s analysts are sharply divided on the motive. One reading is that this is a genuine safety mechanism, a way to prevent a destabilizing race where labs cut corners on alignment. The other, more cynical interpretation—which the hosts lean into—is that this is a “safety cartel” in the making. By proposing a speed limit, OpenAI effectively asks rivals to agree to a ceiling that OpenAI itself is closest to hitting. It converts a technical advantage into a regulatory moat, making it harder for smaller labs or state-backed players to catch up without appearing reckless. The tension is unresolved in the video: is this a responsible actor sharing the brakes, or a dominant firm writing the rules of the road?

For practitioners, the implication is concrete. The perplexity chart is not just a model card; it is a template for how frontier labs will weaponize transparency. When evaluating AI vendors, ask what they are not publishing alongside their metrics. A lab that shares loss curves but withholds inference costs or alignment stress-test results is curating a narrative. More importantly, the “speed limit” debate will reshape procurement. If a cartel forms, enterprises may face a choice between compliant, slower-moving frontier models and faster, riskier alternatives. Your organization’s AI governance framework should anticipate this: build internal benchmarks for safety and capability that do not rely solely on vendor self-reported charts, and prepare for a market where the fastest model may not be the most strategically prudent one.
