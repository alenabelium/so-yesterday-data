## 3. Chain Theory derives fine structure constant to 10 digits, proposing a new foundation for physics

Alex Wissner-Gross's Chain Theory rebuilds physics from quantum information, deriving a parameter-free candidate for the fine structure constant that matches measurements to 10 significant digits. This contrasts with string theory and anthropic approaches, offering a testable, single-axiom framework that could redefine fundamental physics.

<!--detail
Chain Theory, the new research program from Alex Wissner-Gross, makes a startling claim: it derives the fine structure constant—the dimensionless number governing the strength of electromagnetic interactions—to 10 significant digits from a single axiom. The axiom is deceptively simple: reality consists of finitely many maximally entangled pairs of finite-dimensional quantum systems. From this, Wissner-Gross produces a candidate value of 137.035999189566 for the inverse fine structure constant, sitting just 0.60 standard deviations from the CODATA 2022 recommendation. This is not a fit; it is a derivation with no free parameters.

The theory's ambition is to reinterpret three long-standing puzzles—the finite information bound of the universe, the specific gauge group of the standard model, and the point-like nature of particles—as clues pointing toward a quantum-informational foundation. Where string theory posits extra dimensions and the anthropic principle shrugs at environmental selection, Chain Theory insists the constants are logically necessary consequences of entanglement structure. The source material, however, reveals a significant tension: the theory's own framework admits that only two of its six link types—those corresponding to spacetime and electromagnetism—are directly observable. The other four are "dark," observable only at second order. This is both the theory's boldest move and its most vulnerable point: it explains why we see so little of the fundamental structure while simultaneously making falsification extraordinarily difficult.

For practitioners driving AI transformation, the lesson is less about physics and more about epistemic discipline. Chain Theory demonstrates the power of first-principles derivation over incremental fitting—a mindset directly transferable to building AI systems that reason from core axioms rather than pattern-matching training data. But it also warns of the seduction of elegant frameworks that explain everything while remaining stubbornly hard to test. The actionable takeaway: pursue unifying principles aggressively, but demand that your organization's "derivations" specify, in advance, which observable outcomes would falsify them. A theory that explains why you cannot see its foundations is often just a theory that cannot be wrong.
