## 1. Anthropic's Claude cracks the mystery of NIST ECDSA curve seeds, boosting trust in global cryptography

Claude reverse-engineered the origins of the NIST ECDSA curve seeds, revealing they were generated with a weak pseudo-random number generator seeded by 1997-1998 Unix timestamps—not a deliberate backdoor. This resolves a decades-old cryptographic mystery and significantly increases confidence in the security of the curves underpinning much of the internet's encryption.

<!--detail
The cryptographic community has long harbored a quiet unease about the NIST ECDSA curves, whose "random" seeds were published without explanation, fueling speculation about a possible backdoor. Anthropic's Claude has now effectively closed that chapter. By reverse-engineering the seeds, Claude determined they were produced by the `lrand48` pseudo-random number generator, seeded with Unix timestamps clustered in 1997–1998. This is a mundane, almost anticlimactic origin—no malicious selection, no hidden trapdoor, just a developer's routine choice of a weak PRNG at the time. The finding dramatically increases confidence in the integrity of the curves that secure a vast portion of internet traffic.

The source videos, however, do not present a fully closed case. While the seed mystery is solved, Claude could only account for some of the base points, using a different generator (Arjen Lenstra's long integer arithmetic package). The base points for P224, P256, P384, and P521 remain unexplained, leaving a residual gap in the forensic record. There is also a subtle tension in the narrative: the discovery of a weak PRNG is reassuring for backdoor concerns, but it simultaneously highlights that the original generation process was far from rigorous—a reminder that "not malicious" is not the same as "cryptographically pristine."

For practitioners driving AI transformation, this is a powerful case study in AI-assisted audit. It demonstrates that large language models can perform genuine forensic reverse-engineering on legacy systems, uncovering provenance that human analysts missed for decades. The actionable implication is twofold: first, treat AI as a first-class tool for cryptographic and codebase audits, not just for generation but for historical reconstruction; second, recognize that the value here was not raw compute but the ability to hypothesize, test, and iterate across a vast search space. Organizations should build workflows that pair AI's pattern-finding with human cryptographic expertise to validate findings, especially where the stakes involve trust in foundational infrastructure.
