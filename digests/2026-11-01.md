## 2. OpenAI's scientific breakthroughs spark open-access debate in mathematics

OpenAI's proofs of the Exponential Time Hypothesis, local smoothing conjecture, and Yang-Mills existence have ignited a controversy over credit and access. The Math AI Advisory Group criticizes OpenAI's 'shepherding' model as elitist, advocating for open release—a debate that will shape how AI-driven discoveries are validated and shared.

<!--detail
OpenAI’s recent proofs—covering the Exponential Time Hypothesis, the local smoothing conjecture, and Yang-Mills existence—have landed like a depth charge in the mathematical community, but the shockwave is less about the theorems themselves and more about who gets to touch them. The Math AI Advisory Group, voiced by Dr. Samuel Allen Alexander, is not disputing the results; it is disputing the delivery. OpenAI’s practice of appointing specific human “shepherds” to steward each proof is being framed as a form of elite gatekeeping—what Alexander calls “kingmaking”—that grants privileged access to a chosen few while stripping the broader community of agency. The group’s counter-proposal is blunt: release everything, immediately, on independent repositories, with full process documentation and lean formalization.

The tension here is not merely procedural; it is epistemic. Alexander’s critique implies that the validation of AI-generated mathematics should be a communal, organic act, not a curated one. Yet the second video complicates the stakes by questioning whether these breakthroughs even matter practically. Reflecting on Trefethen’s pre-OpenAI essay, Alexander concedes that the Riemann hypothesis, P vs. NP, and even the now-solved Navier-Stokes problem have diminished real-world relevance—heuristics already handle NP-hard problems, and physical singularities likely don’t occur. This creates a strange paradox: OpenAI is being accused of hoarding discoveries that may be theoretically magnificent but practically inert.

For practitioners driving AI transformation, the actionable lesson is about governance, not mathematics. The debate exposes a fundamental choice in how AI labs release high-stakes outputs: curated shepherding versus radical openness. The former offers quality control but breeds resentment and perceived bias; the latter builds trust and community momentum but risks chaos. Organizations adopting AI for internal R&D should watch this closely—the “shepherding” model is essentially a template for how to manage AI outputs that exceed human comprehension. If the community rejects it, your enterprise governance should too. The future of AI adoption hinges less on the power of the models and more on the legitimacy of the release mechanisms around them.
