## 4. Michael Tomasello argues LLMs skip foundational agency, suggesting bottom-up AI development is needed

In discussing 'The Evolution of Agency,' Tomasello critiques LLMs for starting at language—the pinnacle of human achievement—while lacking the shared intentionality and perspective-taking that underpin true agency. This challenges the current scaling paradigm and suggests that building AI from the bottom up may be necessary for robust, context-sensitive decision-making in unpredictable environments.

<!--detail
Michael Tomasello’s critique of large language models is not merely a philosophical quibble; it is a direct challenge to the architectural assumptions underpinning the current AI boom. In discussing *The Evolution of Agency*, he reframes the problem: intelligence is not the primary lens for understanding adaptive behavior—agency is. Agency, defined as the capacity for flexible, context-sensitive decision-making in unpredictable environments, exists on a spectrum. Tomasello maps a hierarchy from the basic control systems of reptiles, through the executive intentionality of mammals, to the metacognitive rationality of great apes, and finally to the uniquely human capacity for shared agency. His core accusation is that LLMs begin at the apex—language—while skipping the entire evolutionary substrate that makes language meaningful. They lack the shared intentionality and perspective-taking that allow a human to infer a partner’s goals, not just predict their next token.

The source material introduces a subtle but critical tension: Tomasello’s prescription is speculative. He suggests that building AI "from the bottom up" may be necessary, but the video does not offer a concrete roadmap for instantiating reptilian control systems or mammalian executive function in silicon. This is a hedge, not a blueprint. The implication is that current scaling laws, which assume more data and compute yield more capability, may be hitting a ceiling not because of resource limits, but because they are optimizing the wrong variable.

For practitioners, this is a strategic warning. If Tomasello is right, then investments in ever-larger models for high-stakes, unpredictable environments—like autonomous logistics, crisis management, or clinical decision support—may yield brittle systems. The actionable takeaway is to prioritize context-sensitivity and multi-agent coordination over raw linguistic fluency. Organizations should pilot AI in constrained, predictable domains first, and design evaluation metrics that test for adaptive decision-making under novel conditions, not just benchmark performance on static datasets. The future may belong not to the most articulate model, but to the one that knows what a squirrel knows: how to act when the world refuses to follow the script.

The 'No AI Slop' Manifesto from the AIE Code Summit 2026 defines slop as content where creation effort is less than consumer validation effort, sparking debate on Hacker News. The proposal to version-control prompts instead of AI-generated code reflects a growing cultural shift in software engineering, emphasizing human judgment and accountability as AI-generated code becomes ubiquitous.

<!--detail
The "No AI Slop" Manifesto, emerging from the AIE Code Summit 2026, crystallizes a growing unease in software engineering with a deceptively simple definition: slop is content whose creation effort is less than the validation effort it demands from consumers. Dr. Samuel Allen Alexander's review amplifies this into a broader critique of accountability, noting that while AI can generate code, it cannot bear liability—the human remains the legal and ethical scapegoat, a role that contracting firms increasingly exploit. The manifesto's most provocative proposal is to version-control prompts rather than AI-generated code, effectively treating the repository as a record of human intent rather than machine output.

The source material reveals significant friction beneath the surface consensus. The Hacker News debate exposes a culture war between craftsmanship-oriented developers and profit-driven owners, with Dr. Alexander pragmatically advising practitioners to sell long-term support rather than artistic purity—a tacit admission that the manifesto's ideals may clash with commercial realities. There is also an unresolved tension in the prompt-versioning proposal itself: if prompts are the new source of truth, who validates the prompt's quality, and does this merely shift the slop problem upstream? The academic parallel—journals inundated with near-zero-cost submissions—suggests the issue is systemic, not merely technical.

For practitioners driving AI transformation, the actionable implication is clear: treat AI-generated code as a liability, not an asset. This means establishing explicit human review gates, documenting prompt provenance as part of the engineering record, and—critically—pricing your services around accountability and maintenance, not generation speed. The manifesto's real contribution is reframing developer responsibility: your value is no longer in writing code, but in vouching for it. Organizations that codify this distinction between creation and validation will build trust; those that blur it will inherit the slop.
