## 5. Tomasello's 'agency' framework critiques LLMs, arguing they skip foundational cognitive infrastructure

Michael Tomasello's new book 'The Evolution of Agency' argues that agency—not intelligence—is the key lens for adaptive behavior, and critiques LLMs for starting at language rather than building from shared intentionality and perspective-taking. This challenges the current scaling paradigm, suggesting that true AI agency may require bottom-up development, a provocative idea for AI researchers and builders.

<!--detail
Michael Tomasello's new book, *The Evolution of Agency*, reframes the AI debate by insisting that agency—not intelligence—is the fundamental unit of adaptive behavior. His argument is deceptively simple: agency is the capacity to make flexible, context-sensitive decisions when the environment is unpredictable, and it exists on a spectrum. He maps a hierarchy from basic reptilian control systems, through mammalian executive intentionality, to great ape metacognition, and finally to the uniquely human tier of shared agency, which enables culture. The critique of large language models is sharp: they begin at the apex of this evolutionary pyramid—language—while skipping the entire cognitive and social infrastructure that makes language meaningful. For Tomasello, an LLM is a squirrel that never learned to navigate a tree, yet is asked to write a field guide to forests.

The source video does not present a counterargument so much as a profound tension: Tomasello himself acknowledges that LLMs demonstrably perform feats of apparent reasoning and creativity, yet he insists this is not agency. The hedge is implicit but critical—if agency requires embodied, evolutionary scaffolding like shared intentionality and perspective-taking, then no amount of scaling will produce it. This directly challenges the dominant paradigm that emergent capabilities arise from scale alone. The implication for practitioners is uncomfortable but actionable: if Tomasello is right, then investing in ever-larger models is optimizing for a ceiling. The strategic pivot is toward building AI that develops bottom-up—starting with goal-directed interaction with environments, then social coordination, before language. For organizations, this suggests diversifying AI portfolios beyond pure language models, exploring embodied agents, multi-agent systems with genuine perspective-taking, and architectures that learn from feedback loops rather than static corpora. The question is no longer "how smart can we make it," but "what kind of agent do we want to build."
