## 1. Anthropic's 1,000-agent team formalizes Fermat's Last Theorem in 28.7 hours

Anthropic ran an experiment where 1,000 agents using an early version of Claude Fable 5.2 formalized Fermat's Last Theorem in Lean in under 29 hours—a dramatic speedup from the 11 days it took in September. The agents self-organized into crews and a task tree without human steering, demonstrating that multi-agent scaling can tackle long-horizon problems and potentially accelerate mathematical research by orders of magnitude.

<!--detail
Anthropic’s latest experiment marks a decisive shift in how we should think about AI’s role in knowledge work: it is no longer about a single model completing a task, but about orchestrating a temporary workforce. By deploying 1,000 agents running an early version of Claude Fable 5.2, the company formalized Fermat’s Last Theorem in Lean in 28.7 hours—a staggering reduction from the 11 days required in September. The achievement is notable not just for the speed, but for the efficiency: the team used one-third the tokens and produced a proof one-seventh the length of the original. Crucially, this was accomplished with general-purpose tools like Git and SQL, without the specialized ProveToMe platform, suggesting that the bottleneck in complex problem-solving is shifting from tooling to orchestration.

The source video adds a critical layer of nuance that complicates the triumphalist narrative. While the agents self-organized into crews and a task tree without human steering, their internal judgment proved wildly unreliable: helpers initially deemed 47 of 60 statements infeasible, yet went on to prove most of them. This tension—between emergent capability and flawed self-assessment—is the real story. The system can execute long-horizon tasks, but it cannot yet estimate its own difficulty curve, meaning human oversight must focus on triage and goal-setting rather than micromanagement. The safety architecture (sandboxing, real-time classifiers, offline monitors) also reveals that scaling agent counts multiplies risk surface area, requiring layered governance rather than a single guardrail.

For practitioners driving AI transformation, the implication is twofold. First, multi-agent scaling is now a viable strategy for tackling problems previously considered out of reach, but it demands a shift from evaluating individual outputs to designing task decomposition and coordination protocols. Second, the unreliability of AI’s self-assessment means organizations must build feedback loops that validate agent claims against ground truth, not trust their confidence scores. The future of AI-driven research is not a single brilliant model, but a managed swarm—and the management layer is where human value now resides.

This contrasts with the expensive, slow frontier models like Fable 5.2, highlighting a growing split between high-cost, high-capability models and efficient, low-cost models for routine work—a key factor in the K-shaped adoption economy.

At 2.5 times the speed of its predecessor, the model is engineered for latency-sensitive workflows where frontier-model intelligence is overkill. This is not merely a product launch; it is an explicit market segmentation play that acknowledges the economic reality that most organizational AI usage does not require the cognitive ceiling of models like Claude Fable 5.2.
