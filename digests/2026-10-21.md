## 1. Claude reverse-engineers NIST ECDSA curve seeds, dispelling backdoor fears

Anthropic's Claude AI solved a long-standing cryptographic mystery by revealing that the NIST ECDSA curve seeds were generated with a weak PRNG seeded by Unix timestamps from 1997-1998, not a deliberate backdoor. This significantly boosts trust in the security of these widely used curves and showcases AI's power in cryptographic forensics.

<!--detail
For over two decades, a persistent shadow has hung over the NIST ECDSA curves: the suspicion that their seemingly random seed values were chosen to conceal a backdoor. Anthropic's Claude AI has now effectively dispelled that fear by reverse-engineering their origin. The model determined that the seeds were not the product of a malicious selection process, nor a hash of a humorous phrase as previously rumored, but rather the output of a weak pseudo-random number generator, `lrand48`, seeded with Unix timestamps from 1997-1998. The clustering of these timestamps within a single year dramatically narrows the search space, making any deliberate malicious selection practically impossible and significantly boosting confidence in the integrity of these foundational cryptographic primitives.

However, the resolution is not absolute. The source video highlights a critical nuance: while Claude cracked the seed generation, it did not fully explain the origin of the base points for several curves, including P-256, P-384, and P-521. Furthermore, one binary curve seed remains unexplained. This leaves a residual, albeit smaller, area of uncertainty. The tension here is between the triumphant headline—"backdoor fears dispelled"—and the technical reality that a complete forensic reconstruction has not yet been achieved. The discovery is a powerful vindication of the curves' integrity, but it is not a closed case.

For practitioners driving AI transformation, this is a landmark demonstration of AI's capability in advanced cryptographic forensics and mathematical discovery. The actionable implication is twofold. First, it provides a strong, evidence-based argument for maintaining trust in existing NIST curve infrastructure, potentially avoiding costly and disruptive migrations. Second, it signals a new era where AI can be deployed to audit and validate the foundational security assumptions of legacy systems, turning a tool of generation into a tool of verification. The lesson is to view AI not just as a productivity enhancer, but as a critical instrument for institutional risk assessment and trust-building in core technological infrastructure.

This marks a new era where AI lowers the barrier for both finding and scaling exploits, forcing a rethink of security patching and AI regulation.

This shifts the economics of vulnerability research fundamentally, compressing what once took skilled researchers weeks into days or hours.

For practitioners driving AI transformation, the implication is stark and operational. Moss Image, a 5B-parameter open-source text-to-image model, delivers high-quality results (realistic, anime, infographics) on GPUs with as little as 4GB VRAM, making it the best in its weight class. Its layout control and LoRA support promise rapid community adoption and customization, democratizing image generation.

The model arrives with a full toolkit: layout control via bounding boxes for precise object placement, LoRA support for style customization, and a turbo LoRA that cuts generation steps from roughly 30 to 8, delivering a 3-4x speedup. For practitioners, the actionable implication is about hardware democratization.
