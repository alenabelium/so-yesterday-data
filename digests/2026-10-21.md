## 2. Anthropic's Claude solves cryptographic mystery, boosting trust in ECDSA curves

Claude reverse-engineered the origin of NIST ECDSA curve seeds, revealing they were generated with a weak PRNG seeded by Unix timestamps from 1997-1998, not a deliberate backdoor. This significantly increases confidence in the security of widely used cryptographic curves and demonstrates AI's power in mathematical forensics.

<!--detail
The cryptographic community has long harbored a quiet unease about the NIST ECDSA curves: the seeds from which they were derived were published without explanation, fueling speculation about a possible backdoor. Anthropic's Claude has now effectively closed that chapter. By reverse-engineering the seed generation process, Claude determined that the seeds were produced by the `lrand48` pseudo-random number generator, seeded with Unix timestamps clustered in 1997-1998. This is a mundane, almost anticlimactic origin—a developer running a standard library function on a workstation, not a state actor crafting a trapdoor. The timestamp clustering is particularly significant: it shrinks the theoretical search space for any malicious seed selection to a one-year window, making a deliberate backdoor statistically implausible.

The source video, however, is careful not to declare total victory. While the curve seeds are explained, the base points for P224, P256, P384, and P521 remain unaccounted for, and one binary curve seed also resists explanation. The video notes that some base points trace to a different random number generator (Liarjen Lenstra's long integer arithmetic package), but the gap is a reminder that forensic reconstruction is not proof of innocence—it is evidence of likely innocence. The tension here is between the headline's confidence and the video's measured caveats: Claude solved the mystery, but not all of it.

For practitioners, the implication is twofold. First, this is a case study in using AI for adversarial forensics—not just generating code, but interrogating legacy artifacts for hidden assumptions. Second, it offers a defensible answer to the "what if the curves are compromised?" question that occasionally surfaces in security reviews. You can now point to a concrete, auditable explanation rather than a vague reassurance. The remaining unexplained base points are a footnote, not a red flag, but they are a useful reminder that AI-driven discovery accelerates scrutiny; it does not replace human judgment about what constitutes sufficient evidence.
