## 3. Tomasello argues LLMs skip foundational agency, suggesting bottom-up AI development is needed

Cognitive scientist Michael Tomasello critiques LLMs for starting at the pinnacle of human achievement (language) rather than building from foundational cognitive and social infrastructures like shared intentionality and perspective-taking. He argues that true agency—flexible, context-sensitive decision-making—requires a bottom-up approach, challenging the current scaling paradigm and offering a potential roadmap for more robust AI.

<!--detail
Michael Tomasello’s critique of large language models is not merely a philosophical quibble; it is a fundamental challenge to the scaling paradigm that dominates AI development. In discussing his book *The Evolution of Agency*, Tomasello argues that the field has inverted the natural order of cognitive evolution. LLMs begin at the apex of human achievement—language—while skipping the entire developmental substrate that makes language meaningful. His hierarchy of agency, from reptilian control systems to mammalian executive function, great ape metacognition, and finally human shared agency, positions language not as the foundation of intelligence but as its most recent and sophisticated expression.

The core tension in Tomasello’s argument lies in what he implies about the necessity of this bottom-up path. He does not claim LLMs are useless; rather, he suggests that without shared intentionality—the ability to coordinate attention and goals with others—and perspective-taking, models may achieve linguistic fluency without genuine agency. This is where the nuance matters: Tomasello is not saying current models are merely "stochastic parrots," but that they are missing the cognitive scaffolding that allows a squirrel to navigate an unpredictable environment or a child to understand another’s viewpoint. The implication is that scaling compute and data alone will not produce flexible, context-sensitive decision-making; it may require architecting for social cognition from the ground up.

For practitioners, this reframes the question of AI transformation. The immediate value of LLMs in automating language tasks is undeniable, but Tomasello’s critique suggests a ceiling on their autonomy. Organizations should not expect these systems to exhibit true agency in unpredictable, socially complex environments—customer negotiations, crisis management, or collaborative problem-solving—without significant human oversight. The actionable insight is to design workflows that treat LLMs as powerful linguistic tools embedded within human-driven agency structures, rather than as autonomous agents. Investing in hybrid systems that pair LLM fluency with human shared intentionality may be the pragmatic path forward, even as researchers explore whether bottom-up architectures can eventually deliver the foundational cognition Tomasello argues is missing.

## 5. AI 'slop-code' manifesto sparks debate on human judgment and version control for prompts

The 'No AI Slop' Manifesto from the AIE Code Summit 2026 defines slop as content where creation effort is less than consumer understanding effort, and argues that AI cannot replace human judgment, especially as a liability holder. The ensuing Hacker News debate reveals a culture war between craftsmanship and commercial pressures, with suggestions that prompts—not AI-generated code—should be under version control, signaling a shift in software development practices.

<!--detail
The 'No AI Slop' Manifesto, unveiled at the AIE Code Summit 2026, attempts to give a precise, testable definition to a vague anxiety: slop is content where the creator's effort is less than the consumer's effort to understand it. This framing inverts the usual productivity calculus, arguing that while AI collapses creation costs to near zero, validation costs remain stubbornly human. The manifesto's core assertion is that AI cannot assume liability, and therefore cannot replace human judgment—firms contracting AI work are essentially purchasing a scapegoat, a human who can be held accountable when the output fails.

The most provocative operational suggestion emerging from the debate is that prompts, not generated code, should be the artifact under version control. This is a quiet but radical inversion of software development practice: the repository becomes a record of human intent and decision-making, while the machine's output is treated as a disposable build artifact. Yet this is where the sources diverge sharply. The Hacker News reaction reveals a genuine culture war, not a technical disagreement. One camp sees AI-generated code as a threat to craftsmanship, a degradation of the discipline into mere assembly. For practitioners, the actionable insight is to stop debating whether AI output is "good" and instead focus on where human judgment is irreplaceable. The manifesto's asymmetry—cheap creation, expensive validation—suggests that the bottleneck in AI transformation is not generation but review. The culture war will not be resolved by manifesto or market, but by designing systems where accountability is explicit and the cost of understanding is budgeted as seriously as the cost of creation.
