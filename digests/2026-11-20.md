This milestone accelerates the economic viability of space travel and could reshape the space industry's cost structure.

That figure, if realized, collapses the cost curve by orders of magnitude, moving space from a government-subsidized endeavor to a volume business where the marginal cost of a seat approaches that of a long-haul flight.

The video also juxtaposes this achievement with cyber incidents affecting 178 organizations, hinting at a broader fragility—the same centralized systems that enable breakthroughs like Starship are vulnerable to single points of failure.

The incident, occurring alongside a Microsoft outage and an FAA ground stop, forms a troubling trifecta that reveals how a single point of failure in a content management or experimentation platform can cascade into systemic disruption. The source video frames this not as a series of isolated IT failures, but as a collective indictment of centralized infrastructure and the legacy systems that underpin it.

The hosts hedge on whether the answer lies in decentralization, which can introduce its own operational complexity, or in more rigorous vendor governance, which is easier said than done in a fast-moving procurement environment.

For practitioners driving AI transformation, the actionable implication is clear: resilience must be a first-class design principle, not an afterthought. ## 4. Michael Tomasello argues LLMs skip foundational agency, sparking debate on AI's path to true intelligence

In discussing 'The Evolution of Agency,' Tomasello critiques LLMs for starting at language rather than building from foundational cognitive and social infrastructures like shared intentionality. This challenges the dominant scaling paradigm and suggests bottom-up approaches may be necessary for genuine AI agency.

<!--detail
Michael Tomasello’s critique of large language models is not merely an academic quibble about cognitive architecture—it is a direct assault on the scaling paradigm that currently dominates AI development. By framing agency as the foundational lens for understanding adaptive behavior, he argues that LLMs have inverted the evolutionary sequence: they begin with language, the pinnacle of human achievement, while skipping the entire substrate of embodied, context-sensitive decision-making that makes language meaningful in the first place. His hierarchy—from reptilian control systems through mammalian executive agency, great ape metacognition, and finally human shared agency—positions language as the *output* of a long developmental process, not its starting point.

The source material reveals a significant tension that Tomasello himself does not fully resolve. He suggests that building AI from the bottom up, incorporating shared intentionality and perspective-taking, "may be necessary" for true agency—but this is hedged language. The video does not clarify whether he believes such bottom-up construction is *practically achievable* or merely *theoretically coherent*. This ambiguity matters: if agency requires the full evolutionary scaffolding he describes, then current LLMs are not just incomplete but categorically different entities, and no amount of scaling will bridge that gap. If, however, agency can emerge from language alone through sufficient data and compute, his critique becomes a philosophical preference rather than a technical constraint.

For practitioners, the actionable implication is strategic humility. Organizations investing heavily in LLM-based automation should recognize that these systems excel at linguistic pattern-matching but lack the foundational agency required for genuinely unpredictable environments. This suggests a pragmatic division of labor: deploy LLMs where language proficiency suffices, but invest in complementary systems—whether embodied agents, symbolic reasoning, or hybrid architectures—for tasks demanding context-sensitive decision-making. The debate also cautions against over-indexing on benchmark performance, which measures linguistic competence rather than the agency that Tomasello argues is the true marker of adaptive intelligence.

## 5. 'No AI Slop' manifesto gains traction, redefining quality standards in AI-generated code

The AIE Code Summit's manifesto defines slop as when creation effort is less than validation effort, and Dr. Alexander's analysis sparks debate on Hacker News about craftsmanship versus commercial pressure. This is shaping how developers and firms approach AI code quality, version control, and liability.

<!--detail
The 'No AI Slop' manifesto emerging from the AIE Code Summit is less a technical standard than a philosophical line in the sand, and its central definition is deceptively simple: slop is what happens when the effort to create something falls below the effort required to validate it. Dr. Samuel Allen Alexander's analysis sharpens this into a practical framework, noting that the asymmetry is brutal—creation cost approaches zero while validation cost remains stubbornly human-priced. This is not merely an aesthetic complaint; it is an economic argument about where value actually resides in the software lifecycle.

The source material reveals genuine friction, particularly around the manifesto's most provocative suggestion: that prompts, not generated code, should be the artifact under version control. This is a radical inversion of standard practice, and the Hacker News reaction shows a culture war beneath the surface. One camp sees AI as a craft-destroying force, while the other sees it as a profit enabler, and Dr. Alexander's pragmatic advice—sell long-term support rather than artistic purity—sidesteps the ideological fight entirely. The deeper tension is unresolved: if code isn't human-authored, who owns its defects? The manifesto's answer, that humans must remain the liability holders because you cannot sue an AI, is legally sound but operationally messy.

For practitioners, the actionable implication is clear: quality gates must shift from generation to validation. Organizations should invest in verification infrastructure, human review protocols, and contractual frameworks that assign responsibility for AI-produced artifacts. The manifesto's definition gives teams a measurable heuristic—if your team spends more time deciphering than building, you are producing slop, regardless of how fast the code appears. The firms that treat AI as a way to reduce validation costs, not just creation costs, will be the ones that avoid the liability trap.
-->
