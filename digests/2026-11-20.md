## 3. AI 'slop' manifesto sparks debate on human responsibility and version control for prompts

The 'No AI Slop' Manifesto from the AIE Code Summit 2026 defines slop as content where creation effort is less than consumer validation effort, and argues that AI cannot replace human judgment, especially as liability holders. The debate extends to software development, with proposals that prompts—not AI-generated code—should be under version control, reflecting a growing cultural tension between craftsmanship and commercial pressures.

<!--detail
The 'No AI Slop' Manifesto, emerging from the AIE Code Summit 2026, attempts to give a precise, testable definition to a term that has been floating vaguely through tech discourse: slop is content where the creator's effort is less than the consumer's validation effort. This asymmetry—creation cost approaching zero while verification cost remains stubbornly high—is the manifesto's sharpest contribution. Dr. Samuel Allen Alexander's review endorses this framing while extending it into uncomfortable territory: you cannot sue an AI, so human judgment remains legally and ethically non-negotiable, with contracting firms serving as "scapegoat utility" for liability.

The source material reveals genuine tension rather than consensus. The most provocative proposal—that prompts, not AI-generated code, should be under version control—is presented as a logical extension of the manifesto's principles, yet it immediately collides with the reality that codebases are already polluted with machine output. The Hacker News reaction layer exposes a culture war: developers who see craftsmanship as intrinsic value versus owners who see only commercial efficiency. Alexander's pragmatic advice—sell long-term support rather than artistic merit—sidesteps the manifesto's moral high ground, suggesting the debate is less about quality and more about who bears the cost of validation.

For practitioners driving AI transformation, the actionable implication is structural, not stylistic. The slop definition gives you a measurable criterion for AI adoption: if your team spends more time reviewing AI output than it would take to write the original, you have not achieved productivity—you have achieved delegation of effort to the wrong side of the equation. Version-controlling prompts rather than generated code forces a discipline where human intent is the artifact, not the machine's interpretation of it. This is a governance model that scales accountability, and it is the difference between treating AI as a junior developer and treating it as a tool that requires a human author.

## 4. Evolutionary psychologist argues LLMs skip foundational agency, suggesting bottom-up AI development

Michael Tomasello critiques LLMs for starting at the pinnacle of human achievement (language) rather than building from foundational cognitive and social infrastructures like shared intentionality and perspective-taking. He proposes a hierarchy of agency from reptiles to humans, suggesting that true AI agency may require building from the bottom up—a significant challenge to current scaling approaches.

<!--detail
The critique leveled by evolutionary psychologist Michael Tomasello reframes the AI debate by shifting the lens from intelligence to agency. His central argument is that LLMs are built backwards: they begin with the most complex human artifact—language—while entirely skipping the evolutionary scaffolding that makes language meaningful. Tomasello defines agency not as raw processing power, but as the capacity for flexible, context-sensitive decision-making in unpredictable environments. He maps a developmental hierarchy from reptilian control systems through mammalian executive function and great ape metacognition, culminating in uniquely human shared agency, which requires joint attention, perspective-taking, and shared intentionality. The implication is stark: current scaling approaches may be optimizing for linguistic mimicry while missing the cognitive substrate that gives language its adaptive purpose.

The source video does not present a direct counterargument, but it carries an implicit tension that practitioners should not overlook. Tomasello’s proposal is speculative—he suggests bottom-up AI development may be necessary, but he does not outline a concrete engineering path. The hedge is significant: he is not claiming that current LLMs are useless, but that they are fundamentally different kinds of systems, more akin to sophisticated pattern-completers than agents. This distinction matters because it challenges the assumption that more data and compute will eventually produce genuine understanding or autonomous decision-making.

For AI transformation leaders, the actionable takeaway is to stop treating LLM fluency as a proxy for capability. When deploying these systems in organizational contexts—where unpredictable environments and context-sensitive decisions are the norm—practitioners should design for human oversight and explicit agency boundaries. Tomasello’s hierarchy offers a useful diagnostic: assess whether your AI deployment requires only reactive control, executive decision-making, or true shared agency with humans. Most enterprise use cases fall into the first two categories, where LLMs can excel. The danger lies in assuming that linguistic competence implies the deeper social and cognitive infrastructure required for autonomous, trustworthy action.

This raises urgent questions about how to modernize legacy infrastructure to withstand AI-driven vulnerabilities and autonomous cyberattacks.
