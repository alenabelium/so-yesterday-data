# Daily Digest — March 16, 2026

The 5 most important AI developments from recent content.

## 1. The first open-source agentic AI physicist is released

Physical Super Intelligence (PSI), co-founded by Alex Wissner-Gross, released "Get Physics Done" (GPD) — the first open-source AI agent that can scope a physics problem, plan the research, execute derivations, run numerical verification, and package results autonomously. Supporting 16+ subfields from quantum field theory to astrophysics under Apache 2.0, GPD compresses the research cycle from weeks to hours and frames physics as the key domino for downstream breakthroughs in chemistry, energy, and materials science.

<!--detail
The release of "Get Physics Done" (GPD) by Physical Super Intelligence marks a significant departure from the typical AI research assistant paradigm. Rather than a chatbot that answers physics questions, GPD operates as a full research project manager: it scopes a problem, devises a research plan, executes derivations, runs numerical verification, and packages results in LaTeX, all while maintaining consistent notation and sign conventions across the entire project. The inclusion of an AI peer reviewer that checks dimensional consistency, limiting cases, symmetry constraints, and conservation laws is particularly notable, as it targets the class of subtle errors that traditionally consume months of human referee time. The agent runs inside existing developer tools like Claude Code and Gemini CLI, and its Apache 2.0 license makes it freely accessible.

The source material reveals a tension between the agent's autonomy and its practical limitations. While Wissner-Gross frames this as ending the "century-old bottleneck of scarce physicist-hours," the description of "autopilot mode" requiring "minimal human intervention" hedges on the degree of oversight still needed. The claim that physics is the "key domino" for downstream breakthroughs in chemistry and energy is an ambitious framing that assumes progress in those fields is primarily physics-limited, an assertion that practitioners in those domains might contest. The 16+ subfields supported, from quantum field theory to astrophysics, suggest breadth, but the video does not address how GPD handles the messy, ill-defined problems that characterize real industrial research versus textbook-style derivations.

For AI-transformation practitioners, the actionable implication is clear: GPD represents a template for domain-specific agentic systems that compress research cycles from weeks to hours. The lesson is not that physics is uniquely suited for automation, but that any field with well-established formal constraints—dimensional analysis, conservation laws, symmetry principles—can benefit from agents that enforce those constraints systematically. Organizations should identify their own "physics": the domain-specific rules and verification procedures that can be codified into an agent's workflow, enabling autonomous execution with confidence in the output's validity.

A Harvard Business School experiment at P&G found individuals with AI were 3x more likely to produce top-10% quality ideas. The implication for companies: remove overhead or lose your best talent to solo founding.

The Carta data points to a structural shift: AI has effectively commoditized the coordination layer that once required teams, meetings, and management. The source material is emphatic that the bottleneck was never talent — it was the friction of moving ideas through committees. The Harvard Business School experiment at P&G, where individuals with AI were three times more likely to produce top-10% quality ideas, reinforces this by showing that AI doesn't just augment output; it collapses the need for cross-functional consensus to reach quality.

The videos introduce a crucial tension, however, between the promise of individual empowerment and the reality of organizational design. The uncomfortable implication is that companies are now competing with the frictionless alternative of solo founding. If a top performer can ship in 30 days what a team planned for a quarter, the retention calculus has fundamentally changed.

For practitioners driving AI transformation, the actionable takeaway is blunt: you are not just deploying technology, you are dismantling overhead. He drew a firm line against AI legal personhood, arguing replicable agents with perfect memory would outcompete biological humans, and advocated for defensive co-scaling of safety alongside capability as the pragmatic middle ground.

<!--detail

By defining the "modern Turing test" as an agent autonomously turning $100,000 into $1 million within two years, he shifts the goalposts from conversational mimicry to economic execution. The timeline itself is aggressive but not unprecedented; it rests on the assumption that scaling laws will continue to transfer logical reasoning from coding and mathematics into broader domains of decision-making. What is more striking is the boundary he draws around this capability: Suleyman explicitly rejects AI legal personhood, arguing that a replicable entity with perfect memory would inevitably outcompete biological humans for resources. Both advocate for "defensive co-scaling" — dedicating a percentage of compute and headcount to safety while sharing best practices across labs — yet this pragmatic middle ground papers over a deeper question: if agents can pass the economic Turing test by 2027, why would the same reasoning not extend to solving novel scientific problems? Suleyman hedges here, arguing that abstract domains resist human oversight, which is a softer claim than it appears. It suggests that the bottleneck is not capability but verification, and that the two-year timeline applies only to problems where success is measurable in dollars, not in truth.

For practitioners, the actionable implication is uncomfortable but clear: the window for designing human oversight into agentic systems is closing faster than most organizational roadmaps anticipate. If Suleyman is right, the competitive advantage will not go to the firm that deploys the most capable agents, but to the one that builds the most auditable ones. Defensive co-scaling is not a compliance checkbox; it is a strategic posture. Organizations should treat the 2027 benchmark as a forcing function to define, today, what provable alignment means for their specific workflows — before the market defines it for them.

## 4. Four AI labs independently converge on the same multi-agent architecture

Anthropic, Google DeepMind, OpenAI, and Cursor all built the same decompose-parallelize-verify-iterate pattern without coordinating — suggesting this is a discovered solution to scaling intelligence, not a design choice. The convergence implies AI's "jagged frontier" was an artifact of single-turn interaction, and that "sniff-checking" (evaluating whether output is correct) is becoming the most valuable human skill across every job family.

<!--detail
Four independent labs — Anthropic, Google DeepMind, OpenAI, and Cursor — have converged on the same multi-agent coordination pattern: decompose a problem, parallelize sub-tasks, verify outputs, and iterate. The striking detail is that none of them coordinated. This isn't a design choice; it's a discovered constraint, like how human organizations independently arrived at division of labor. The pattern appears to be the fundamental structure for scaling intelligence, whether biological or synthetic.

The most provocative claim in the source material is that AI's "jagged frontier" — the puzzling inconsistency where models ace some tasks and fail at seemingly similar ones — was never an inherent property of intelligence. It was an artifact of single-turn interaction. When you ask a model to solve everything in one shot without organizational structure, you get jagged results. With a proper harness that decomposes and verifies, capabilities smooth out across most knowledge work. Cursor's coding harness, built to construct web browsers, ran for four days with zero human guidance and solved an open research-grade mathematics problem. The harness generalized far beyond its original domain.

But the source video hedges on one critical point: it frames this convergence as inevitable, yet doesn't address what happens when decomposition fails. Not every problem can be broken into verifiable sub-problems. Creative synthesis, ambiguous stakeholder trade-offs, and novel strategy resist clean verification. The video's optimism about smooth capabilities may flatten this residual jaggedness.

For practitioners, the actionable shift is clear. The question is no longer "can AI do this task?" but "can this work be decomposed into verifiable sub-problems?" That reframing changes hiring, training, and process design. The most valuable human skill becomes "sniff-checking" — the meta-competency of evaluating whether output is correct. As execution gets cheaper, the organizations that build delegation structures with explicit verification checkpoints will define the next era of work. Those that keep treating AI as a single-turn oracle will be left with the jagged frontier they never learned to smooth.
