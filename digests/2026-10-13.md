This reveals that power and permitting bottlenecks—not just chip supply—can derail AI expansion, and that the debt-financed structure of the AI boom is vulnerable to cascading financial risks.

## 2. Evolutionary algorithms with LLM mutation operators beat direct generation in code and simulation search

New results show that using LLMs as mutation operators in evolutionary loops outperforms zero-shot generation, beating 96% of human-written Core War warriors and enabling open-ended discovery of simulations via the ASEL algorithm. This validates a powerful alternative to direct prompting, with implications for automated programming and artificial life research.

<!--detail
The most striking finding in this week's digest is not that LLMs can write code, but that they fail at it in the most instructive way possible. When asked to generate Core War warriors zero-shot, LLMs produce mediocre results. But when the same models are repurposed as mutation operators inside an evolutionary loop—proposing small variations on existing programs that are then selected by a verifier—they beat 96% of human-written warriors. This is a fundamental reframing: the LLM is no longer the oracle that produces the answer, but a stochastic explorer that perturbs partial solutions, with the environment doing the selecting.

The source video adds a crucial layer of nuance by explicitly rejecting the "evolution is random" misconception. The speakers argue that evolution is a powerful combinatorial algorithm that builds on partial solutions, and that path dependency—the historical accumulation of small improvements—is what creates robust, generalizable representations. This is where the tension lies: the LLM's strength is its vast prior knowledge, but that same prior can anchor it to familiar solutions. The evolutionary loop breaks this anchoring by forcing exploration through mutation, while the verifier provides the selection pressure that pure prompting lacks.

The ASEL algorithm extends this logic to simulation search, using foundation models as critics to discover open-ended rules that produce emergent complexity. This matters enormously for practitioners. The actionable implication is that direct prompting is often the wrong tool for open-ended problems. Instead, organizations should build pipelines where LLMs propose variations, automated or human verifiers select, and the loop iterates. This turns the LLM from a single-shot generator into a component of a search system—a shift that applies to automated programming, simulation design, and any domain where the space of good solutions is too vast for direct generation. The lesson is not to ask the model for the answer, but to ask it for the next step.

## 3. AI-driven math community 'Beyond Enlaw' rapidly improves integer multiplication algorithm

Following OpenAI's proof that integer multiplication can beat O(n log n), the 'Beyond Enlaw' community has iteratively improved the exponent using a Git-based workflow, with the best kappa now at 1.48 * 2^-11. This showcases a new model of collaborative, version-controlled mathematics where AI-generated proofs are progressively refined by a distributed community.

<!--detail
The "Beyond Enlaw" community has turned a single OpenAI proof into a living, version-controlled research program. What began on October 6, 2026 as a marginal theoretical result—integer multiplication in O(n log^(1-2^-182) n) time—has been iteratively sharpened by a distributed group of humans and AI systems working through a Git-based workflow. The current best exponent, kappa at 1.48 * 2^-11, represents a dramatic improvement over the original, achieved not through a single breakthrough but through hundreds of small, verified refinements. This is mathematics as open-source software: every change is committed, reviewed, and checked against a formal proof assistant before being merged.

The source video adds crucial philosophical friction to this narrative of progress. Dr. Samuel Allen Alexander argues that the community's trajectory toward "kappa infinity"—the theoretical limit of these improvements—is not a purely mathematical question but a physical one, contingent on human choices about which AI models to use, which proof-checking frameworks to adopt, and which refinements to prioritize. A purely mathematical version of this result would require fixing a specific AI and a specific version of Lean, which would strip away the very dynamism that makes the community effective. He also sketches a theoretical meta-algorithm that could reach the limit directly, but dismisses it as impractical, requiring an oracle or a frozen AI-proof-checker pair that would no longer be "alive."

For practitioners, this is a template for how AI-transformation should work in knowledge-intensive domains. The lesson is not that AI generates final answers, but that it generates *candidate improvements* that gain value through a rigorous, transparent, and collaborative refinement loop. The Git-based workflow provides the missing governance layer: version control for ideas, formal verification for quality, and community review for trust. Organizations should stop treating AI outputs as deliverables and instead build infrastructure where AI-generated hypotheses enter a continuous improvement pipeline, with human judgment steering the direction and formal checks ensuring integrity. The math community has accidentally built the operating system for AI-augmented knowledge work.

These tools signal a shift toward AI-accelerated discovery in life sciences, with potential for industrial chemical production and environmental remediation.
