This is a concrete example of the broader trend toward cost-efficient AI usage, where the value shifts from model intelligence to orchestration and integration.

## 3. AI deciphers Gödel's notebooks, reconstructing a proof that passes Coq

Jan von Plato used Claude to analyze Gödel's handwritten notebooks, producing a 650-page reconstruction of a proof of the independence of the axiom of choice that passed the proof assistant Coq. This demonstrates AI's power in historical mathematical research, though skepticism remains about the novelty and hype.

<!--detail
The headline is deceptively simple: Jan von Plato used Claude to reconstruct a proof from Gödel’s handwritten notebooks, producing a 650-page reconstruction that passed the Coq proof assistant. But the real story is a collision between archival scholarship and AI-assisted discovery, and the source material is far more contentious than the celebratory framing suggests. The core claim — that Gödel was on the verge of proving the independence of the axiom of choice decades before Paul Cohen — is dramatic, but the video’s host immediately undercuts it with a crucial detail: the notebooks were already transcribed by John and Cheryl Dawson. This isn’t a case of AI cracking an undecipherable code; it’s a case of AI accelerating the analysis of existing, albeit difficult, primary sources.

The tension here is not about whether Claude did the work, but about what "deciphering" means and whether the result is genuinely novel. The host notes that Claude found 663 theorems, with roughly 400 verified, 200 repaired, and 50 false — a fascinating mix that reveals Gödel’s own critical annotations. This suggests the AI wasn’t just transcribing; it was engaging in a form of mathematical judgment, distinguishing sound reasoning from errors. Yet the host’s skepticism about the lack of broader academic discussion beyond the Guardian article is telling. If this were a paradigm-shifting result, where is the peer review, the conference talk, the independent replication? The absence of that ecosystem chatter is a red flag for practitioners who know that hype often outpaces verification.

For AI-transformation leaders, this is a cautionary tale about the difference between capability and credibility. The technical feat — using an LLM to reconstruct and formally verify a historical proof — is genuinely impressive and points to a future where AI can serve as a research assistant for dense, specialized domains. But the operational lesson is that AI outputs, no matter how polished or formally verified, require institutional validation. The Coq proof is a strong signal, but it is not a substitute for academic consensus. The actionable takeaway: when deploying AI in your organization, treat its outputs as high-quality drafts that still need human gatekeepers, peer review, and a clear chain of custody from raw data to final conclusion. The Gödel case shows the power of AI to unlock hidden value, but also the risk of mistaking a compelling narrative for a settled fact.
