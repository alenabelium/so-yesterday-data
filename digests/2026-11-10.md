For practitioners driving AI transformation, the implication is direct and urgent. The 60-20-20 rule—building for current and future capabilities—highlights a new discipline for product teams navigating AI's non-deterministic progress.

<!--detail

Tulsi, a PM at Google DeepMind, describes development as a "Potions lesson"—a process where the outcome is not engineered but emerges, often unpredictably, from a cauldron of compute, data, and architecture. The concrete shift is the codification of this uncertainty into practice: the "60-20-20 rule," where 60% of product work targets current model capabilities, 20% anticipates near-term improvements, and 20% builds for speculative leaps. This is a deliberate hedge against the non-deterministic nature of scaling, where a model might suddenly gain a capability no one predicted, or fail to improve in a domain everyone assumed was solved.

The source video reveals a significant tension beneath this tidy framework. While the rule implies a structured allocation of effort, Tulsi’s emphasis on "vibe" management suggests the opposite: that PMs must rely on intuition and qualitative sensing of model behavior, not just quantitative benchmarks. The video does not resolve whether the 60-20-20 rule is a rigorous planning methodology or a post-hoc rationalization of a chaotic process. It also hedges on the safety front, framing it as a matter of "confidence" through monitoring and collaboration, rather than a guarantee—an acknowledgment that even the builders cannot fully predict what their creations will do.

For practitioners driving AI transformation, the implication is profound: you cannot manage frontier AI like traditional software. Roadmaps must be elastic, and teams must be structured to pivot quickly when a model surprises. The actionable takeaway is to build evaluation and feedback loops that let you detect emergent capabilities early, and to allocate a portion of your portfolio to speculative bets. The "vibe" is not a luxury; it is a signal. An old interview with mathematician Yuri Manin is being re-examined, where he argued that proofs remain central even as computers influence mathematics, and that good proofs make us wiser. This resonates with current AI-assisted formal verification efforts, framing the philosophical shift in how proofs are created and trusted.

<!--detail
Yuri Manin’s 1998 interview, recently resurfaced, is less a prediction than a provocation. His core claim—that proofs are the only vehicle for communicating mathematical truth and that “good proofs make us wiser”—lands differently in 2026, when formal verification systems like Lean are not just checking arguments but increasingly generating them. The video’s host, Dr. Samuel Allen Alexander, does not simply celebrate Manin’s foresight; he uses it to expose a live tension. Manin accepted computer-assisted proofs as legitimate *if* they are “good proofs,” but he never defined what goodness means when the proof’s cognitive burden shifts from a human reader to a machine verifier. The host’s critique sharpens this: a proof that is formally verified may be *correct* without being *wise*—it can be a certificate of truth that offers no insight into why the theorem holds. This is the crux the sources refuse to flatten. Manin’s cultural argument adds another layer: he warned that public alienation from Enlightenment values threatens funding for pure mathematics. That warning now applies to AI-transformation teams, who face a parallel alienation—stakeholders distrust black-box outputs, yet demand automation. The actionable implication for practitioners is to treat formal verification not as a replacement for human reasoning but as a new genre of communication. When deploying AI for critical decisions, the goal should not be merely to produce verifiable outputs, but to design proofs—or audit trails—that make the organization wiser. This means investing in explainability infrastructure that translates machine-checked results into human-readable narratives, preserving the epistemic value Manin prized. The resurfacing of this interview is a reminder that the AI proof problem is not technical but cultural: the challenge is not getting machines to prove things, but getting humans to trust and learn from those proofs.
