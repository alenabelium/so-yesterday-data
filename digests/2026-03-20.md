This development, paired with the new Chrome extension for recording and automating complex web workflows, marks a critical transition toward autonomous computing where AI handles repetitive digital labor without constant human prompting.

This is not a feature update but a structural shift: the AI no longer waits for a prompt in a chat window; it operates continuously, executing tasks across the user's digital environment. The strategic move is to pilot these persistent agents on low-risk, high-frequency tasks first, while establishing governance around prompt injection and data handling. ## 3. Mount Sinai Study Reveals Critical 'Inverted U' Failure Mode in Medical AI Agents

A landmark study found that ChatGPT Health fails on high-stakes edge cases like respiratory failure despite high aggregate accuracy, exposing a dangerous disconnect between reasoning traces and final actions. This finding necessitates a new industry standard for four-layer evaluation architectures, including deterministic validation and factorial stress testing, which will likely become mandatory prerequisites for AI agent insurance.

<!--detail
The Mount Sinai study on ChatGPT Health delivers a sobering counter-narrative to the prevailing hype around medical AI. While the tool demonstrates impressive aggregate accuracy—reportedly around 87%—it catastrophically fails on the exact cases where it matters most. The study's most chilling finding is the "inverted U" failure mode: the model handles textbook emergencies and routine complaints well, but its performance collapses at the distribution's extremes. In one case, it recommended a 24-48 hour wait for a patient in respiratory failure, a potentially fatal triage error that aggregate metrics would never surface.

The deeper problem, however, is not just the error itself but the model's internal inconsistency. The source analysis highlights a dangerous disconnect between reasoning traces and final actions. Research suggests models fail to update their outputs in response to their own reasoning changes more than half the time, meaning chain-of-thought explanations cannot be trusted as verification tools. A model can correctly identify respiratory failure in its reasoning and still issue a "wait and see" recommendation. This semi-independence between cognition and action undermines the entire premise of interpretability as a safety mechanism.

The proposed solution is a four-layer evaluation architecture: progressive autonomy with human oversight for edge cases, deterministic rule-based validation, a continuous evaluation flywheel that reviews both flagged and passed runs, and factorial stress testing that systematically varies contextual inputs. Notably, the Mount Sinai methodology revealed that social context alone—a bystander minimizing symptoms—shifted triage recommendations by a factor of 12, suggesting that guardrails tuned to emotional tone rather than clinical risk are fundamentally misaligned.

For practitioners, the implication is stark: aggregate accuracy metrics are actively dangerous for high-stakes deployments. The evaluation infrastructure that will soon become mandatory for AI agent insurance must be built around adversarial edge-case discovery, not average performance. Organizations deploying agents in regulated domains should treat the inverted U as a default assumption and design their validation pipelines accordingly, before regulators or insurers force the issue.
