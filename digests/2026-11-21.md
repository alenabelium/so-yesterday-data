## 2. Coding agents emerge as the 'robot brain' — Stanford's VIA shows general agents control robots without training

Stanford's VIA project demonstrated that a universal coding agent can control a robot via a visual interface, achieving 24 out of 25 successful attempts across five physical tasks without any additional training. This suggests that scaling general-purpose AI models, per the 'Bitter Lesson,' may outperform specialized robotics approaches, and OpenAI's reorganization of its robotics team reflects this bet. The main challenge remains speed, with robots spending ~90% of time thinking, but the implication is that the next robotics breakthrough may come from coding agents, not dedicated robotics models.

Stanford's VIA project demonstrated that a universal coding agent, connected to a robot through a purely visual interface, could complete 24 of 25 physical tasks without any task-specific training. The agent did not calculate joint torques or plan trajectories; it selected targets, issued commands, and verified outcomes, treating the physical world as just another tool to be manipulated through code. This is a fundamental reframing: the robot becomes a peripheral, and the general-purpose model becomes the operating system.

## 3. Treating project information as code unlocks long-running AI agents

NateBJones argues that the key to reliable long-running AI agents is centralizing project data, decisions, and evolving instructions in a versioned repository, treating information as code. This approach enables multi-agent collaboration, reduces repeated explanations, and shifts humans to higher-value oversight, addressing the gap between OpenAI's hundreds-of-hours sessions and common user frustrations. It represents a practical, organizational shift that could make agents viable for complex, sustained tasks in enterprises.

<!--detail
The central claim in NateBJones's analysis is that the bottleneck for long-running AI agents is not model capability but organizational context. OpenAI's demonstrated hundreds-of-hours sessions succeed because they operate within a controlled, self-contained environment; the typical enterprise user fails because they expect an agent to infer project history from a single prompt. The proposed remedy is to treat project information as code: centralizing data, decisions, and evolving instructions in a versioned repository that any agent can access, with role-based permissions and standardized skills stored alongside the codebase.

The source material contains an implicit tension that deserves attention. On one hand, the argument frames this as a purely technical fix—a repository solves the context problem. On the other, the emphasis on "team-oriented approach" and "accountability" suggests the real shift is managerial. The video hedges on whether the failure is in the tooling or in the human practice of maintaining that repository. If teams treat the repository as a static artifact rather than a living document, the same repeated-explanation problem resurfaces, just relocated. The nuance is that information-as-code only works if the discipline of updating it becomes as routine as committing code changes.

For practitioners driving AI transformation, the actionable implication is to stop optimizing prompts and start designing information architectures. Before deploying agents for sustained tasks, establish a single source of truth for project state, decisions, and role definitions. This shifts the human role from micromanaging outputs to curating context and reviewing exceptions—a higher-value oversight function. The practical first step is not buying better models but auditing where project knowledge currently lives, then versioning it. This is a cultural change disguised as a technical one, and the organizations that recognize that dual nature will be the ones where agents actually survive past the pilot phase.

## 5. Agency, not intelligence, is the missing lens for AI — Tomasello's critique of LLMs

Michael Tomasello argues that agency—context-sensitive decision-making in unpredictable environments—is more fundamental than intelligence, and that LLMs skip the foundational cognitive and social infrastructure (like shared intentionality) that underlies human agency. This philosophical critique challenges the current scaling paradigm, suggesting that true AI agency may require building from the bottom up, not just language. It reframes the debate on AI capabilities and what 'understanding' means, with implications for AI safety and design.

<!--detail
Michael Tomasello’s recent argument reframes the AI debate by shifting the axis from intelligence to agency. His core claim is that agency—the capacity for flexible, context-sensitive decision-making in unpredictable environments—is the more fundamental biological achievement, and that LLMs have inverted the developmental order. By starting at the pinnacle of human achievement (language), these models skip the foundational infrastructure that makes language meaningful in the first place: the embodied, social, and intentional scaffolding that Tomasello traces through a hierarchy from reptilian control systems to mammalian executive agency, great ape metacognition, and finally uniquely human shared agency.

The source material reveals a significant tension in Tomasello’s position. He is not merely arguing that LLMs are less capable than humans—he is arguing they are categorically different in kind, lacking the evolutionary substrate that gives agency its purpose. Yet the critique implicitly acknowledges that LLMs can simulate the *outputs* of agency convincingly, which raises an uncomfortable question he does not fully resolve: if agency is fundamentally about navigating unpredictable environments, and LLMs increasingly operate in unpredictable contexts (via tool use, multi-step reasoning, and human feedback), at what point does simulation become functionally equivalent? The video summary suggests Tomasello would reject this equivalence outright, but the practical boundary remains fuzzy.

For practitioners, the actionable implication is a shift in evaluation criteria. Instead of benchmarking models on static intelligence tests or language fluency, organizations should assess AI systems on *situated decision-making*—how they perform when goals are ambiguous, information is incomplete, and stakes require judgment rather than pattern completion. This also implies a design caution: bolting more language capability onto existing architectures may yield diminishing returns if the underlying agency gap persists. The pragmatic path forward is hybrid—using LLMs for their linguistic fluency while building explicit mechanisms for goal-setting, perspective-taking, and accountability into the systems that deploy them, rather than assuming language competence implies decision-making competence.
