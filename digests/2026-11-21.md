For practitioners, the strategic signal is clear. ## 4. AI agency debate deepens: Tomasello argues LLMs skip foundational cognitive infrastructure

Cognitive scientist Michael Tomasello critiques LLMs for starting at the pinnacle of human achievement (language) rather than building from foundational agency and shared intentionality, suggesting true AI agency may require bottom-up development. This challenges the field's current scaling paradigm and adds a philosophical dimension to the pursuit of AGI.

<!--detail
The latest volley in the AI agency debate comes not from a lab but from comparative psychology. Michael Tomasello, in discussing his book *The Evolution of Agency*, reframes the entire conversation by arguing that agency—not intelligence—is the fundamental lens for understanding adaptive behavior. He proposes a hierarchy of agency, from the basic control systems of reptiles through the executive agency of mammals and the metacognitive tier of great apes, culminating in the uniquely human capacity for shared agency. His critique of large language models is pointed: they begin at the endpoint of human achievement—language—while skipping the foundational cognitive and social infrastructure that makes that achievement meaningful. For Tomasello, true agency requires the bottom-up development of shared intentionality and perspective-taking, not just statistical mastery of linguistic form.

The source material does not present a unified front; it carries an implicit tension between Tomasello's developmental account and the field's dominant scaling paradigm. While he argues that LLMs are missing something essential, the summary leaves open whether this is a hard philosophical claim about what agency *is* or a softer empirical claim about what current architectures *lack*. The hedge matters: if agency is a graded phenomenon, as his hierarchy suggests, then LLMs may possess a thin form of agency already, and the question becomes one of degree rather than kind. If agency requires the full evolutionary infrastructure, then no amount of scaling will suffice.

For practitioners, this is not idle philosophy. It reframes what "agentic AI" means in enterprise settings. If Tomasello is right, then deploying LLMs as autonomous decision-makers is building on sand—they may produce language that *sounds* agentic without the underlying capacity for context-sensitive, flexible judgment that unpredictable environments demand. The actionable implication is to treat LLM agency as a design constraint, not an emergent property: organizations should architect human oversight and shared intentionality into their AI workflows, rather than assuming that linguistic fluency confers genuine decision-making authority. The debate forces a choice between betting on scale or betting on structure.
