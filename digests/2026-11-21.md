## 2. Stanford's VIA project shows coding agents can control robots without specialized training

Stanford's VIA project connected a universal coding agent to a robot via a visual interface, achieving 24 out of 25 successful attempts across five physical tasks without additional training. This supports the 'Bitter Lesson' that scaling general models may outperform specialized robotics designs, though speed remains a major hurdle with robots spending 90% of time thinking.

<!--detail
The Stanford VIA project is a concrete demonstration that the "brain" of a robot may not need to be purpose-built for robotics at all. By connecting a universal coding agent to a robot through a visual interface, researchers achieved 24 out of 25 successful task completions across five physical manipulations, with zero additional training. The agent operates by treating the physical world as a tool: it selects targets, executes actions, and verifies results through visual feedback, mirroring how a coding agent interacts with an API or file system. This is not incremental progress; it is a fundamental reframing of the robotics stack, where the planning layer is borrowed wholesale from software engineering.

The source video leans heavily on Richard Sutton's "Bitter Lesson" to explain the result, arguing that scaling general models will outpace hand-crafted, specialized robotics designs. It also points to OpenAI's reorganization of its robotics team toward basic research as institutional validation of this bet. However, the video does not flatten the tension: the most glaring caveat is speed. In tests, robots spent roughly 90% of their time "thinking," making real-time interaction impractical for any task requiring rapid response. This is not a minor inefficiency—it is the difference between a robot that can sort objects in a lab and one that can catch a falling cup in a kitchen.

For practitioners driving AI transformation, the implication is strategic: do not invest heavily in bespoke robotics pipelines or domain-specific models when general frontier models are improving faster. The VIA result suggests that the bottleneck is shifting from model capability to inference latency and interface design. Organizations should prioritize building clean, visual or API-based interfaces between general agents and physical systems, and be honest about the latency trade-offs. The winning architecture may already exist; the challenge is making it fast enough to matter.

## 5. Cognitive scientist argues LLMs skip foundational agency, suggesting bottom-up AI development needed

Michael Tomasello critiques LLMs for starting at the pinnacle of human achievement (language) rather than building from foundational cognitive and social infrastructures like shared intentionality and perspective-taking. He argues that true agency requires a hierarchy of capabilities, and building AI from the bottom up may be necessary for genuine autonomous decision-making.

<!--detail
Cognitive scientist Michael Tomasello is reframing the AI debate by shifting the focus from intelligence to agency. In his recent work, he argues that the capacity to make flexible, context-sensitive decisions in unpredictable environments—not raw processing power—is the true benchmark of adaptive behavior. He proposes a biological hierarchy: basic control systems in reptiles, executive intentionality in mammals, metacognitive rationality in great apes, and uniquely human shared agency built on collaboration and culture. His critique of large language models is pointed: they begin at the apex of human achievement—language—while skipping the entire evolutionary and developmental scaffolding that makes language meaningful. For Tomasello, an LLM is like a squirrel that has memorized the entire forest map but never learned to navigate a single tree.

The source video does not present a counterargument, but it leaves a critical tension unresolved. Tomasello suggests that building AI "from the bottom up"—installing shared intentionality and perspective-taking before language—may be necessary for genuine agency. Yet he does not specify how such bottom-up construction would proceed in silicon, nor whether it is even feasible outside biological evolution. The implication is that current scaling approaches are fundamentally misguided, but the alternative pathway remains largely theoretical.

For practitioners driving AI transformation, this is a strategic warning disguised as a philosophical argument. Organizations that deploy LLMs for autonomous decision-making are betting on a system that excels at linguistic pattern completion but lacks the foundational agency to handle genuine unpredictability. The actionable takeaway is to design human-in-the-loop workflows that compensate for this missing infrastructure—particularly in high-stakes, novel situations where context-sensitivity matters more than fluency. Treat LLMs as powerful tools within a broader agency framework, not as autonomous agents themselves. The gap between language mastery and true decision-making is not a bug to be patched; it is a structural feature that demands architectural humility.
