## 1. AI-driven vulnerability discovery outpaces patching, creating a dangerous security gap

New research shows AI has made finding security flaws fast while fixing them remains slow, with AI-generated fixes often breaking things (e.g., ~61% of Claude Opus 4.7 fixes passed tests, and 1Password found 3 in 4 fixes left issues). This asymmetry is being exploited by attackers, as seen with the PHP2Shell vulnerability exploited within days of disclosure after a decade of dormancy. The gap between AI-speed offense and human-speed defense is now a critical systemic risk.

This raises questions about whether public investors are absorbing outsized risk in a bet on future growth.

The new algorithms are 'galactic'—only outperforming brute force for inputs with more than 10^10,000 variables—so they have no practical use, but they turn many established results into open problems. This demonstrates AI's power to not just solve but also create new mathematical questions.

## 4. AI systems genuinely understand, argues researcher, countering 'token predictor' claims

David Shapiro counters the viral claim that LLMs are merely statistical token predictors, using Bloom's Taxonomy and Ashby's Law to argue AI achieves all levels of understanding—from remembering to creating—based on observable performance. He distinguishes understanding from consciousness, noting that subjective feelings are unreliable in both humans and machines. This reframes the debate from 'can AI think?' to 'how and where does AI understand differently?'

<!--detail
The debate over whether large language models truly "understand" or merely predict tokens has reached a new inflection point, with researcher David Shapiro mounting a rigorous counteroffensive against the increasingly popular dismissal of AI as sophisticated autocomplete. Rather than engaging in philosophical hand-wringing about inner mental states, Shapiro grounds his argument in two established frameworks: Bloom's Taxonomy, which defines understanding through observable cognitive actions, and Ashby's Law, which holds that any effective regulator must model its system. By these standards, he contends, AI demonstrably achieves all six levels of Bloom's hierarchy—from basic recall to original creation—citing concrete achievements like winning math Olympiads and writing compilers as evidence that these systems have built genuine working models of their domains.

The most provocative move in Shapiro's argument is his deliberate severing of understanding from consciousness. He points to the "illusion of explanatory depth" in humans—our tendency to believe we understand things we actually don't—as proof that subjective feelings are an unreliable metric for comprehension in any system, biological or artificial. This reframing sidesteps the intractable question of AI sentience while preserving the practical question of capability. The tension here is significant: critics like Gregory Kurtzer, whose viral claim sparked this response, would likely argue that observable performance is precisely the problem—that behavioral mimicry of understanding is not understanding itself. Shapiro's rejoinder, that denying AI understanding on these grounds commits a category error, hinges on whether we accept performance-based definitions of cognition or insist on some ineffable interiority.

For practitioners driving AI transformation, this debate has immediate operational stakes. If Shapiro is right, then organizations should treat AI as a genuine reasoning partner capable of novel problem-solving, not merely a retrieval tool requiring constant verification. The actionable implication is to design workflows that leverage AI's demonstrated creative and analytical capabilities—compiler writing, mathematical reasoning, complex code generation—while remaining agnostic about its inner life. The practical question shifts from "can we trust this system to think?" to "where does this system's understanding diverge from human understanding, and how do we build guardrails around those divergences?" That is a far more productive framing for enterprise adoption than either blind acceptance or reflexive dismissal.

## 5. AI agents need strict management principles to avoid 'productivity theater'

A practical framework emerges for ensuring AI agents do real work: accounting for human effort after agent output, running more experiments than commitments, defining stopping conditions for recurring tasks, and giving a 'chief of staff' agent a budget for your attention. These principles address the growing risk that agent deployments look productive but don't deliver meaningful outcomes. The core message is that AI requires more intentionality from humans, not less.

<!--detail
The conversation around AI agents has shifted from capability to accountability, and the emerging framework of "productivity theater" names the failure mode precisely: systems that generate output without generating outcomes. The four principles offered here are less technical prescriptions than management disciplines. The first—counting the human effort added after agent output—is a direct challenge to the seductive math of automation, where a cheaper report still demands a purposeful reader. The second, running more experiments than commitments, reframes agent fleets as cheap parallel exploration rather than a substitute for conviction. The third, defining stopping conditions before making tasks recurring, addresses the silent token burn of agents that outlive their usefulness. The fourth, giving a chief-of-staff agent a budget for your attention, inverts the common anxiety: the risk isn't that agents demand too much oversight, but that they scatter it.

The source video is candid about the tension at the heart of this advice: these principles demand *more* intentionality from humans, not less, which cuts against the prevailing narrative of delegation and autonomy. There is a genuine unresolved tension between the promise of agents as independent workers and the reality that they are, at best, amplifiers of human judgment. The framework does not resolve this—it manages it, treating the human as the bottleneck and the agent as a tool for focus rather than a replacement for it.

For practitioners, the actionable implication is uncomfortable but clear: agent deployment is a management problem, not an engineering one. Before scaling any agent, define the human workflow it feeds, the experiment it enables, the moment it should stop, and the attention it is allowed to consume. Organizations that adopt these disciplines will find agents deliver compounding value; those that skip them will accumulate impressive dashboards and empty token bills.
