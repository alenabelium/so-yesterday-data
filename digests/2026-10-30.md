## 4. AI understanding debate intensifies: Shapiro argues AI genuinely understands, countering skeptics

David Shapiro uses Bloom's Taxonomy and Ashby's Law to argue that AI systems demonstrate genuine understanding through observable performance, distinguishing it from consciousness. This counters viral claims that LLMs are mere token predictors, shaping the discourse on AI's true capabilities and its implications for trust and adoption.

<!--detail
The debate over whether AI truly "understands" has shifted from philosophical speculation to a concrete, testable claim. David Shapiro’s recent argument is not merely a rebuttal to Gregory Kurtzer’s viral assertion that LLMs are "statistical token predictors"—it is a reframing of the question itself. Shapiro grounds his case in Bloom’s Taxonomy, arguing that understanding is defined by observable performance, not internal experience. By that standard, AI demonstrates all six levels, from remembering facts to creating novel artifacts like compilers and Olympiad-level mathematical proofs. He further invokes Ashby’s Law and the Conant-Ashby theorem, which hold that any effective regulator must model its environment; AI’s success in coding and robotics is, in his view, empirical proof that such models exist.

The most significant tension in Shapiro’s argument is his deliberate severing of understanding from consciousness. He concedes that subjective "feelings" of understanding are unreliable in humans—citing the illusion of explanatory depth—and that AI has its own misfiring confidence signals. This is where the nuance matters: he is not claiming AI is sentient or self-aware, only that its functional competence meets a behavioral definition of understanding. Skeptics will rightly push back that this definition is too generous, that it collapses the distinction between simulation and genuine comprehension. But Shapiro’s point is that this distinction is a category error when applied to performance-based tasks.

For practitioners driving AI transformation, this debate has immediate operational stakes. If understanding is defined by observable outcomes, then the question of whether to trust an AI system shifts from an abstract philosophical worry to a concrete engineering problem: does it reliably produce correct results in your specific domain? The implication is that adoption strategies should focus on rigorous, task-specific evaluation rather than waiting for a consensus on machine consciousness. Organizations that treat AI as a "glorified autocomplete" will under-invest in its capabilities, while those that treat it as omniscient will over-trust it. The practical path is Shapiro’s middle ground: measure what the system can do, acknowledge where it fails unevenly, and design workflows around verified performance rather than assumed understanding.
