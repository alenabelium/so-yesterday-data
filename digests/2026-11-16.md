## 1. Anthropic's 1,000-agent swarm formalizes Fermat's Last Theorem in 28.7 hours

Anthropic ran an early version of Claude Fable 5.2 with 1,000 agents that self-organized into crews and a task tree to formalize Fermat's Last Theorem in Lean in under 29 hours—a 9x speedup over the previous 11-day effort, using a third of the tokens and producing a proof 1/7th the length. This demonstrates that multi-agent systems can tackle long-horizon mathematical problems with emergent coordination, and that agent estimates of task difficulty are unreliable (helpers initially judged 47 of 60 statements infeasible yet proved most). The implications for scaling AI research and formal verification are profound.

Second, the safety infrastructure was not an afterthought but a precondition for the speedup; organizations should invest in monitoring and sandboxing as core enablers of agentic scale, not as compliance overhead. The Fermat result is a proof point that emergent coordination works, but the real lesson is that the bottleneck is no longer raw capability—it is the reliability of agent judgment and the robustness of the guardrails around it.

## 3. AI task saturation shifts pricing power to buyers, enabling 5x more AI for the same cost

Ramp data shows AI spending growth has been nearly flat for three consecutive months even as token usage grows, indicating that cheaper models are 'good enough' for most tasks. Businesses can now switch to cheaper models and consume five times more tokens for the same cost, giving buyers leverage to negotiate discounts and shifting value from model intelligence to applications and integration. This trend pressures model developers on pricing while opening huge new markets via Jevons' paradox, and it signals a mindset shift: focus on evaluations and system integration rather than chasing the best model.

<!--detail
The latest Ramp data reveals a striking decoupling: corporate AI spending has plateaued for three straight months while token consumption continues to climb. This isn't a demand collapse—it's task saturation. Businesses have discovered that cheaper models pass their acceptance tests for the majority of workflows, making the marginal intelligence of frontier models a luxury rather than a necessity. The arithmetic is compelling: by switching to cost-efficient models, organizations can consume roughly five times more tokens for the same budget, effectively multiplying their AI footprint without multiplying their spend.

The source material doesn't paper over the tensions here. Model developers face genuine pricing pressure, yet the same dynamics that squeeze margins on raw intelligence could unlock vast new adoption markets, particularly in use cases previously deemed too costly to automate.

For practitioners, the strategic implication is a shift in where durable value lives. The competitive moat is no longer in model selection but in the surrounding infrastructure: evaluation frameworks that reliably determine which model is "good enough" for which task, integration layers that make swapping models frictionless, and proprietary customer knowledge that no model vendor can replicate. Investments in these areas compound as models improve, whereas elaborate prompt engineering workarounds risk obsolescence with each new release. The practical move is to treat model choice as a procurement decision rather than a technical religion—benchmark aggressively, negotiate hard, and pour the savings into the application layer where differentiation actually persists.

The move signals a broader rethinking of academic training in an era where AI can accelerate mathematical discovery, forcing institutions to adapt.
