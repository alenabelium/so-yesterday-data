## 1. Anthropic's 1,000-agent team formalizes Fermat's Last Theorem in 28.7 hours

Anthropic's experiment with 1,000 agents running an early version of Claude Fable 5.2 formalized Fermat's Last Theorem in Lean in 28.7 hours—a dramatic speedup from 11 days in September, using 1/3 the tokens and producing a proof 1/7th the length. The agents self-organized into crews and a task tree without human steering, demonstrating emergent coordination for long-horizon problems. This marks a major leap in multi-agent scaling and could accelerate formal verification across mathematics and software.

<!--detail
Anthropic’s latest multi-agent experiment marks a decisive shift in what we can expect from AI-driven research: a fleet of 1,000 agents running an early version of Claude Fable 5.2 formalized Fermat’s Last Theorem in Lean in 28.7 hours—a dramatic compression from the 11 days required in September. The achievement is notable not just for the speed, but for the efficiency: the team used one-third the tokens and produced a proof one-seventh the length of the original, all while relying on general-purpose tools like Git and SQL rather than specialized theorem-proving platforms.

Helpers initially judged 47 of 60 statements infeasible, then proved most of them anyway. This tension between execution capability and self-assessment is a recurring pattern in frontier AI, and it has direct implications for practitioners: you cannot trust an agent’s confidence signal as a gating criterion for work allocation.

For organizations driving AI transformation, the actionable takeaway is twofold. First, multi-agent systems are now viable for genuinely complex, multi-week projects—but only with robust safety scaffolding. The experiment’s layered controls (sandboxing, real-time classifiers, offline monitoring) were not incidental; they were the enabling condition for the speedup. Second, the unreliability of agent self-assessment means human oversight should focus on validating outputs and re-scoping work, not on pre-judging feasibility. The future of AI-driven research is not about replacing human judgment, but about re-positioning it around the bottlenecks that agents still cannot see.

The real market crash was triggered by midterm election results reflecting public opposition to data centers, but Huang's exit now exposes the fragility of the circular financing model. This could reshape AI infrastructure economics, giving buyers more leverage and accelerating the erosion of Nvidia's dominance.

Ramp data shows AI spending growth has been nearly flat for three consecutive months even as token usage grows, indicating that cheaper models are now 'good enough' for most tasks. This shift gives buyers pricing power, allowing them to negotiate discounts and switch to cheaper models, potentially increasing token consumption fivefold without higher costs. The value in the AI economy is moving from model intelligence to applications and value-added services, pressuring model developers on price.

This is not a demand slowdown but a repricing of intelligence itself. Businesses are discovering that cheaper frontier models now clear the bar for most routine tasks, and they are systematically downgrading their model choices. The real hedge is about where value accrues: the video argues that durable advantage lies less in model choice and more in the surrounding infrastructure—evaluation frameworks, system integration, and proprietary customer knowledge—that remains valuable regardless of which model wins the next benchmark race.

For practitioners driving AI transformation, this is a mandate to renegotiate. ## 4. Meta's Muse becomes #1 app, revealing the new playbook for consumer AI agents

Meta's personal AI agent Muse became the number one app of the year, built on a foundation of trust, safety, and personalization rather than speed to market. The team deliberately delayed launch to ensure reliability, and unexpected features like the Jolly avatar and shopping use cases drove adoption. This success story highlights a shift in product management toward taste and empathy, and signals that consumer AI agents are becoming a major new interface for commerce and daily tasks.

<!--detail
Meta's Muse reaching the top of the app charts this year is less a story about a technological breakthrough and more a case study in disciplined product philosophy. The team, led by VP Mona, deliberately chose a slower, trust-first path to market, prioritizing reliability and safety over the industry's default bias for speed. This wasn't just a risk-averse choice; it was a strategic bet that a consumer AI agent's primary currency is user confidence, and that a single early failure could permanently poison the well. The unexpected virality of the "Jolly" avatar and the emergence of shopping as a core use case further underscore that the team's initial roadmap was a hypothesis, not a certainty.

The source material reveals a productive tension between the narrative of deliberate planning and the reality of emergent success. While the team's caution was intentional, the features that drove adoption—the avatar and commerce—were largely discovered through user behavior, not predicted. This suggests a nuanced playbook: you can control the foundation (trust, safety) but must remain radically open to where users take the agent. The shift in the product manager role, from roadmap owner to empathetic taste-maker who can prototype quickly, is the connective tissue. It implies that in the AI era, the ability to feel user pain and iterate on a hunch is more valuable than the ability to execute a predefined plan.

For practitioners driving AI transformation, Muse offers a clear, actionable counterweight to the "move fast and break things" instinct. It argues for building a "trust moat" as a competitive advantage, especially in consumer-facing agents. The practical implication is to institutionalize a two-track approach: a rigorous, safety-first core development process, paired with a flexible, observation-driven layer for feature discovery. The role of the product leader must evolve to become a curator of user empathy, empowered to make taste-based calls quickly, because in this new interface, the product's personality and reliability are the product.
