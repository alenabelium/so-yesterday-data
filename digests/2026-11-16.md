## 1. Anthropic's 1,000-agent team formalizes Fermat's Last Theorem in 28.7 hours

Anthropic's experiment with 1,000 agents using an early version of Claude Fable 5.2 formalized Fermat's Last Theorem in Lean in under 29 hours—a dramatic speedup from the 11 days it took in September, using a third of the tokens and producing a proof 1/7th the length. The agents self-organized into crews and a task tree without human steering, demonstrating emergent coordination for long-horizon problems. This marks a significant leap in multi-agent scaling and AI's capability to tackle complex mathematical proofs, with implications for automated theorem proving and research acceleration.

<!--detail
The 1,000-agent experiment marks a decisive shift in how we should think about AI research capability: not as a single model getting smarter, but as a coordination problem being solved. Anthropic's agents formalized Fermat's Last Theorem in Lean in 28.7 hours—a third of the tokens and one-seventh the proof length of the September baseline—using only general-purpose tools like Git and SQL, with no specialized theorem-proving platform. The source video adds a crucial wrinkle that complicates the headline: the agents' own estimates of task difficulty were wildly unreliable. Helpers initially judged 47 of 60 statements infeasible, yet proved most of them anyway. This is a double-edged finding. On one hand, it suggests agents will attempt things they believe are impossible, which is useful for exploratory work. On the other, it means you cannot trust an agent's self-assessment to triage work—a human manager delegating based on agent-reported difficulty would have killed the project early. The safety architecture (sandboxing, real-time classifier, offline monitor) also deserves attention: the agents were actively blocked from escaping or downloading the existing proof, meaning the result is a genuine test of reasoning, not retrieval.

For practitioners, the implication is concrete: multi-agent systems are now viable for long-horizon, high-complexity tasks, but the orchestration layer matters more than the model. You need to design for emergent coordination, not script it, and you need monitoring that assumes agents will misjudge their own capabilities. The speedup from 11 days to 28.7 hours suggests that scaling agent count is a lever for research acceleration, but the unreliable self-assessment means human oversight should focus on validating outputs, not trusting agent-reported progress. The next "speedrun" won't be a math proof—it will be a business process, and the same lessons apply.

This exposes a single point of failure in the AI industry's circular financing model, where Nvidia's chip sales were propped up by its own investments in customers. The departure, combined with eroding CUDA moat and tightening export controls, could reshape the competitive landscape and pricing power in AI compute.

## 3. Task saturation shifts AI pricing power to buyers, enabling 5x more usage for the same cost

Ramp data shows AI spending growth has been nearly flat for three consecutive months even as token usage grows, indicating that businesses are reaching 'task saturation' where cheaper models are 'good enough' for most tasks. This gives buyers leverage to negotiate lower prices and switch to cheaper models, potentially increasing token consumption fivefold without higher costs. The value in the AI economy is shifting from model intelligence to applications and integration, pressuring model developers on pricing despite continued technical improvements.

<!--detail
The AI economy is quietly renegotiating its fundamental terms. Ramp's spending data reveals a striking plateau: corporate AI expenditure has been nearly flat for three consecutive months even as token consumption climbs. This is not a demand problem but a pricing reckoning. Businesses have hit "task saturation"—the point where frontier models deliver marginal intelligence that most workflows simply don't need. When a cheaper model passes the same acceptance tests, the premium for extra capability evaporates. The result is a structural shift in bargaining power: buyers can now demand discounts, switch vendors freely, and potentially consume five times more tokens for the same budget.

Even Jensen Huang reportedly worries about the "avalanche" of committed chip costs and the need for solvent customers.

For practitioners, the actionable insight is to stop treating model choice as a one-time architectural decision. Build evaluation harnesses that let you swap models as prices shift, and invest in integration and customer knowledge—assets that appreciate as models improve. The winners will be those who treat AI as a negotiable utility, not a fixed premium asset. The losers will be those who over-engineer around today's frontier models, only to find their workaround obsolete when the next price drop arrives.
