This exposes a single point of failure in the AI industry's circular financing model, where Nvidia's own investments and guarantees fueled demand for its products, and his exit could unravel that system, impacting data center financing and the broader AI economy.

## 2. Anthropic's 1,000-agent team formalizes Fermat's Last Theorem in 28.7 hours

Anthropic deployed 1,000 agents running an early version of Claude Fable 5.2 to formalize Fermat's Last Theorem in Lean in under 29 hours—a dramatic speedup from 11 days in September—using only general-purpose tools like Git and SQL. The agents self-organized into crews and a task tree, demonstrating emergent coordination for long-horizon problems, and produced a proof 1/7th the length of the original, signaling a new era of multi-agent scaling for complex mathematical verification.

<!--detail
Anthropic’s latest experiment marks a step-change in how we should think about multi-agent systems: 1,000 agents running an early version of Claude Fable 5.2 formalized Fermat’s Last Theorem in Lean in 28.7 hours, using only general-purpose tools like Git and SQL. That is a 9x speedup over the 11-day September effort, achieved with one-third the tokens and a proof one-seventh the length. The agents self-organized into crews and a task tree without human steering, with a lead assigning statements and helpers splitting work into subtasks—emergent coordination that looks less like orchestration and more like a small software company forming itself.

The source video adds crucial nuance that complicates the headline. The agents’ own difficulty estimates were wildly unreliable: they initially judged 47 of 60 statements infeasible, yet proved most of them anyway. This is a double-edged signal—impressive persistence, but a warning that AI self-assessment cannot yet be trusted for planning. The video also notes the experiment ran without the specialized ProveToMe platform, suggesting the bottleneck is not tooling but coordination logic. Safety was layered via sandboxing, a real-time classifier, and an offline monitor that blocked escape attempts, including preventing agents from downloading the existing proof—so the speedup is genuine, not memorization.

For practitioners, the actionable implication is that long-horizon, verifiable work—code refactoring, compliance documentation, formal specification—is now plausibly parallelizable at scale, but only with human oversight on task decomposition. Do not let agents estimate feasibility; assign work and let them fail fast. The 28.7-hour result is less a proof about math than a proof about process: given a verifiable target and enforced isolation, agent swarms can outpace single-agent efforts by an order of magnitude. The next question is whether that holds when the target is not a theorem but a messy, ambiguous business problem.
