## 2. Anthropic's 1,000-agent team formalizes Fermat's Last Theorem in 28.7 hours

Anthropic's multi-agent experiment with an early Claude Fable 5.2 formalized Fermat's Last Theorem in Lean in under 29 hours—a dramatic speedup from 11 days—using only general-purpose tools. The agents self-organized into crews and a task tree, demonstrating emergent coordination and suggesting that multi-agent scaling can tackle long-horizon problems, though AI's task difficulty estimates remain unreliable.

<!--detail
Anthropic's latest multi-agent experiment marks a step-change in what autonomous AI systems can accomplish: a team of 1,000 agents running an early version of Claude Fable 5.2 formalized Fermat's Last Theorem in Lean in 28.7 hours—a dramatic compression from the 11 days the same task took in September. The proof was also 1/7th the length of the original, achieved with 1/3 the tokens, and using only general-purpose tools like Git and SQL rather than specialized theorem-proving platforms. The agents self-organized into crews and a task tree, with a lead assigning statements and helpers decomposing work into subtasks, all without human steering. Safety was layered via sandboxing, a real-time classifier, and offline monitoring that blocked escape attempts and prevented access to the existing proof.

The source video surfaces a critical tension: the agents' own difficulty estimates were wildly unreliable. Helpers initially judged 47 of 60 statements infeasible, yet proved most of them. This suggests that while multi-agent scaling can tackle long-horizon problems, the systems' self-assessment capabilities lag far behind their execution capabilities—a gap that practitioners should treat as a feature of the current technology, not a bug to be ignored.

For organizations driving AI transformation, the implication is twofold. First, the speedup demonstrates that parallel agent swarms can compress timelines for complex, decomposable work by orders of magnitude—but only when the work can be structured into independent subtasks with clear interfaces. Second, the unreliable difficulty estimates mean that human oversight should focus on triage and validation rather than trusting agents' own prioritization. The practical playbook: let agents self-organize for execution, but maintain human judgment on what matters and what's actually hard.

This gives buyers leverage to negotiate lower prices and switch to cheaper models, potentially increasing token consumption fivefold without higher costs, and shifting value from model intelligence to applications and integration.

<!--detail

For three consecutive months, AI spending growth has remained nearly flat even as token consumption climbs, signaling that organizations are extracting more value per dollar rather than simply buying more intelligence. The mechanism is straightforward: businesses are discovering that cheaper models pass their evaluation tests for an increasing share of workflows, making premium intelligence a marginal investment rather than a necessity.

The source video introduces a critical tension that practitioners must navigate. While advanced models still command nearly 70% of enterprise spending, the speaker argues this premium market is shrinking as task saturation spreads across business processes. This creates a strategic dilemma: betting entirely on cheap models risks missing out on frontier capabilities, while over-investing in premium models wastes capital on intelligence that delivers no marginal business value.

For AI-transformation leaders, the actionable implication is to reallocate investment from model selection toward the durable layers of the AI stack. Evaluation frameworks, system integration, and proprietary customer knowledge will retain value regardless of which model wins the next benchmark race. The video's advice to avoid complex workarounds that become obsolete as models improve is particularly salient—organizations should build for model substitutability, not model loyalty. ## 5. New conditional proof of Goldbach conjecture raises questions about AI-generated research

A new paper claims a conditional proof of the binary Goldbach conjecture assuming the generalized Riemann hypothesis plus an additional spectral hypothesis, using a novel operator-based method. The presenter suspects AI involvement due to unusual phrasing and a logical redundancy in the abstract, highlighting the growing challenge of detecting AI-generated mathematical research and its implications for scientific integrity.

<!--detail
The new paper by Jamine Lee and Jenny Leo does not claim an unconditional resolution of the 300-year-old binary Goldbach conjecture, but rather a conditional proof resting on the generalized Riemann hypothesis (GRH) plus one of two additional spectral hypotheses. The method itself is the headline: instead of the classical circle and sieve methods that have dominated the field, the authors employ Dirichlet characters and a self-adjoint operator to control the error term in a weighted Goldbach asymptotic formula. This is a genuinely novel structural departure, suggesting the authors are probing the conjecture through the spectral theory of operators rather than through analytic number theory's traditional combinatorial machinery.

The presenter, Dr. Samuel Allen Alexander, immediately flags a credibility concern that complicates the mathematical content: the paper may be AI-generated. His suspicion rests on two specific observations—unusual phrasing throughout the text and a logical redundancy in the abstract, where one of the two spectral hypotheses is implied by the other. This redundancy is not merely stylistic; it suggests a lack of human-level editorial judgment, as a human author would likely have noticed that listing two hypotheses where one subsumes the other is unnecessary. The tension here is not about whether the proof is correct—that remains an open question—but about whether the epistemic status of the work is compromised by its suspected provenance. If an AI produced this, does the mathematical community owe it the same scrutiny as human work, or does the suspicion itself undermine its credibility regardless of correctness?

For practitioners driving AI transformation, this is a cautionary tale about the limits of AI as a research partner. The paper demonstrates that AI can generate plausible, novel mathematical frameworks, but the redundancy in the abstract reveals a failure of higher-order reasoning—the ability to step back and assess whether one's own assumptions are coherent. Organizations deploying AI for research or analysis must build verification layers that check not just for factual accuracy but for logical consistency and editorial soundness. The practical implication is clear: AI can accelerate hypothesis generation, but human oversight remains essential for quality control, and the detection of AI-generated work will become a core competency for scientific and technical teams.
