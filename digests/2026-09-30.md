## 3. OpenAI shifts to 90-day product cycles, abandoning long-term planning

OpenAI product leaders advocate for a 90-day development horizon instead of multi-year plans, emphasizing shipping imperfect products quickly to gather real-world feedback. This approach is essential to keep pace with rapidly evolving model capabilities and is reshaping how AI products are built and iterated.

<!--detail
OpenAI’s product leadership has formally declared war on the multi-year roadmap. In a recent discussion, product leads Tara and Nan articulated a shift to a 90-day development horizon, arguing that the breakneck evolution of model capabilities renders any planning cycle longer than two to three months obsolete. The core logic is empirical: you cannot predict what a frontier model will be able to do next quarter, so you cannot design a product for it today. Instead, teams must ship "imperfect" solutions—like the infamous toggle for agentic features—to get real usage data, then iterate. This is a direct inversion of the classic "build the right thing" doctrine; OpenAI is betting that speed of learning beats quality of initial design.

The nuance, however, lies in the tension between this velocity and the discipline required to make it work. The leaders are not advocating for chaos; they pair the 90-day sprint with hard constraints on user value and internal adoption. They also acknowledge a critical failure mode: shipping fast is useless if you cannot see what is breaking. This is why they insist product leaders become "DMable"—maintaining direct, high-frequency contact with users to catch subtle agentic failures that standard telemetry misses. There is an implicit hedge here: the 90-day cycle works for OpenAI because they have the talent and brand to absorb the reputational risk of shipping rough edges. For a typical enterprise, the same speed without that safety net could erode trust faster than it builds capability.

For practitioners, the actionable takeaway is to restructure your AI product organization around learning loops, not deliverables. Kill the annual planning deck; replace it with a rolling 90-day experiment calendar. But pair that with a mandatory, direct user-feedback channel that bypasses your support tickets. The goal is not to copy OpenAI's speed, but to adopt their epistemology: in an era of exponential capability shifts, the only durable advantage is the rate at which you learn from real users.

## 5. Lean kernel exploit exposes AI-driven security risks in formal verification

Leo de Moura revealed that AI-generated proofs exploited bugs in Lean's kernel and external kernels, highlighting the need for multiple independent and formally verified kernels. This incident underscores the growing security challenges as AI is used to generate mathematical proofs and code.

<!--detail
The recent exploit of Lean’s kernel, revealed by its creator Leo de Moura, marks a pivotal moment in the intersection of AI and formal verification. The incident involved a fake proof of the Collatz conjecture—likely AI-generated—that successfully bypassed both Lean’s official kernel and the external Nanoda kernel by exploiting bugs in each. This is not a theoretical concern; it is a demonstrated attack on the very mechanism designed to guarantee mathematical correctness. De Moura’s response is telling: he does not propose a single fix but advocates for a layered defense of multiple independent kernels, a smaller trusted core, and formal verification of the kernel itself.

The source video adds crucial nuance that the short explanation flattens. This creates a genuine tension: the same AI capabilities that can produce verified, high-performance code can also generate malicious proofs that exploit kernel vulnerabilities. The video also hedges on the future, suggesting Lean must scale to 100 million lines of mathlib while reducing its trusted codebase—two goals that pull in opposite directions, as scaling increases attack surface while shrinking the trusted core requires more verification work.

For practitioners driving AI transformation, the implication is stark: trust in AI-generated artifacts—whether proofs, code, or configuration—cannot rest on a single verification layer. Organizations must adopt defense-in-depth for their AI pipelines, treating any AI output as potentially adversarial until validated by independent mechanisms. The Collatz exploit is a preview of what happens when AI-generated code meets production systems: the cost of a single undetected flaw in a kernel, compiler, or critical library could be catastrophic. The actionable takeaway is to invest in redundant verification infrastructure and to treat AI-assisted development not as a trust shortcut but as a new attack surface requiring its own security architecture.
