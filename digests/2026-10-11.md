This inverts decades of software design where the application was the center of gravity and the user navigated its menus; now the agent mediates intent, and the app becomes an execution engine.

## 5. AI labs buy corporate work archives, raising questions about the value of human labor

Google's $10 million bid for Spirit Airlines' work records highlights a new market where AI labs purchase corporate communication data to train agents. Critics argue these records capture only the chaotic imitation of work, not the tacit knowledge and judgment that drive success, sparking debates about data ownership and the true nature of work.

<!--detail
The $10 million bid by Google for Spirit Airlines' corporate archives marks a formalization of a strange new asset class: the digital residue of work itself. Emails, Teams messages, and documents are being treated as training ore for AI agents, with the implicit bet that intelligent behavior can be distilled from the chaotic traces of daily corporate life. Yet the source analysis cuts against this premise, arguing that what these archives capture is not work but its imitation—the performative coordination, status signaling, and reactive firefighting that fill most inboxes, while the tacit judgment, contextual intuition, and hard-won expertise that actually drive outcomes remain stubbornly uncaptured.

The tension here is not merely technical but economic. Buyers are paying for volume and pattern, while the sellers—the employees who generated the data—receive nothing, and the companies selling may be liquidating their own competitive moat for a one-time fee. The video's critique sharpens this: training agents on archives from a failing airline risks encoding the very dysfunctions that led to its collapse, producing agents fluent in mediocrity. There is also a quiet acknowledgment that resistance is already emerging, with unions challenging data sales and employees passively refusing AI tools, suggesting the market's legitimacy is far from settled.

For practitioners, the implication is twofold. First, treat any corporate archive as a biased sample, not ground truth—agents trained on it will inherit its pathologies. Second, the real strategic move is not selling data but redefining work's value proposition: identifying which tasks are genuinely automatable repetition versus which require human judgment that cannot be scaled. The organizations that thrive will be those that use agents to strip away the bureaucratic noise, freeing humans for the complex, judgment-heavy work that archives can never capture. That distinction, not the data itself, is the durable asset.
