# Daily Digest — November 7, 2026

The 5 most important AI developments right now, ranked by importance.

## 1. AI models now edit other models' weights, unlocking recursive self-improvement

GPT-6 Astra improved a small Qwen model's math performance from 8% to 48% by directly editing its weights, and MIT CSAIL researchers achieved similar gains by altering just 0.016% of weights. This marks a shift from micromanaging agents to delegating improvement tasks, accelerating the path to self-improving AI systems.

Two independent demonstrations show AI systems editing the raw weights of other models, bypassing the traditional pipeline of fine-tuning and gradient descent. GPT-6 Astra lifted a small Qwen model's math performance from 8% to 48% by directly manipulating its weights, while MIT CSAIL researchers achieved nearly identical gains—from 7.9% to 48.1%—by altering just 0.016% of a model's parameters. For practitioners, the actionable implication is about rethinking where optimization effort goes. The MIT result, in particular, is striking: a 0.016% weight change producing a sixfold improvement suggests that much of current fine-tuning is grossly inefficient. Organizations should begin auditing their fine-tuning pipelines for waste, and more importantly, start designing evaluation frameworks that can detect when a model has been improved by another model—because that capability is now cheap enough to be a competitive differentiator, not a research curiosity.

This, combined with a $3 trillion selloff, suggests the AI infrastructure boom may be cracking under financial pressure.

This capital shift is driven by AI's high costs and stagnant stock performance, and it's creating new investment opportunities in tokenized compute and data markets.

Anthropic is allocating 5% of its shares, valued at ~$100 billion, to a public benefit foundation to distribute AI wealth and support workers. This is a concrete step toward addressing post-labor economics, moving beyond theory to institutionalize ownership-based income models.

<!--detail
Anthropic’s decision to carve out 5% of its equity—roughly $100 billion—into a public benefit foundation marks one of the first concrete institutional attempts to answer the question that has haunted AI discourse: what happens to labor when the marginal cost of intelligence approaches zero. Rather than leaving wealth distribution to post-hoc taxation or corporate philanthropy, Anthropic is embedding ownership-based income into its capital structure from the outset. The foundation’s mandate to support workers signals a shift from treating AI’s economic disruption as a problem to be managed after the fact, to one that is priced into the company’s very valuation.

The video does not reconcile this contradiction—it simply presents both as forward-looking. Similarly, the $100 billion figure is presented without scrutiny; the video treats the valuation as stable, though the foundation’s actual purchasing power will depend on how Anthropic’s equity performs in a market increasingly shaped by the very models it builds.

For practitioners, the actionable insight is not the dollar amount but the precedent. Anthropic has demonstrated that AI wealth redistribution can be engineered into corporate governance rather than left to political accident. Organizations driving AI transformation should watch how this foundation operationalizes “supporting workers”—whether it funds retraining, basic income pilots, or worker-owned cooperatives—because those choices will become templates. The deeper lesson is that AI strategy can no longer be separated from labor economics; every deployment decision now carries an implicit answer to the question of who benefits, and companies that ignore that question will find it answered for them.

## 5. AI-assisted mathematics achieves record speed: Hilbert's tenth problem for rationals solved and refined

A groundbreaking proof that the integers are Diophantine in the rationals, built on OpenAI's earlier work, refutes Mazur's conjecture and solves Hilbert's tenth problem for rational numbers. The original 300-billion-variable polynomial was rapidly improved to a quartic with 962 variables through collaborative, AI-assisted efforts, showcasing a new era of fast-paced mathematical discovery.

<!--detail
The mathematical community has just witnessed a paradigm shift in how results are discovered, verified, and refined. A proof that the integers are Diophantine in the rationals—meaning a single polynomial with integer coefficients captures exactly the integers among its rational solutions—has landed with unprecedented force. The initial construction, built on OpenAI's earlier work on Hilbert's tenth problem, was a monstrous object of roughly 300 billion variables with absurd coefficient bounds. Within days, a grassroots collaborative effort slashed this to a quartic polynomial with just 962 variables. This is not merely a cosmetic improvement; it transforms an existence proof into something approaching a usable tool, while simultaneously refuting Mazur's conjecture and, via the DPRM theorem, settling Hilbert's tenth problem for rational numbers.

The proof currently leans on published theorems that have not yet been fully checked in the proof assistant, leaving a gap between what the community accepts and what machines have certified. This is a telling moment: the speed of human-AI collaboration has outpaced the formal verification infrastructure designed to keep pace with it. The presenter does not gloss over this—it is presented as an open problem, not a settled detail.

For practitioners driving AI transformation, this is the clearest signal yet that AI-assisted discovery is not a future abstraction but a present workflow. The lesson is twofold. First, AI can generate results that are correct but practically unusable; the human value-add lies in rapid iteration, refactoring, and simplification—turning a 300-billion-variable monster into a 962-variable quartic. Second, verification is the bottleneck. Organizations adopting AI for high-stakes work must invest in formal verification and audit trails, or they will find themselves with brilliant outputs that cannot be trusted or deployed. The race is no longer about generating ideas; it is about making them trustworthy fast enough to matter.
