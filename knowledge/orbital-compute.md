Orbital compute is the frontier answer to terrestrial AI's two binding constraints: power and cooling. The thesis — space offers continuous solar, radiative cooling, and no land or grid politics — moved from science fiction to engineering studies when hyperscalers and startups began funding demonstrator missions: test platforms measuring whether accelerator hardware survives launch and thermal cycling, and whether the economics of launch mass, radiation tolerance, and microwave/laser downlink can close against ground-based builds.

The corpus's honest position: this is an option, not a plan. Even optimistic roadmaps put meaningful orbital capacity a decade out, physics is indifferent to hype, and the near-term money is in terrestrial optimizations (see [data centers](/knowledge/data-center)). The reason it still earns a concept is strategic: if [inference](/knowledge/inference) demand grows into the exawatt scale that current AI forecasts imply, the marginal siting decision eventually leaves the planet — and the entities holding the launch infrastructure and radiation-hardened accelerator designs at that point will have an infrastructure moat nobody can quickly replicate.
